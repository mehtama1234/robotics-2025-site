01 · the problem

Specify what, not how

Classic robotics wrote the how: if this, do that — a rule for every case. But the world has endless cases, and the rules grow brittle and never end. Some behaviors, like a fluid recovery from a shove, no one knows how to write down at all.

Reinforcement learning changes the question. Instead of programming the behavior, you specify a reward — what counts as success — and let the robot find a policy that earns it, through trial and error. You give it the goal; it discovers the means.

the actual math — one level deeper

Formalize as a Markov decision process (S, A, P, r, γ): states, actions, transition P, reward r, discount γ.

The goal is a policy π maximizing expected discounted return E[Σ γᵗ rᵗ] — you specify r, learning finds π.

02 · the loop

State, action, reward, repeat

Every RL method is the same loop underneath. The agent observes the state, its policy picks an action, the environment responds with a new state and a scalar reward, and around it goes. Learning is nudging the policy so that, over many turns, it collects more reward.

The subtlety is ‘over the long run’. A good move now may only pay off much later, so the agent must value future reward, not just immediate — which is what makes credit assignment (which action deserves the eventual win?) the hard core of RL.

the actual math — one level deeper

Value: Qπ(s,a) = expected return from taking a in s then following π; improve π toward higher-Q actions.

Credit assignment: temporal-difference updates propagate later reward back to earlier actions — the crux when rewards are delayed.

03 · the dilemma

Explore or exploit

An agent that only does what it already knows works can never discover something better. An agent that only tries new things never benefits from what it learned. This is the exploration-exploitation trade-off, and it’s unavoidable.

It gets worse when reward is sparse — when success is rare and most tries yield nothing. Then the agent can wander for ages without a signal to learn from, which is why so much of RL is really about smarter exploration and shaping the reward so learning ever gets started.

the actual math — one level deeper

Balance is formal: e.g. ε-greedy or entropy bonuses keep the policy trying non-greedy actions.

Sparse reward makes the return signal near-zero almost everywhere — shaping, curiosity, or demonstrations restore a gradient to climb.

04 · copy first

Learn from demonstrations

Designing a good reward is hard, and trial-and-error is slow. So the most practical route is often to show the robot: collect a handful of expert demonstrations and train the policy to copy them. This is imitation learning, and it skips the whole search.

But copying has a famous failure. The policy is only reliable where the demos went; one small error takes it to an unfamiliar state, where it errs more, and it spirals. The fix is to ask the expert what to do in those new states too (DAgger), or to add a little reward-driven refinement on top.

the actual math — one level deeper

Behavior cloning: supervised learning of π(a|s) on expert (s,a) pairs — but errors compound as O(T²) over a horizon T.

DAgger: roll out the policy, query the expert on the states it actually visits, and retrain — fixing the covariate shift.

05 · practice safely

A million tries in a dream

Trial-and-error learning is hungry — often millions of episodes. You cannot run that on real hardware without breaking it or waiting years. So RL trains in simulation, thousands of copies in parallel, at many times real speed.

The gap is that simulation isn’t reality. The trick that bridges it is domain randomization: vary the sim’s physics, textures, and delays so widely that the real world looks like just another sample the policy already learned to handle. It’s the same data-engine pressure that runs under the whole field.

the actual math — one level deeper

Parallel sim collects samples at massive scale; domain randomization samples physics params θ ~ p(θ) so the policy is robust across the range.

Reality is treated as one draw of θ inside the trained distribution — zero-shot transfer when the range is wide enough.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every family below leans on one or more of these — the recurring reasons to learn rather than hand-code.

Behaviors no one can hand-write

RL discovers fluid, reactive skills — recovery from a shove, a dexterous regrasp — that resist being programmed rule by rule.

Show instead of specify

Imitation turns a few human demonstrations into a policy, sidestepping the hard art of reward design.

Practice without breaking things

Training in simulation means millions of safe, free trials, then transfer — no worn hardware, no danger.

Adapts to the body and the task

One learning recipe retrains across robots, morphologies, and goals, rather than a bespoke controller each time.

Improves from its own experience

Online and residual methods let a deployed policy keep getting better from the data it collects in the real world.

Deep RL for control

robotics
the problem

A quadruped on uneven terrain needs a policy that maps joint angles and IMU readings to torques every 50 ms — but no one knows how to write those torque equations by hand.

the approaches

Learn a control policy straight from interaction — no hand-derived controller — letting trial and error discover motor skills that generalize across embodiment and disturbance.

The recurring struggle is sample cost and the sim-to-real gap.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. State s: 48-dim vector — 12 joint angles, 12 velocities, 3-axis IMU, 3-axis gyro, 6 contact booleans, target velocity (2)
  2. Action a: 12 target joint angles; PD controller converts to torques at 100 Hz
  3. Reward r: +1.0 × forward velocity − 0.01 × torque² − 0.2 × foot-clearance penalty
  4. Training: 4,096 parallel sim instances × 24 M steps = ~100 billion torque commands in 8 hours on an A100
  5. Transfer: domain-randomized mass ±30%, friction [0.3, 1.5], latency [0, 20 ms]; deployed zero-shot on ANYmal-C
  • ROBORoCoDA: Counterfactual Data Augmentation for Data-Efficient Robot Learning — causal invariance + SE(3) equivariance augment RL data
  • ROBOFrom Imitation to Refinement — Residual RL for Precise Assembly — RL sharpens an imitation policy for contact-rich assembly
  • ROBOVision Transformers for End-to-End Quadrotor Obstacle Avoidance — learned high-speed flight control from images
  • ROBOLearning Adversarial Policies for Swarm Leader Identification — RL discovers a probing behavior to find a leader

Sim-to-real RL

robotics
the problem

The sim policy walks perfectly but stumbles on real carpet: small physics mismatches — motor friction, ground compliance, sensor noise — cascade into failure within seconds.

the approaches

Train cheaply in simulation, then transfer to a real robot by randomizing physics and appearance so reality falls inside the trained range.

The open question is which parameters to randomize — transfer success is still variable.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Simulation: MuJoCo at 2,000 Hz, 10× real-time; 2,048 envs in parallel
  2. Domain randomization: sample floor friction U[0.3, 1.6], motor damping U[0.8, 1.2]×nominal, latency U[0, 20 ms] each episode
  3. RL run: PPO, 500 M steps, policy learns to handle the full range
  4. Reality check: real robot joints have 8 ms latency and ±2% gear-ratio error — both inside the randomized range
  5. Outcome: zero-shot success rate rises from 23% (no randomization) to 87% on a 5 m trot across gravel
  • ROBODemoStart: Demonstration-Led Auto-Curriculum for Multi-Fingered Sim-to-Real — demo-initialized auto-curriculum RL for dexterity
  • ROBOReal-Time Whole-Body Control of Legged Robots with MPPI — parallel sampling control transferred to hardware
  • CVPROpening the Sim-to-Real Door for Humanoid Pixel-to-Action Transfer — stage-reset exploration + GRPO, 83% real door-opening
  • ROBOIncremental Few-Shot Adaptation via Parallelizable Physics Simulators — adapts sim parameters online to close the gap

Reward design & shaping

both
the problem

Specifying 'grasp the cup cleanly' as a scalar: 0 if failed, 1 if succeeded — gives zero gradient for 10,000 steps until first accidental success. The policy never gets started.

the approaches

The reward is the specification — and getting it right is the hardest part. Learn it from preferences or human feedback instead of hand-tuning a sparse signal.

The danger is reward hacking: policies that game a mis-specified reward.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Sparse baseline: reward = 1 only on full grasp; first success after 180,000 steps on average
  2. Shaped reward: r = 0.3 × (finger–object distance decreasing) + 0.4 × (object height > 2 cm) + 0.3 × grasp success
  3. Effect: first grasp at step 12,000 (15× faster); policy converges in 80,000 steps vs 600,000
  4. Preference fine-tune: 200 human A/B comparisons refine grip style (no squeezing, natural wrap); RLHF reward model R² = 0.81
  5. Hacking check: clip height bonus to +0.4 max — prevents policy balancing cup on fingertip to collect distance bonus perpetually
  • ROBOFDPP: Fine-Tune Diffusion Policy with Human Preference — preference feedback shapes a manipulation policy
  • ROBOMulti-Type Preference Learning — learns reward from several kinds of human preference
  • CVPRCCCaption: Dual-Reward RL for Complete and Correct Captioning — completeness + correctness rewards with hallucination checks
  • CVPRPerceptual-Evidence Anchored Reinforced Learning for Multimodal Reasoning — anchors reward to verifiable perceptual evidence

Offline RL

both
the problem

You have 50,000 logged trajectories from a hospital robot but cannot run any new trials (patient safety). How do you improve the policy without a single new interaction?

the approaches

Learn from a fixed dataset with no live interaction — essential when exploration is unsafe or expensive — while guarding against overestimating actions the data never tried.

Dataset quality is the ceiling; distribution shift is the enemy.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Dataset D: 50 k trajectories, mixed quality — expert (20%), average (60%), poor (20%)
  2. Conservative Q: CQL adds −α × E[Q(s,a)] for out-of-dataset actions; α = 5.0 clamps overestimation
  3. Training: batch size 256, 1 M gradient steps; Q-values for unseen actions stay below the dataset floor
  4. Evaluation: offline val loss 0.032; real-world test on 200 held-out scenarios: 74% task success vs 61% for behavior-cloned baseline
  5. Dataset quality ceiling: swapping in 10% expert data raises success to 84% — the data, not the algorithm, is the bottleneck
  • ROBOLanguage-Conditioned Offline RL — offline policies conditioned on language goals
  • ROBODistributional Decision Transformer — sequence-model offline RL over return distributions
  • CVPRSRCP: Saliency-Guided Consistency Policy for Visual Unsupervised RL — saliency-guided representations + consistency policies
  • CVPREfficient Frame Selection for Long Video Understanding via RL — offline-style RL with rollback to prevent drift

Imitation & behavior cloning

both
the problem

A teleop operator demonstrates 50 grasps of a USB plug. At test time, 8 mm off the canonical approach angle and the policy collapses: it was never shown that state.

the approaches

Copy expert demonstrations directly into a policy — the fastest way to a working behavior — bootstrapping skill without any exploration.

The weakness is drift off the demonstrated states; pretrained representations and augmentation help.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Dataset: 50 demonstrations, each ~120 steps at 10 Hz = 6,000 (s,a) pairs
  2. BC training: minimize MSE(π(s), a_expert) — converges in 20 epochs, val loss 0.008 N·m
  3. On-distribution: 94% success when start pose matches demonstrations within 5 mm / 3°
  4. Off-distribution: 1 cm lateral shift → 31% success; errors compound across the 12-step approach
  5. Fix: pretrained ViT backbone cuts the needed demos from 50 to 12 by reusing visual features from ImageNet
  • ROBOGeneralizable Imitation Learning Through Pre-Trained Representations — pretrained vision backbones make BC data-efficient
  • ROBOProDapt: Proprioceptive Adaptation Using Long-Term Memory Diffusion — contact-memory diffusion for robust imitation
  • CVPRLifelong Imitation with Multimodal Latent Replay — latent replay prevents forgetting across tasks
  • CVPRVideoWeaver: Multi-View Video-to-Video Transfer for Embodied Agents — 4D-grounded multi-view domain randomization for BC

Interactive imitation (DAgger)

robotics
the problem

BC drifts into states it was never shown; asking the expert to demonstrate there — DAgger — turns a 31% policy into a 92% policy, but every query costs operator time.

the approaches

Fix behavior cloning’s drift by rolling out the policy and asking the expert what to do in exactly the states it visits — then retraining on those.

The cost is an expert on call during training, and choosing which queries to make.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Round 0: BC on 50 demos → 31% success; collect 200 policy rollouts, find 42 failure states
  2. Round 1: expert labels 42 failure states → retrain on 6,242 pairs → 67% success
  3. Round 2: 80 more failure states labeled → retrain on 7,042 pairs → 82% success
  4. Round 3: 35 more → 7,077 pairs → 92% success
  5. Cost: 157 expert labels total; pure BC needed 300 demos to reach 85%; DAgger uses 2× fewer queries
  6. Stopping rule: stop when failure-state pool drops below 10 per 100 rollouts — convergence criterion
  • ROBORobot Policy Transfer with Online Demonstrations — interactive demos correct the policy where it fails
  • ROBOFrom Imitation to Refinement — Residual RL for Precise Assembly — combines demos with online refinement
  • ROBOSAFE-GIL: Safety-Guided Imitation Learning — injects safety-aware queries into imitation
  • CVPROpening the Sim-to-Real Door (DAgger + GRPO) — DAgger mixture stabilizes pixel-to-action transfer

Inverse RL

robotics
the problem

A driver expert navigates safely — but what is their actual objective? Comfortable deceleration? Minimum time? Copying actions fails on unseen roads; recovering the reward transfers.

the approaches

Run imitation backwards: instead of copying actions, infer the reward that explains the expert’s behavior — an interpretable, transferable objective.

It’s ill-posed (many rewards fit) and assumes the expert is rational.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Data: 2,000 expert trajectories, 10 s each, in a 4-way intersection scenario
  2. Features φ(s,a): speed, acceleration, jerk, distance to nearest car, headway time (5-dim)
  3. MaxEnt IRL: optimize θ so E_expert[φ] = E_θ[φ]; 50 gradient steps with 128-env forward RL
  4. Recovered reward: θ = [−0.4 jerk, +0.3 speed, −0.5 near-collision]; matches human ratings R² = 0.77
  5. Transfer: same θ on a roundabout (never seen): policy achieves 91% comfort score vs 74% for BC
  • ROBOMulti-Agent Inverse Q-Learning from Demonstrations — recovers multi-agent rewards 2–5× better than prior IRL
  • ROBOCost Function Estimation Using Inverse RL — infers a planner’s hidden cost from behavior
  • ROBOGenerating Causal Explanations of Vehicular Agents — IRL-style recovery of driver objectives for explanation
  • ROBOThink Deep and Fast: Neural Opinion Dynamics from Inverse Games — recovers interaction objectives from expert play

Learning from video & human demos

both
the problem

YouTube has millions of humans cooking, folding, building — but the action labels (joint angles, forces) are invisible. How do you turn pixels-only video into a robot policy?

the approaches

Skip teleoperation: learn tasks from ordinary human video, where the demonstration is visual and the action labels are missing — a vast, cheap data source.

The gaps are missing actions, viewpoint mismatch, and fragile temporal segmentation.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Video corpus: 5,000 YouTube clips, 480p, kitchen tasks; no robot data, no action labels
  2. Optical-flow proxy actions: compute dense flow between frames → 2D wrist-motion vectors as latent actions
  3. Latent policy: Transformer maps image + latent action to next-frame prediction; 200 epochs pre-training
  4. Robot fine-tune: 20 real robot demos + RLHF to map latent → joint angles; 3 hours GPU
  5. Result: 68% success on pick-and-place vs 41% for policy trained on 20 robot demos alone; video pre-training worth ~300 extra robot demos
  • ROBOViViDex: Vision-Based Dexterous Manipulation from Human Videos — trajectory-guided rewards from human video
  • ROBORTAGrasp: Task-Oriented Grasping from Human Videos — retrieve, transfer, align grasps from video memory
  • CVPRMotus: A Unified Latent Action World Model — optical-flow latent actions bridge video and robot data
  • CVPRNIL: No-Data Imitation Learning — a video diffusion model generates the reference to imitate

Sample efficiency & exploration

robotics
the problem

A dexterous hand must thread a needle — first contact is 1-in-1000 random; without a smarter search the policy never gets a positive reward signal.

the approaches

Trial-and-error is expensive, so squeeze more learning from every step — smarter exploration, data reuse, and structure that restores a gradient under sparse reward.

Efficiency tricks are often task-specific and can transfer negatively.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Baseline: PPO with ε-greedy exploration; first needle contact at step 2.3 M on average
  2. Curiosity bonus: intrinsic reward r_i = ‖φ(s') − φ̂(s')‖²; RND network predicts next-state embedding
  3. Effect: first contact at step 180 k (13× faster); curiosity pushes fingers into unexplored configurations
  4. Data reuse: HER relabels failed trajectories as if goal = where the finger actually ended; 4× data efficiency
  5. Convergence: 94% threading success at 4 M steps; baseline needs 22 M steps for 88%
  • ROBOEfficient Imitation Without Demonstrations — bootstraps exploration without a demo set
  • ROBORoCoDA: Counterfactual Data Augmentation — reuses each demo across symmetries for efficiency
  • ROBOCaDRE: Curriculum/data-efficient RL — orders experience to speed convergence
  • ROBOMatch Policy: From Point-Cloud Registration to Manipulation — recasts action inference to need very little data

Model-based RL & world models

both
the problem

Model-free RL needs 10 M real-robot steps for a manipulation skill. A world model lets the policy plan inside an imagined future — but model errors compound over long rollouts.

the approaches

Learn the environment’s dynamics, then plan or train inside that learned model — imagining outcomes instead of paying for every real trial. See the world-models explainer.

Model error compounds over long rollouts; learning dynamics from pixels is hard.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. World model: DreamerV3-style RSSM; trained on 5,000 real episodes (50 k steps)
  2. Imagination: unroll 16-step trajectories inside the model at 10,000× real speed
  3. Policy update: actor-critic trained entirely on imagined data; gradient through model dynamics
  4. Model error: prediction error grows to 18% at horizon 16; clip rollout to 12 steps to stay below 12%
  5. Real-world sample budget: 8,000 real steps (vs 800,000 for model-free) achieves 82% success on block-stacking
  • ROBOLIMT: Language-Informed Multi-Task Visual World Models — language-conditioned learned dynamics for planning
  • ROBOPrivileged-Dreamer: Explicit Imagination of Privileged Information — imagines hidden state for fast adaptation
  • CVPRDreamSAC: Learning Hamiltonian World Models via Symmetry — physics-structured world model with curiosity
  • CVPRWPT: World-to-Policy Transfer via Online World-Model Distillation — distills a world model into an actionable policy

Hierarchical RL

robotics
the problem

A 200-step peg-in-hole task: the high-level agent must approach, align, then insert — but reward only fires at insertion. Flat RL cannot credit the approach 180 steps earlier.

the approaches

Split a long task into a hierarchy — a high-level policy picks sub-goals, low-level policies execute — making credit assignment tractable and skills reusable.

Automatic skill discovery is unsolved; a bad decomposition erases the benefit.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. High-level policy: chooses subgoal every k=20 steps from {approach, align, insert}
  2. Low-level policy: 20-step MPC optimizing toward the subgoal; reward = subgoal reached
  3. Credit flow: high-level reward = task success only at step 200; but low-level reward fires every 20 steps
  4. Training: co-train both; high-level PPO, low-level SAC; 2 M steps total vs 8 M for flat RL
  5. Reuse: same low-level policies reused on 3 different peg geometries — only high-level retrained (300 k steps)
  • ROBOLearning Multi-Agent Loco-Manipulation for Quadrupedal Pushing — hierarchical MARL, 36% over baselines
  • ROBODiffusion Meets Options for Hierarchical Control — diffusion over temporally-extended options
  • ROBOQuadWBG: Hierarchical Whole-Body Grasping — high-level goals over low-level whole-body control
  • ROBOAutomated Hybrid Reward Scheduling — schedules sub-rewards across a hierarchy

Residual RL

robotics
the problem

An imitation policy places a bolt near the hole — ±3 mm accuracy — but insertion requires ±0.3 mm. RL from scratch takes weeks; learning only the correction takes hours.

the approaches

Start from a decent controller or imitation policy and learn only the correction with RL — a small residual that trains in hours, not days.

The base policy choice is critical, and residuals don’t always stay small.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Base policy π_0: BC on 100 demos; places end-effector within 3 mm of target, 78% close-enough rate
  2. Residual δ(s): small MLP, output clipped to ±5 mm per axis; trained with SAC on top of π_0
  3. Combined action: a = π_0(s) + δ(s); π_0 handles coarse approach, δ learns fine correction
  4. Training cost: 80,000 real robot steps (vs 2 M for RL from scratch)
  5. Result: 94% insertion success (±0.3 mm); base policy alone 31%; flat RL needs 10× more data for 87%
  • ROBOFrom Imitation to Refinement — Residual RL for Precise Assembly — residual RL on top of an imitation base
  • ROBOHigh-Frequency Residual Policy for Robotic Throwing — a fast residual sharpens a nominal throw
  • ROBOLearning-Based MPC for Autonomous Driving — learned residual corrects a model-based controller
  • ROBOPredictive Kinematic Coordinate Control + Residual — residual learning over a kinematic base

RL + foundation models & VLA

both
the problem

A language-conditioned robot must follow 'put the apple in the blue bowl' — but the action space is continuous torques, and the LLM has no notion of motor control.

the approaches

Put a web-pretrained model in the loop — an LLM to plan or shape reward, a VLM as the policy backbone — and use RL to fine-tune it for embodied control.

Foundation models hallucinate, and grounding language to precise action is unsolved.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. VLA backbone: PaLI-X 55B vision-language model, finetuned on 130 k robot episodes (RT-2 style)
  2. RL fine-tune: GRPO with sparse binary reward (task success/fail); group size G=8, 20 k steps
  3. Reward signal: GPT-4V judges the final scene ('apple in blue bowl?') → binary 0/1
  4. Gain: 43% success (SFT only) → 68% success after 20 k GRPO steps; 3 hours on 8 × A100
  5. Failure mode: GRPO boosts success but increases verbosity by 40%; add brevity penalty to reward
  • ROBOSAS-Prompt: LLMs as Numerical Optimizers for Robot Self-Improvement — an LLM proposes and refines control parameters
  • ROBOLLM-as-BT-Planner: Behavior-Tree Generation for Task Planning — an LLM writes the task structure RL fills in
  • CVPRMergeVLA: Cross-Skill Model Merging Toward a Generalist VLA — merges skills into one vision-language-action agent
  • CVPRRehearseVLA: Simulated Post-Training for VLAs with a World Model — world-model rollouts post-train a VLA with RL

RLHF & preference optimization

vision
the problem

A radiology-report generator produces factually correct sentences — but radiologists consistently prefer a different ordering and emphasis. No rule captures the preference: it must be learned.

the approaches

The technique that aligned chatbots, turned on vision: optimize a model directly against human (or model) preferences with policy-gradient methods like GRPO and DPO — now the default for tuning generators and VLMs.

It lives or dies on the reward model; group-relative methods cut variance.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Preference data: 800 A/B pairs, each two model outputs; 3 radiologists label preferred; κ = 0.71
  2. Reward model: fine-tuned BERT-medical, 220 M params; test AUC 0.84 on held-out pairs
  3. GRPO: group size 8, 15 k update steps; KL penalty β = 0.04 keeps outputs near SFT baseline
  4. Gain: preferred-by-radiologist rate rises from 41% (SFT) to 73% (GRPO); factuality unchanged (F1 = 0.89)
  5. Cost: 800 preference labels + 3 hours GPU; equivalent quality via supervised data would need ~5,000 labeled reports
  • CVPRDr. Seg: Revisiting GRPO Training for Visual LLMs — perception-oriented GRPO with distribution-ranked rewards
  • CVPRGDPO-SR: Group Direct Preference Optimization for Super-Resolution — DPO-GRPO hybrid with attribute-aware rewards
  • CVPROraPO: Oracle-Educated RL for Factual Radiology Reports — oracle-supervised GRPO with fact-based reward
  • CVPRCurriculum Group Policy Optimization for Text-to-Image — uses group reward variance as an online curriculum signal

Safe RL & curriculum

robotics
the problem

A legged robot learning to run will fall — but on hardware, each fall risks ±$40 k damage. Safety constraints must hold from step 1, not only after convergence.

the approaches

Learn while respecting hard safety constraints — and order the training from easy to hard so a policy can reach skills it could never learn from scratch.

Constraints are hand-crafted, conservatism costs performance, and curriculum design is manual.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Safety constraint: body roll/pitch < 30°; CBF h(s) = 30° − |roll| ≥ 0 must hold at every step
  2. Safe projection: QP projects unsafe actions back to constraint boundary; adds <2 ms per step
  3. Curriculum stage 1: flat terrain, target speed 0.5 m/s; 500 k steps; 0 falls
  4. Curriculum stage 2: add 3 cm random steps, target 1.0 m/s; 500 k more steps; 2 CBF activations
  5. Curriculum stage 3: 8 cm steps, 2.0 m/s; 1 M steps; policy reaches target speed with zero safety violations
  6. Comparison: unconstrained RL reaches 2.0 m/s in 600 k steps but causes 47 falls during training
  • ROBORAIL: Reachability-Aided Imitation Learning for Safety — reachability constraints keep imitation safe
  • ROBOUncertainty-Aware Input-Constrained Control Barrier Functions — safe RL under uncertainty via CBFs
  • ROBOLearning Quiet Walking via Curriculum — a curriculum grows a hard locomotion skill
  • ROBOCTSAC: Curriculum Soft Actor-Critic — curriculum-ordered exploration for stable RL

Part IV

Where it stands

Learning is how modern robots get their skills — and it is still far from solved. What is genuinely still hard:

Sample cost. Model-free RL still needs millions of interactions; despite efficiency tricks, learning complex skills from scratch is prohibitively hungry.
The sim-to-real gap. No principled way to pick domain-randomization ranges; transfer from simulation to hardware remains variable and hand-tuned.
Reward design & hacking. The reward is the whole specification, and a subtly wrong one gets gamed — specifying intent without loopholes is unsolved.
Credit assignment at horizon. Attributing a distant success to the right early action, across hundreds of sparse-reward steps, breaks current methods.
Distribution shift. Imitation and offline policies fail off their data; staying reliable in states the training never covered is fragile.
The human bottleneck. Demonstrations, preferences, and safety specs all need expert humans — scaling learning past a small expert pool is open.
