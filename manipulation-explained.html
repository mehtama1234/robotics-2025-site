01 · the problem

Everything happens through contact

Perception can happen at a distance, but changing the world cannot. To move, open, place, or assemble anything, a robot has to make physical contact — and that contact is the only channel through which it can push the world around.

This is what makes manipulation its own hard problem. Control only exists while the robot is touching; the forces have to point the right way or the object slips or crushes; and the instant a finger touches down or lifts off, the physics changes character. Grasping and manipulation are the science of shaping the world through those fleeting contacts.

the actual math — one level deeper

A contact applies a wrench (force + torque) to the object; the robot can only change the object’s motion through the sum of contact wrenches — no contact, no control authority.

Making/breaking contact switches the system’s equations of motion, so manipulation dynamics are hybrid, not smooth.

02 · hold it

A grasp is a cage of forces

What makes a grasp actually hold? Not raw squeezing force, but where the contacts are. Each fingertip can only push inward and, thanks to friction, resist some sideways force — a ‘cone’ of forces it can apply. Place the fingers well and those cones together can cancel a push or twist from any direction.

That property is called force closure, and it’s the difference between a grip that survives a bump and one that drops the object. Much of grasping is finding, from an object’s shape, contact points that achieve it — fast, and even when you can only see part of the object.

the actual math — one level deeper

Force closure: the contact wrenches positively span the whole wrench space — any external wrench can be resisted by a non-negative combination of contact forces.

Each contact obeys the friction cone ‖f_t‖ ≤ μ f_n (tangential force bounded by friction times normal force); grasp synthesis places contacts so the cones jointly close.

03 · the hard part

Contact makes the physics jump

Away from contact, a robot’s motion is smooth and predictable. The moment it touches, everything changes: pressing a peg into a hole, the system jumps between distinct modes — floating free, touching at one point, wedged at two, sliding home — and each mode follows different equations.

That’s why contact-rich tasks resist the usual tools. You can’t just follow a smooth gradient to the goal, because the gradient breaks at every mode switch. Handling these non-smooth, hybrid dynamics — with force feedback, clever solvers, or learning — is the core challenge of assembly, insertion, and tool use.

the actual math — one level deeper

Contact is a complementarity condition: 0 ≤ force ⊥ gap ≥ 0 — either touching (gap 0, force ≥ 0) or separated (gap > 0, force 0).

This makes the dynamics non-smooth and mode-switched (stick / slip / separate); gradients are undefined across switches, so smooth optimizers stall.

04 · dexterity

Rearranging the grip itself

A parallel gripper can only clamp; a multi-fingered hand can rearrange. To turn an object in place, the hand lifts one finger, moves it to a new spot, and presses again — all while the remaining fingers keep the object secure. Repeat, and the object rotates without ever being dropped.

This is finger gaiting, and it demands coordinating many joints through a constantly changing pattern of which fingers are in contact where. It’s the hardest, most human part of manipulation — and where learned policies and tactile feedback have made the biggest recent gains.

the actual math — one level deeper

A dexterous hand has many DoF; reorientation is a sequence of sub-grasps, each a force-closure grip, linked by finger gaiting (relocate one contact while others hold).

Planning is over both continuous finger motion and the discrete contact schedule — a hybrid problem RL handles well in simulation.

05 · learn it

Many good grasps, commit to one

You can’t hand-code every grasp, so modern manipulation is learned — often by imitation from a handful of human demonstrations. But there’s a subtlety: a mug affords many good grasps, and the policy has to represent that whole set of valid options.

The trap is averaging. Blend two perfectly good grasps and you can land on the bad one in between — a grip on empty air. This is exactly why diffusion and flow-matching policies took over: they can hold a multimodal distribution of actions and commit to one clean choice, instead of collapsing to a mushy average.

the actual math — one level deeper

Learn πθ(a | observation) from demos; the action posterior is multimodal (many valid grasps), so a unimodal regressor averages modes into an invalid action.

Diffusion / flow policies model the full distribution and sample one mode — committing to a decisive action instead of the mean.

Grasp synthesis & generation

both
the problem

An object has infinitely many contact-point pairs, but only a small fraction achieve force closure — the property that the grip can resist a push from any direction. Finding those contact configurations on an arbitrary shape, especially a dexterous hand with many joints, is a high-dimensional nonlinear search over geometry and friction.

the approaches

The core question: given an object, where should the fingers go? Synthesis computes contact placements that achieve force closure, scaling from single objects to huge generated datasets, and now to full dexterous-hand configurations.

Recent work makes generation geometry-aware, physically plausible, and diverse.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: a 3D mesh of a coffee mug (2,840 triangles); target is a 16-DOF Shadow Hand.
  2. Sample candidates: draw 512 contact pairs uniformly from the surface; compute the 6×N grasp matrix G for each pair.
  3. Force-closure test: check if the convex hull of G·f spans zero; 47 of 512 pass the test.
  4. Quality-diversity filter: keep the 8 most diverse passing grasps (BODex style); discard near-duplicates by joint-config distance < 0.15 rad.
  5. Physics refinement: run 200 ms contact simulation per grasp; 5 survive with max wrench resistance > 3 N·m.
  6. Output: 5 stable dexterous grasps, diverse in palm orientation, usable for downstream task-oriented selection.
  • ROBO
    BODex: Scalable Dexterous Grasp Synthesis Using Bilevel Optimization — a QP enforces force closure while the outer level scales dataset generation

    Grasping a complex object like a mug or tool with a dexterous hand (16+ joints) involves searching over an enormous space of possible contact points and configurations. BODex solves force closure via bilevel optimization: the inner loop (quadratic program) enforces that contact forces can resist a push from any direction, while the outer loop scales dataset generation by finding high-quality solutions. The payoff is a tractable pipeline that generates thousands of valid grasps from geometry alone, with no real-world data needed.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Given a 3D mesh and a hand with N fingers, find contact points (position + orientation per finger) such that the grasp matrix G (the wrench-generating rows from each contact's geometry) has a positive span — i.e., the grasp achieves force closure. This is a nonlinear search over a huge space.

    The mechanism: BODex uses bilevel optimization: (1) Inner loop: a QP that, given hand pose, finds contact points enforcing force-closure constraints (‖Gf‖² minimized, positive weights f ≥ 0). (2) Outer loop: evolutionary search or gradient-based methods that adjust hand configuration (palm pose, finger angles) to maximize the quality metric returned by the inner loop. (3) Physics refinement: run contact simulation (collision-free, stable) on the top K candidates.

    Why it works: Decoupling the search (outer loop, discrete) from the verification (inner loop, smooth QP) makes the problem tractable. Repeating this across random configurations and objects builds a large diverse dataset of valid grasps.

  • CVPR
    GeoDexGrasp: Geometry-aware, Physics-plausible Dexterous Grasping — SIM(3)-equivariant pose/shape split + physics refinement cuts penetration 40%

    A grasp synthesis system that ignores the object's actual shape class (mug vs. wrench) may compute grasps that work in theory but fail in simulation or reality due to penetration, misalignment, or wrong contact normals. GeoDexGrasp adds geometry awareness: an equivariant encoder splits the object into pose (position/orientation) and shape parts, then physics-aware refinement removes invalid grasps. The lever is equivariance — respecting geometric symmetries reduces sample complexity and improves generalization across object classes.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Grasp synthesis generates candidates geometrically, but many fail when contact physics is checked (deep penetration, friction too low, hand self-collision). Physics refinement is expensive (one simulation per candidate, minutes for a dataset).

    The mechanism: (1) Equivariant representation: encoder maps object point clouds to a pose descriptor (SE(3) on the object) + shape latent (invariant under object rotation). (2) Grasp generator conditions on the shape latent, not the raw pose, so it learns shape-conditional strategies (e.g., 'cups grasp the rim'). (3) Physics refinement: differentiable simulator evaluates grasps, runs in parallel, filtering the worst 60% before expensive collision checks. (4) Penetration metric: per-contact depth relative to surface normal, optimized away via gradient descent.

    Why it works: Splitting pose and shape removes spurious correlations (e.g., learning object orientation rather than grasping strategy). Physics early (before sim) cuts 40% penetration errors and rejects infeasible grasps before expensive simulation.

  • CVPR
    MaskDexGrasp: Generative Masked Modeling for Part-Aware Grasp Synthesis — anatomical part codebooks + masked transformer, editable per finger

    A mug's handle is a better grasp point than the body; a tool's shaft than its head. But classical grasp synthesis has no notion of semantic parts — it treats the object as an undifferentiated surface. MaskDexGrasp adds part semantics: a masked transformer learns an anatomical part codebook (handle, bowl, spout for a mug), then conditionally generates grasps per part. Part awareness makes grasps both task-aware and editable — the user can request 'grasp the handle' and the model adapts the finger placement.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Generic grasp synthesis ignores semantic parts. Even a high-quality force-closure grasp on the bowl of a mug may be wrong if the task demands handing it to someone (which requires grasping the handle safely).

    The mechanism: (1) Part codebook learning: VQ-VAE learns a discrete codebook of anatomical part embeddings (handle, spout, body, etc.) on a dataset of part-annotated 3D objects. (2) Masked modeling: transformer predicts masked-out part tokens from visible parts (autoregressive or masked-language-model style). (3) Conditional grasp generation: once parts are identified (or user-specified), a separate transformer samples grasp configurations conditioned on the part embedding. (4) Per-finger editing: user can specify 'thumb on handle, fingers on bowl' and the model adjusts the grasp to satisfy constraints.

    Why it works: Part semantics reduce the search space (grasping the handle is a smaller, more coherent set than any contact on the mug) and make the system interpretable and user-controllable, unlike black-box neural graspers.

  • ROBO
    Qdgset: a Large-Scale Grasping Dataset via Quality-Diversity — quality-diversity search generates diverse grasps at scale

    Creating a large dataset of valid grasps by simulation is expensive: most randomly sampled grasps fail force-closure tests. Quality-diversity search flips the problem: instead of 'find the best grasp,' it asks 'find many grasps, each good in a different way.' Quality-diversity optimization fills a diversity map with high-quality solutions, ensuring the dataset spans many grasp styles (antipodal, power, pinch) and object poses. This single algorithmic shift turns a thin set of 'optimized' grasps into a rich distribution that learned models can fit.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Grasp datasets trained on a narrow set of high-quality 'winners' teach a policy to overfit to a single style. Real objects afford many valid grasps, and a learned policy should capture that diversity. Growing such a dataset by random sampling and filtering is extremely slow.

    The mechanism: Quality-diversity algorithms (e.g., MAP-Elites) maintain a grid (diversity map) where each cell is defined by a behavior descriptor (e.g., contact location on the object, hand orientation). The algorithm iteratively: (1) Samples a grasp config; (2) Simulates it, measuring both quality (success rate, wrench resistance) and the behavior descriptor; (3) If the quality is better than the incumbent in that cell, replace it. (4) The result is a population of grasps spanning the behavior space, each locally optimized.

    Why it works: MAP-Elites incentivizes both quality and diversity without explicit multi-objective scalarization. A learned grasp detector trained on Qdgset learns a mode-seeking policy naturally because the data itself is multimodal and the model's diversity helps it generalize.

  • CVPR
    DextER: Language-driven Dexterous Grasp Generation with Embodied Reasoning — contact-token autoregression for interpretable, language-driven grasps

    Most grasp systems operate in a vacuum: 'where should the fingers go?' But real grasping is task-laden: you grasp a knife by the blade to hand it over, by the handle to cut. DextER embeds language-driven task reasoning into grasp generation. An autoregressive token model (discrete contact tokens) reasons over object affordances and language instructions, then generates contact sequences explicitly. Contact-token autoregression makes reasoning transparent — you can see which finger touches where and why, unlike a black-box neural network.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Language-conditional grasping requires bridging two gaps: (1) from language ('hand the knife') to task semantics ('grasp the blade'); (2) from semantics to contact points. End-to-end networks struggle with interpretability and often hallucinate contacts unrelated to the language.

    The mechanism: (1) Embodied reasoning module: VLM (or fine-tuned transformer) reasons step-by-step over the scene, object, and language ('To hand a knife, I should grasp the blade away from the sharp edge'). (2) Contact token vocabulary: discrete codebook of contact primitives (e.g., 'thumb on handle, index at edge, pressure 2 N'). (3) Autoregressive generation: language embedding + scene embedding condition a transformer that generates contact tokens one by one (thumb, then index, etc.), each outputting a discrete contact pose. (4) Physics validation: each predicted contact is checked for force closure with already-placed contacts.

    Why it works: Discrete contact tokens bottleneck the model to physically valid sequences (must close the grasp) and make the decision tree interpretable. The autoregressive formulation naturally handles the sequential nature of finger placement (some contacts must come before others to avoid self-collision).

02

Grasp detection & 6-DoF pose

both
the problem

A robot looking at a bin of objects must decide, in under 100 ms, exactly where and how to place its gripper — a 6-degree-of-freedom prediction (3 position + 3 orientation) from a single depth image. The hard cases are transparent glass, mirror-like metal, and partially hidden objects where the depth sensor gives garbage or nothing.

the approaches

Skip the model: predict grasps — and object poses — straight from vision, handling material, occlusion, and uncertainty. This is the workhorse that turns a depth image into something to grab.

The edges are transparent/specular objects, multi-view fusion, and honest uncertainty.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: one 640×480 RGB-D frame; a wine glass (transparent) sits in a cluttered bin.
  2. Surface reconstruction: NeuGrasp trains a neural signed-distance function on multi-view observations; fills the transparent surface with plausible geometry.
  3. Grasp candidate generation: sample 1,024 grasp poses from the completed surface (GraspSAM variant); each pose is (x,y,z, roll, pitch, yaw).
  4. Scoring: a small MLP scores each by predicted antipodal quality and collision clearance; top-32 kept.
  5. Refinement: gradient-descent on the score field for 20 steps (step size 1 mm / 0.5°) moves each toward a local maximum.
  6. Output: best pose with predicted success probability 0.87; gripper opens 68 mm, approaches from 120° above horizontal.
  • ROBO
    GraspSAM: When Segment Anything Meets Grasp Detection — adapts SAM with light adapters for promptable grasp detection

    Segment Anything Model (SAM) is a foundation model trained on billions of pixels to segment anything on cue. GraspSAM asks: can we adapt SAM to segment and detect grasps? The answer is yes, with light adapters. A user points to an object ('prompt this object for grasps') and a small adapter layer fine-tunes SAM's mask to the grasp domain. Prompting replaces training — the same foundation model adapts to new objects, scenes, and even gripper types without retraining.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Grasp detection requires both segmentation (which pixels are the object) and pose estimation (where/how to grasp). Training a detector from scratch on new objects is slow. SAM can segment, but it knows nothing about grasps.

    The mechanism: (1) Foundation model: SAM encoder extracts rich image features (millions of parameters, frozen). (2) Grasp adapter: a small trainable head (few hundred parameters) learns to map SAM's features to grasp-relevant masks and confidence scores. (3) Prompt types: point (click on object), box, or mask prompt tells SAM which object to focus on. (4) Multi-scale decoding: adapter operates at multiple scales (coarse to fine) to handle small grippers and large objects. (5) Grasp pose regression: from the segmented region, regress 6-DoF gripper pose (center, orientation, width).

    Why it works: SAM's pre-training on diverse segmentation tasks teaches it rich representations of boundaries and shape, which transfer to grasp boundaries (where the gripper closes). The adapter is tiny, so few-shot fine-tuning (even zero-shot prompting) works. Different gripper types require only prompt variation, not retraining.

  • ROBO
    NeuGrasp: Material-Agnostic Grasp Detection via Neural Surface Reconstruction — neural surfaces recover transparent/specular geometry for grasping

    Transparent glass, reflective metal, and translucent plastic are invisible to standard depth sensors: the depth image is garbage or missing. A robot that depends on depth for grasp detection fails on these materials. NeuGrasp learns to reconstruct the hidden surface: a neural SDF (signed-distance function) trained on multi-view observations infers the likely 3D surface of the transparent object, then grasp detection proceeds on the reconstructed geometry. Neural surface inpainting fills in what the sensor cannot see — a generative model completes the object's shape.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Transparent, specular, and semi-transparent materials break depth sensors. RGB images alone offer limited surface cues. A robot cannot grasp if it cannot see (or infer) where the object is.

    The mechanism: (1) Multi-view collection: move the camera around the object or object around the camera (or both); collect RGB images from 4-6 views. (2) SDF learning: a small MLP(x, y, z) → distance-to-surface is trained to minimize the photo-consistency loss across views (pixels should be sharp when rendered from the SDF at the known poses). (3) Implicit regularization: the SDF naturally prefers smooth surfaces and fills gaps (inpainting) where views disagree. (4) Grasp candidate generation: sample points on the learned SDF surface, compute normals, and generate antipodal grasp pairs. (5) Scoring: grasp quality metrics (contact area, force closure) are evaluated on the reconstructed surface, not the broken depth image.

    Why it works: Neural SDFs are continuous and differentiable, so they smoothly interpolate across occlusions and handle specular reflections better than depth fusion. The implicit regularization biases the solution toward plausible (closed, smooth) surfaces even when depth is unreliable.

  • CVPR
    A Cross-view Fusion Framework for Robust 6-DoF Grasp Pose Estimation — cross-view attention fuses multi-view RGB-D with adaptive weighting

    A single RGB-D view may occlude part of the object or show it at a poor viewing angle for grasp detection. Moving the camera or looking from multiple angles gives much more information: the object's full 3D shape, all grasping surfaces, and confidence in each grasp's feasibility. This paper fuses multi-view RGB-D observations with adaptive attention, where the model learns to weight which views are most informative. Adaptive cross-view attention prioritizes the most confident views — a occlusion-reliable mechanism built into the architecture.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Single-view grasp detection is brittle: occlusions hide grasping surfaces, viewing angle distortion makes pose estimation noisy, and confidence is hard to calibrate. Multiple views provide redundancy, but naive averaging can muddle occlusions (averaging a grasp on empty air with one on the object).

    The mechanism: (1) Multi-view encoder: for each of K views, an image encoder (CNN or ViT) extracts per-pixel features. (2) View alignment: features are warped to a canonical 3D coordinate frame using camera poses (calibrated or estimated via structure-from-motion). (3) Cross-view attention: a transformer layer computes attention weights *across* views, with queries from one view and keys/values from all views. High attention means 'this view is informative here.' (4) Grasp refinement: the fused feature map goes to a pose regressor (6D predictor: x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw) and a quality confidence map. (5) Per-view quality: the attention weights also serve as view-specific confidence scores; if one view is blurry, it gets down-weighted automatically.

    Why it works: Learned adaptive attention is more reliable than hardcoded fusion (e.g., averaging) because it can handle occlusions, noise, and blur gracefully. The transformer learns to say 'ignore this blurry view' and 'trust this sharp one,' which single-view or early-fusion methods cannot do.

  • ROBO
    vMF-Contact: Uncertainty-Aware Evidential Learning for Contact-Grasp — models aleatoric + epistemic uncertainty for noisy clutter

    A grasp detector trained on clean, well-lit data may confidently predict grasps in a cluttered, shadowy bin — and fail. The model does not know what it does not know. vMF-Contact trains the detector to output not just a grasp pose but also uncertainty: aleatoric uncertainty (sensor noise, inherent ambiguity) and epistemic uncertainty (the model's ignorance of out-of-distribution clutter). Evidential learning models belief distributions over poses — von Mises-Fisher (vMF) distributions capture orientation uncertainty, Gaussian distributions capture position uncertainty.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Neural grasp detectors output point estimates (single pose) without uncertainty. In a cluttered bin, when the scene is ambiguous, the detector should either abstain or flag high uncertainty, but it does not. This leads to 'confident errors' that cause picks to fail.

    The mechanism: (1) Evidential system: instead of regressing a single grasp pose, the network outputs parameters of a belief distribution. For orientation: a von Mises-Fisher distribution (concentration κ and mean direction μ on SO(3)). For position: a Gaussian (mean, variance). (2) Loss function: evidential loss encourages the network to widen the variance when uncertain (large κ⁻¹) and narrow it when confident. Incorrect predictions are penalized by both the likelihood and the evidence (how concentrated the distribution is). (3) Aleatoric term: comes from sensor noise; captured by the width of the distribution. (4) Epistemic term: model uncertainty; captured by network dropout or ensemble disagreement, reflected in variance scaling. (5) Inference: sample poses from the belief distribution; if variance is high, the grasp is risky.

    Why it works: Evidential learning forces the model to admit doubt when data is ambiguous or out-of-distribution. Clutter introduces high epistemic uncertainty (the model sees occlusions it was not trained on), which inflates the predicted variance, allowing a downstream planner to reject risky grasps or trigger multi-view collection.

  • CVPR
    ComPose: Unified Completion-Pose for Category-Level Object Pose — joint shape completion + 6-DoF pose under occlusion

    A partially hidden object (say, a cup inside a bin with another cup on top of it) has an invisible base, but its category (cup) and visible parts (rim, handle) hint at what the full shape should be. ComPose jointly solves shape completion and 6-DoF pose under occlusion: a single model outputs both a completed point cloud and the object's 3D pose. Joint optimization couples geometry and pose — the reconstructed shape is consistent with the observed partial shape and the inferred pose.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Separate completion and pose estimation is suboptimal. A shape-completion model may hallucinate an implausible geometry (e.g., fill the underside with random points), and a pose regressor sees only the partial object, leading to drift. Coupling them constrains the solution space: the completed shape must explain the observations and be pose-consistent.

    The mechanism: (1) Category-level prior: during training, the model learns a per-category implicit function (e.g., all mugs share a canonical shape). (2) Partial observation encoding: the visible points (and their mask) are encoded via a point-net encoder. (3) Unified decoder: outputs two heads: (a) shape parameters (deformation of the canonical shape); (b) 6-DoF pose (rotation + translation). (4) Consistency loss: the predicted pose and shape are checked: render the completed shape at the predicted pose; it should match the observed partial points (photo-consistency). (5) Geometric loss: completed shape should be closed, have plausible curvature, not interpenetrate known obstacles.

    Why it works: Multi-task learning with a shared representation (encoding + decoder) encourages the model to find a pose and shape that are mutually consistent. A shape completion alone might invent geometry; pose alone might misalign. Together, they reinforce each other.

Dexterous & in-hand manipulation

both
the problem

A parallel gripper can clamp; a multi-fingered hand must re-orient an object already held — turning a screwdriver 180° without dropping it. This requires a planned sequence of finger lifts and re-placements (finger gaiting) while the remaining contacts keep the object stable, all coordinated across 20+ joints with tactile feedback.

the approaches

Multi-finger hands reorient objects in-hand by coordinating many joints through making and breaking contact — finger gaiting, regrasp, sliding. Tactile feedback disambiguates which finger touches where.

RL in simulation plus sim-to-real touch has driven the biggest gains here.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: rotate a cylinder 180° in-hand using a 4-finger, 16-DOF hand; initial grip is side-on, target is end-on.
  2. Simulation training (RL): 500 M steps in Isaac Gym; reward = negative angular error to target; AnyRotate-style dense tactile sim at 256 sensor points per finger.
  3. Gait schedule found by RL: finger 0 lifts → rotates 45° → re-plants; repeat for fingers 1, 2, 3 in sequence; full 180° in 4 gaits.
  4. Sim-to-real transfer: tactile randomisation (noise ±8 mN) + domain randomisation (friction 0.6–1.2); policy runs at 50 Hz on real hand.
  5. Real result: 78% success on cylinder, 61% on screwdriver; failure mode is slip during the third gait when mass > 200 g.
  6. Fix: hierarchical policy sequences a 'rotate-30°' primitive 6× rather than tracking continuous angle; raises success to 83%.
  • ROBO
    ViViDex: Vision-Based Dexterous Manipulation from Human Videos — trajectory-guided rewards from human video, vision-only rollouts

    A multi-fingered hand can reorient an object in-hand by a sequence of finger lifts and replants (finger gaiting), but this requires precise coordination across 20+ joints. Instead of hand-engineering the policy or learning from sparse real-robot data, ViViDex learns from unlabeled human videos: extract human hand trajectories, compute object motion, then train a vision-based policy with trajectory-guided rewards that encourage the robot to imitate the object motion. Trajectory-guided rewards provide dense learning signal from passive video — no touch sensor, no real-robot interaction needed for training.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Learning dexterous manipulation on a real hand is sample-hungry (thousands of trials) and risky (fingers wear out, objects break). Supervised learning from kinesthetic demonstrations is slow. Reward-only RL requires a hand-crafted reward function, which is brittle.

    The mechanism: (1) Video dataset: collect human hand videos manipulating objects (e.g., rotating a ball in-hand). (2) Trajectory extraction: use hand-pose and object-pose estimators (HaMeR + FoundationPose) to extract frame-by-frame hand configuration and object position/orientation. (3) Reward function: the robot's reward is the similarity between (a) the observed object trajectory in the video and (b) the object trajectory from the robot's action. Metric: e.g., L2 distance in SO(3) × ℝ³ for object pose, or relative object motion. (4) Vision-only rollout: the policy takes RGB observations from the robot's wrist camera (same camera as the video source). (5) Imitation: RL (e.g., PPO) trains the policy to match the object trajectory, not the hand trajectory (hand geometry differs). (6) Refinement: domain randomize camera pose, lighting, object texture to improve sim-to-real transfer.

robotics
the problem

In a real bin, objects stack on top of each other: you can see the top of a cup but its base is hidden behind a bottle. A robot that plans to grasp the cup may collide with the bottle it cannot see. Amodal reasoning — estimating the complete shape of an object from a partial view — and reasoning about which objects physically block which grasps are both required before the first pick.

the approaches

Real bins are messy: objects occlude each other and only part of each is visible. The robot must segment instances, complete hidden shape, and reason about what blocks what.

worked example — one concrete run

  5. Clear-first sequence: remove the 2 blocking objects (easier grasps) first; then grasp spray bottle.
  6. Result: 82% task-success vs 54% without amodal reasoning; the 18% failures come from under-segmented instances where two objects are merged.

    how it actually works

    Why it works: Obstruction analysis identifies the true dependencies in the scene; topological sort ensures no circular dependencies are created. Manipulation policies & imitation learning

both
the problem

Learning the whole pick-to-place skill from 50 human demonstrations sounds like enough, but the actions at each timestep are not unimodal: there are five equally good ways to approach a mug, and a network that averages them outputs a path down the middle that grabs nothing. Capturing the full distribution of valid actions and committing to one clean choice — without collapsing the modes — is the central statistical problem of imitation learning for manipulation.

the approaches

Learn the whole visuomotor policy from demonstrations — observations to continuous actions — handling the multimodality of valid actions and generalizing across tasks and embodiments.

Diffusion transformers, recovery behaviors, and model merging are the current tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Dataset: 50 human tele-operation demos of picking a mug and placing it on a coaster; 30 Hz, 7-DOF arm, wrist RGB.

  4. Inference: 100-step DDPM denoising at 10 Hz (real-time at 16-step chunk); picks one of 3 approach modes deterministically from the noise seed.
  5. Result: 84% success on seen mugs, 61% on novel objects; REACH recovery policy kicks in on OOD detections (cosine distance > 0.7 from training distribution).

    how it actually works

    PAD-Hand: Physics-Aware Diffusion for Hand Motion Recovery — probabilistic Euler-Lagrange dynamics with variance maps

    Recovering hand motion from monocular video is typically framed as a per-frame pose regression, which treats each frame independently and ignores the physics constraints that smooth, continuous motion obeys. A probabilistic Euler-Lagrange diffusion model can enforce momentum and contact by diffusing over physically plausible hand trajectories instead of individual poses.

    how it actually works
    1. Forward process (diffusion): Start with a plausible motion sequence (from a pre-trained hand-pose model) and gradually corrupt it with Gaussian noise over T steps, learning the forward process distribution of physically valid motions.
    2. Reverse process: A neural network learns to denoise the corrupted trajectory, parameterized by velocity and acceleration (Euler-Lagrange coordinates) rather than joint angles, so the Lagrangian mechanics constraints are implicit.
    3. Variance scheduling: The model learns per-joint uncertainty (variance maps), so high-uncertainty frames (occlusion, ambiguous poses) produce wider distributions, while clear frames converge to narrow peaks.
    4. Contact grounding: When the hand touches an object, a soft contact loss pulls the fingertip trajectory to lie on the object surface; when not in contact, the loss is zero.
    5. Why it works: Physics-aware parameterization (Lagrangian) makes smooth motion the prior; diffusion naturally models multimodal solutions (multiple valid grasping trajectories); uncertainty maps let the model express doubt rather than force a point estimate.

    how it actually works

Sim-to-real for manipulation

both
the problem

A policy trained in simulation for 10 billion steps can fail immediately on the real robot — not because the task is too hard, but because the real table has slightly different friction, the camera has a 5 ms delay, and the joint encoders add 0.5° noise. These small mismatches compound across a manipulation sequence and cause the policy to drift off course within the first 3 seconds. Closing this gap without per-task real-world fine-tuning is the open problem.

the approaches

Manipulation policies are mostly trained in simulation, where data is cheap and failures are free — then they must survive real friction, sensor noise, and delay. Closing that last gap is its own discipline.

Teacher-student distillation, delta actions, and tactile co-training bridge it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Policy: a pixel-to-action diffusion policy for door-opening, trained 100 M steps in IsaacGym.
  2. Sim domain randomisation: friction μ ∈ [0.3, 1.2], door-handle diameter ±2 mm, lighting ±40% intensity, camera pose ±1 cm / ±2°, sensor delay ∈ [0, 20 ms].
  3. Teacher-student transfer (VIRAL style): teacher has privileged state access (perfect pose); student sees only pixel observations; student learns delta-action residuals on top of teacher's actions.
  4. Stage-reset exploration: initialise rollouts from 10 stages of door-opening (GRPO method); each stage trains the policy on the hardest 20% of resets.
  5. Real evaluation: 83% success on the real door after zero real-robot fine-tuning (vs 41% without domain randomisation); mean episode time 4.6 s.
  6. Remaining gap: success drops to 61% on a door with a lever handle (never seen in sim); few-shot real adaptation (5 demos) recovers to 79%.

    Learning Dual-Arm Coordination for Grasping Large Flat Objects — zero-shot transferable dual-arm RL, sim-to-real validated

    Bimanual grasping of large sheets (plywood, glass, textiles) requires both arms to coordinate their forces so the object doesn't twist or fold. Each arm cannot be controlled independently; instead, they must maintain a desired relative grasp configuration.     how it actually works

    5. Sim-to-real: Friction and damping randomization in simulation; on the real robot, tactile feedback at each gripper lets the policy detect slip and compensate with grip force.
    6. Why it works: Joint action spaces couple the arms naturally; object-level penalties (curvature) align the policy with actual task success, not individual arm objectives; sim-to-real tactile feedback disambiguates which arm is slipping without intrusive joint-level tuning.

Part IV

Where it stands

Manipulating the real world as fluently as a person is far from solved. What is genuinely still hard:

Contact you can’t differentiate through. The physics jumps between stick, slip, and separation; smooth optimizers stall at the switches, and learning contact-rich skills stays sample-hungry.
Generalizing to every object. Even with foundation-model priors, grasping and manipulation still lean on large pre-training; true one-shot skill on arbitrary objects is unsolved.
The last 10% of sim-to-real. Policies plateau at 80–90% real success; unmodeled friction, delay, and calibration eat the rest, and closing it without per-task tuning is open.
Deformables at scale. Cloth, rope, and fluids have unbounded state and defeat generic models — each still needs a good deal of per-object engineering.
Grasp meets long-horizon reasoning. Choosing a grasp that serves the next step — and clearing what obstructs it — needs scene reasoning most systems don’t yet have.
Tactile that transfers. Touch sensors vary wildly and the tactile sim-to-real gap is larger than vision’s; policies rarely move between sensor types without retraining.
