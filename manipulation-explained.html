01 · the problem

Everything happens through contact

Perception can happen at a distance, but changing the world cannot. To move, open, place, or assemble anything, a robot has to make physical contact — and that contact is the only channel through which it can push the world around.

This is what makes manipulation its own hard problem. Control only exists while the robot is touching; the forces have to point the right way or the object slips or crushes; and the instant a finger touches down or lifts off, the physics changes character. Grasping and manipulation are the science of shaping the world through those fleeting contacts.

the actual math — one level deeper

A contact applies a wrench (force + torque) to the object; the robot can only change the object’s motion through the sum of contact wrenches — no contact, no control authority.

Making/breaking contact switches the system’s equations of motion, so manipulation dynamics are hybrid, not smooth.

02 · hold it

A grasp is a cage of forces

What makes a grasp actually hold? Not raw squeezing force, but where the contacts are. Each fingertip can only push inward and, thanks to friction, resist some sideways force — a ‘cone’ of forces it can apply. Place the fingers well and those cones together can cancel a push or twist from any direction.

That property is called force closure, and it’s the difference between a grip that survives a bump and one that drops the object. Much of grasping is finding, from an object’s shape, contact points that achieve it — fast, and even when you can only see part of the object.

the actual math — one level deeper

Force closure: the contact wrenches positively span the whole wrench space — any external wrench can be resisted by a non-negative combination of contact forces.

Each contact obeys the friction cone ‖f_t‖ ≤ μ f_n (tangential force bounded by friction times normal force); grasp synthesis places contacts so the cones jointly close.

03 · the hard part

Contact makes the physics jump

Away from contact, a robot’s motion is smooth and predictable. The moment it touches, everything changes: pressing a peg into a hole, the system jumps between distinct modes — floating free, touching at one point, wedged at two, sliding home — and each mode follows different equations.

That’s why contact-rich tasks resist the usual tools. You can’t just follow a smooth gradient to the goal, because the gradient breaks at every mode switch. Handling these non-smooth, hybrid dynamics — with force feedback, clever solvers, or learning — is the core challenge of assembly, insertion, and tool use.

the actual math — one level deeper

Contact is a complementarity condition: 0 ≤ force ⊥ gap ≥ 0 — either touching (gap 0, force ≥ 0) or separated (gap > 0, force 0).

This makes the dynamics non-smooth and mode-switched (stick / slip / separate); gradients are undefined across switches, so smooth optimizers stall.

04 · dexterity

Rearranging the grip itself

A parallel gripper can only clamp; a multi-fingered hand can rearrange. To turn an object in place, the hand lifts one finger, moves it to a new spot, and presses again — all while the remaining fingers keep the object secure. Repeat, and the object rotates without ever being dropped.

This is finger gaiting, and it demands coordinating many joints through a constantly changing pattern of which fingers are in contact where. It’s the hardest, most human part of manipulation — and where learned policies and tactile feedback have made the biggest recent gains.

the actual math — one level deeper

A dexterous hand has many DoF; reorientation is a sequence of sub-grasps, each a force-closure grip, linked by finger gaiting (relocate one contact while others hold).

Planning is over both continuous finger motion and the discrete contact schedule — a hybrid problem RL handles well in simulation.

05 · learn it

Many good grasps, commit to one

You can’t hand-code every grasp, so modern manipulation is learned — often by imitation from a handful of human demonstrations. But there’s a subtlety: a mug affords many good grasps, and the policy has to represent that whole set of valid options.

The trap is averaging. Blend two perfectly good grasps and you can land on the bad one in between — a grip on empty air. This is exactly why diffusion and flow-matching policies took over: they can hold a multimodal distribution of actions and commit to one clean choice, instead of collapsing to a mushy average.

the actual math — one level deeper

Learn πθ(a | observation) from demos; the action posterior is multimodal (many valid grasps), so a unimodal regressor averages modes into an invalid action.

Diffusion / flow policies model the full distribution and sample one mode — committing to a decisive action instead of the mean.

Grasp synthesis & generation

both
the problem

An object has infinitely many contact-point pairs, but only a small fraction achieve force closure — the property that the grip can resist a push from any direction. Finding those contact configurations on an arbitrary shape, especially a dexterous hand with many joints, is a high-dimensional nonlinear search over geometry and friction.

the approaches

The core question: given an object, where should the fingers go? Synthesis computes contact placements that achieve force closure, scaling from single objects to huge generated datasets, and now to full dexterous-hand configurations.

Recent work makes generation geometry-aware, physically plausible, and diverse.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: a 3D mesh of a coffee mug (2,840 triangles); target is a 16-DOF Shadow Hand.
  2. Sample candidates: draw 512 contact pairs uniformly from the surface; compute the 6×N grasp matrix G for each pair.
  3. Force-closure test: check if the convex hull of G·f spans zero; 47 of 512 pass the test.
  4. Quality-diversity filter: keep the 8 most diverse passing grasps (BODex style); discard near-duplicates by joint-config distance < 0.15 rad.
  5. Physics refinement: run 200 ms contact simulation per grasp; 5 survive with max wrench resistance > 3 N·m.
  6. Output: 5 stable dexterous grasps, diverse in palm orientation, usable for downstream task-oriented selection.

Grasp detection & 6-DoF pose

both
the problem

A robot looking at a bin of objects must decide, in under 100 ms, exactly where and how to place its gripper — a 6-degree-of-freedom prediction (3 position + 3 orientation) from a single depth image. The hard cases are transparent glass, mirror-like metal, and partially hidden objects where the depth sensor gives garbage or nothing.

the approaches

Skip the model: predict grasps — and object poses — straight from vision, handling material, occlusion, and uncertainty. This is the workhorse that turns a depth image into something to grab.

The edges are transparent/specular objects, multi-view fusion, and honest uncertainty.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: one 640×480 RGB-D frame; a wine glass (transparent) sits in a cluttered bin.
  2. Surface reconstruction: NeuGrasp trains a neural signed-distance function on multi-view observations; fills the transparent surface with plausible geometry.
  3. Grasp candidate generation: sample 1,024 grasp poses from the completed surface (GraspSAM variant); each pose is (x,y,z, roll, pitch, yaw).
  4. Scoring: a small MLP scores each by predicted antipodal quality and collision clearance; top-32 kept.
  5. Refinement: gradient-descent on the score field for 20 steps (step size 1 mm / 0.5°) moves each toward a local maximum.
  6. Output: best pose with predicted success probability 0.87; gripper opens 68 mm, approaches from 120° above horizontal.

Dexterous & in-hand manipulation

both
the problem

A parallel gripper can clamp; a multi-fingered hand must re-orient an object already held — turning a screwdriver 180° without dropping it. Hand-object interaction: reconstruction & synthesis

vision
the problem

To teach a robot from video, you need to recover how the human hand was gripping the object — its 3D pose and all 21 joint angles — from a monocular camera that sees only a 2D projection, with the hand frequently hidden behind the object. The reconstruction must also be physically plausible: fingers must not float in the air or penetrate the object surface.

the approaches

Recover, or generate, how a hand grips and moves an object — from monocular video, under occlusion, obeying physics (no floating, no penetration). This is the perception side that teaches robots how humans manipulate.

Foundation models, discrete hand tokens, and physics-aware diffusion drive it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 30-second monocular video of a person picking up and turning a mug; hand occluded ~40% of frames.
  2. Stage 1 — initial estimates: HaMeR hand-pose model + FoundationPose object-pose model run per-frame; gives noisy (x,y,z, 21 joints) estimates with floating fingers in 30% of frames.
  3. Stage 2 — contact reasoning (ArtHOI): MLLM prompted with rendered hand+object image: 'Is the index fingertip in contact with the mug handle?' → binary contact labels per finger per frame.
  4. Stage 3 — physics refinement: RL agent (PAD-Hand style) minimises: penetration depth + contact gap + joint-velocity smoothness, 200 optimisation steps per second of video.
  5. Stage 4 — mesh output: TokenHand VQ-VAE decodes the refined joint angles to a smooth mesh at 65 FPS; outputs MANO parameters for downstream retargeting.
  6. Result: mean per-joint error 8.3 mm (vs 14.1 mm baseline); zero frames with finger penetration > 2 mm after refinement.

Sim-to-real for manipulation

both
the problem

A policy trained in simulation for 10 billion steps can fail immediately on the real robot — not because the task is too hard, but because the real table has slightly different friction, the camera has a 5 ms delay, and the joint encoders add 0.5° noise. These small mismatches compound across a manipulation sequence and cause the policy to drift off course within the first 3 seconds. Closing this gap without per-task real-world fine-tuning is the open problem.

the approaches

Manipulation policies are mostly trained in simulation, where data is cheap and failures are free — then they must survive real friction, sensor noise, and delay. Closing that last gap is its own discipline.

Teacher-student distillation, delta actions, and tactile co-training bridge it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Policy: a pixel-to-action diffusion policy for door-opening, trained 100 M steps in IsaacGym.
  2. Sim domain randomisation: friction μ ∈ [0.3, 1.2], door-handle diameter ±2 mm, lighting ±40% intensity, camera pose ±1 cm / ±2°, sensor delay ∈ [0, 20 ms].
  3. Teacher-student transfer (VIRAL style): teacher has privileged state access (perfect pose); student sees only pixel observations; student learns delta-action residuals on top of teacher's actions.
  4. Stage-reset exploration: initialise rollouts from 10 stages of door-opening (GRPO method); each stage trains the policy on the hardest 20% of resets.
  5. Real evaluation: 83% success on the real door after zero real-robot fine-tuning (vs 41% without domain randomisation); mean episode time 4.6 s.
  6. Remaining gap: success drops to 61% on a door with a lever handle (never seen in sim); few-shot real adaptation (5 demos) recovers to 79%.

Part IV

Where it stands

Manipulating the real world as fluently as a person is far from solved. What is genuinely still hard:

Contact you can’t differentiate through. The physics jumps between stick, slip, and separation; smooth optimizers stall at the switches, and learning contact-rich skills stays sample-hungry.
Generalizing to every object. Even with foundation-model priors, grasping and manipulation still lean on large pre-training; true one-shot skill on arbitrary objects is unsolved.
The last 10% of sim-to-real. Policies plateau at 80–90% real success; unmodeled friction, delay, and calibration eat the rest, and closing it without per-task tuning is open.
Deformables at scale. Cloth, rope, and fluids have unbounded state and defeat generic models — each still needs a good deal of per-object engineering.
Grasp meets long-horizon reasoning. Choosing a grasp that serves the next step — and clearing what obstructs it — needs scene reasoning most systems don’t yet have.
Tactile that transfers. Touch sensors vary wildly and the tactile sim-to-real gap is larger than vision’s; policies rarely move between sensor types without retraining.
