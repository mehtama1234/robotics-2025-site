01 · the problem

Everything happens through contact

Perception can happen at a distance, but changing the world cannot. To move, open, place, or assemble anything, a robot has to make physical contact — and that contact is the only channel through which it can push the world around.

This is what makes manipulation its own hard problem. Control only exists while the robot is touching; the forces have to point the right way or the object slips or crushes; and the instant a finger touches down or lifts off, the physics changes character. Grasping and manipulation are the science of shaping the world through those fleeting contacts.

the actual math — one level deeper

A contact applies a wrench (force + torque) to the object; the robot can only change the object’s motion through the sum of contact wrenches — no contact, no control authority.

Making/breaking contact switches the system’s equations of motion, so manipulation dynamics are hybrid, not smooth.

02 · hold it

A grasp is a cage of forces

What makes a grasp actually hold? Not raw squeezing force, but where the contacts are. Each fingertip can only push inward and, thanks to friction, resist some sideways force — a ‘cone’ of forces it can apply. Place the fingers well and those cones together can cancel a push or twist from any direction.

That property is called force closure, and it’s the difference between a grip that survives a bump and one that drops the object. Much of grasping is finding, from an object’s shape, contact points that achieve it — fast, and even when you can only see part of the object.

the actual math — one level deeper

Force closure: the contact wrenches positively span the whole wrench space — any external wrench can be resisted by a non-negative combination of contact forces.

Each contact obeys the friction cone ‖f_t‖ ≤ μ f_n (tangential force bounded by friction times normal force); grasp synthesis places contacts so the cones jointly close.

03 · the hard part

Contact makes the physics jump

Away from contact, a robot’s motion is smooth and predictable. The moment it touches, everything changes: pressing a peg into a hole, the system jumps between distinct modes — floating free, touching at one point, wedged at two, sliding home — and each mode follows different equations.

That’s why contact-rich tasks resist the usual tools. You can’t just follow a smooth gradient to the goal, because the gradient breaks at every mode switch. Handling these non-smooth, hybrid dynamics — with force feedback, clever solvers, or learning — is the core challenge of assembly, insertion, and tool use.

the actual math — one level deeper

Contact is a complementarity condition: 0 ≤ force ⊥ gap ≥ 0 — either touching (gap 0, force ≥ 0) or separated (gap > 0, force 0).

This makes the dynamics non-smooth and mode-switched (stick / slip / separate); gradients are undefined across switches, so smooth optimizers stall.

04 · dexterity

Rearranging the grip itself

A parallel gripper can only clamp; a multi-fingered hand can rearrange. To turn an object in place, the hand lifts one finger, moves it to a new spot, and presses again — all while the remaining fingers keep the object secure. Repeat, and the object rotates without ever being dropped.

This is finger gaiting, and it demands coordinating many joints through a constantly changing pattern of which fingers are in contact where. It’s the hardest, most human part of manipulation — and where learned policies and tactile feedback have made the biggest recent gains.

the actual math — one level deeper

A dexterous hand has many DoF; reorientation is a sequence of sub-grasps, each a force-closure grip, linked by finger gaiting (relocate one contact while others hold).

Planning is over both continuous finger motion and the discrete contact schedule — a hybrid problem RL handles well in simulation.

05 · learn it

Many good grasps, commit to one

You can’t hand-code every grasp, so modern manipulation is learned — often by imitation from a handful of human demonstrations. But there’s a subtlety: a mug affords many good grasps, and the policy has to represent that whole set of valid options.

The trap is averaging. Blend two perfectly good grasps and you can land on the bad one in between — a grip on empty air. This is exactly why diffusion and flow-matching policies took over: they can hold a multimodal distribution of actions and commit to one clean choice, instead of collapsing to a mushy average.

the actual math — one level deeper

Learn πθ(a | observation) from demos; the action posterior is multimodal (many valid grasps), so a unimodal regressor averages modes into an invalid action.

Diffusion / flow policies model the full distribution and sample one mode — committing to a decisive action instead of the mean.

Grasp synthesis & generation

both
the problem

An object has infinitely many contact-point pairs, but only a small fraction achieve force closure — the property that the grip can resist a push from any direction. Finding those contact configurations on an arbitrary shape, especially a dexterous hand with many joints, is a high-dimensional nonlinear search over geometry and friction.

the approaches

The core question: given an object, where should the fingers go? Synthesis computes contact placements that achieve force closure, scaling from single objects to huge generated datasets, and now to full dexterous-hand configurations.

Recent work makes generation geometry-aware, physically plausible, and diverse.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: a 3D mesh of a coffee mug (2,840 triangles); target is a 16-DOF Shadow Hand.
  2. Sample candidates: draw 512 contact pairs uniformly from the surface; compute the 6×N grasp matrix G for each pair.
  3. Force-closure test: check if the convex hull of G·f spans zero; 47 of 512 pass the test.
  4. Quality-diversity filter: keep the 8 most diverse passing grasps (BODex style); discard near-duplicates by joint-config distance < 0.15 rad.
  5. Physics refinement: run 200 ms contact simulation per grasp; 5 survive with max wrench resistance > 3 N·m.
  6. Output: 5 stable dexterous grasps, diverse in palm orientation, usable for downstream task-oriented selection.
  • ROBOBODex: Scalable Dexterous Grasp Synthesis Using Bilevel Optimization — a QP enforces force closure while the outer level scales dataset generation
  • CVPRGeoDexGrasp: Geometry-aware, Physics-plausible Dexterous Grasping — SIM(3)-equivariant pose/shape split + physics refinement cuts penetration 40%
  • CVPRMaskDexGrasp: Generative Masked Modeling for Part-Aware Grasp Synthesis — anatomical part codebooks + masked transformer, editable per finger
  • ROBOQdgset: a Large-Scale Grasping Dataset via Quality-Diversity — quality-diversity search generates diverse grasps at scale
  • CVPRDextER: Language-driven Dexterous Grasp Generation with Embodied Reasoning — contact-token autoregression for interpretable, language-driven grasps

Grasp detection & 6-DoF pose

both
the problem

A robot looking at a bin of objects must decide, in under 100 ms, exactly where and how to place its gripper — a 6-degree-of-freedom prediction (3 position + 3 orientation) from a single depth image. The hard cases are transparent glass, mirror-like metal, and partially hidden objects where the depth sensor gives garbage or nothing.

the approaches

Skip the model: predict grasps — and object poses — straight from vision, handling material, occlusion, and uncertainty. This is the workhorse that turns a depth image into something to grab.

The edges are transparent/specular objects, multi-view fusion, and honest uncertainty.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: one 640×480 RGB-D frame; a wine glass (transparent) sits in a cluttered bin.
  2. Surface reconstruction: NeuGrasp trains a neural signed-distance function on multi-view observations; fills the transparent surface with plausible geometry.
  3. Grasp candidate generation: sample 1,024 grasp poses from the completed surface (GraspSAM variant); each pose is (x,y,z, roll, pitch, yaw).
  4. Scoring: a small MLP scores each by predicted antipodal quality and collision clearance; top-32 kept.
  5. Refinement: gradient-descent on the score field for 20 steps (step size 1 mm / 0.5°) moves each toward a local maximum.
  6. Output: best pose with predicted success probability 0.87; gripper opens 68 mm, approaches from 120° above horizontal.
  • ROBOGraspSAM: When Segment Anything Meets Grasp Detection — adapts SAM with light adapters for promptable grasp detection
  • ROBONeuGrasp: Material-Agnostic Grasp Detection via Neural Surface Reconstruction — neural surfaces recover transparent/specular geometry for grasping
  • CVPRA Cross-view Fusion Framework for Robust 6-DoF Grasp Pose Estimation — cross-view attention fuses multi-view RGB-D with adaptive weighting
  • ROBOvMF-Contact: Uncertainty-Aware Evidential Learning for Contact-Grasp — models aleatoric + epistemic uncertainty for noisy clutter
  • CVPRComPose: Unified Completion-Pose for Category-Level Object Pose — joint shape completion + 6-DoF pose under occlusion

Dexterous & in-hand manipulation

both
the problem

A parallel gripper can clamp; a multi-fingered hand must re-orient an object already held — turning a screwdriver 180° without dropping it. This requires a planned sequence of finger lifts and re-placements (finger gaiting) while the remaining contacts keep the object stable, all coordinated across 20+ joints with tactile feedback.

the approaches

Multi-finger hands reorient objects in-hand by coordinating many joints through making and breaking contact — finger gaiting, regrasp, sliding. Tactile feedback disambiguates which finger touches where.

RL in simulation plus sim-to-real touch has driven the biggest gains here.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: rotate a cylinder 180° in-hand using a 4-finger, 16-DOF hand; initial grip is side-on, target is end-on.
  2. Simulation training (RL): 500 M steps in Isaac Gym; reward = negative angular error to target; AnyRotate-style dense tactile sim at 256 sensor points per finger.
  3. Gait schedule found by RL: finger 0 lifts → rotates 45° → re-plants; repeat for fingers 1, 2, 3 in sequence; full 180° in 4 gaits.
  4. Sim-to-real transfer: tactile randomisation (noise ±8 mN) + domain randomisation (friction 0.6–1.2); policy runs at 50 Hz on real hand.
  5. Real result: 78% success on cylinder, 61% on screwdriver; failure mode is slip during the third gait when mass > 200 g.
  6. Fix: hierarchical policy sequences a 'rotate-30°' primitive 6× rather than tracking continuous angle; raises success to 83%.
  • ROBOViViDex: Vision-Based Dexterous Manipulation from Human Videos — trajectory-guided rewards from human video, vision-only rollouts
  • ROBOAnyRotate: Gravity-Invariant In-Hand Rotation with Sim-to-Real Touch — dense tactile sim-to-real for multi-axis in-hand rotation
  • ROBOFrom Simple to Complex Skills: In-Hand Object Reorientation — hierarchical policy sequences low-level rotation skills
  • CVPRStructural Action Transformer for 3D Dexterous Manipulation — embodied joint codebook + flow matching, cross-embodiment
  • ROBOComposing Dexterous Grasping and In-Hand Manipulation via an RL Critic — reuses an RL critic to score grasps for downstream reorientation

Bimanual & dual-arm coordination

robotics
the problem

When two arms share an object — lifting a beam, folding a shirt — each arm's forces act on the same thing. If the arms are controlled independently, small mismatches in velocity build up internal stress in the shared object, which can tear fabric or bend the beam. Coordinating the pair so they move as one coherent system, not two independent machines, is the core difficulty.

the approaches

Two arms handle what one can’t — folding cloth, carrying a beam, catching — but coupling through the shared object means each arm must account for what the other does.

State-diffusion, keypoints, and active vision coordinate the pair.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: fold a 60×60 cm shirt in half; one arm grabs the left hem, the other the right.
  2. State diffusion (BiFold approach): diffuse a joint 12-D future state (left+right EE positions) from the current cloth point cloud (4,096 points).
  3. Denoising steps: 50 DDPM steps at 20 Hz; each step conditions on the VLM language token 'fold in half toward you'.
  4. Action from state: inverse dynamics (two 6-DOF arm Jacobians) converts the next joint state into torques; both arms receive torques simultaneously.
  5. Result: fold within 3 cm of target crease in 4.2 s; single-arm baseline achieves 38% success vs 79% bimanual.
  6. Failure mode: when the shirt is bunched, the point cloud is too sparse; adding a top-down camera (Active Vision) raises success to 86%.
  • ROBOBiFold: Bimanual Cloth Folding with Language Guidance — a VLM predicts bimanual fold actions from language
  • ROBOLearning Coordinated Bimanual Policies with State Diffusion — diffuses future scene states, then inverse-dynamics to actions
  • ROBOActive Vision for Bimanual Robotic Manipulation — a separate arm carries a camera to beat occlusion
  • ROBOLearning Dexterous Bimanual Catch Skills via Adversarial-Cooperative RL — 2× over single-agent RL on bimanual catching

Contact-rich & force-controlled manipulation

both
the problem

Inserting a USB plug or wiping a table are not position-tracking tasks — they require the robot to regulate the force it presses with, detect when the plug jams, and adjust. Pure position control overshoots into a hard stop and either breaks the part or triggers a safety stop. The robot needs to feel the contact mode (free / stick / slip) and respond in milliseconds.

the approaches

When the task is the contact — insertion, wiping, pressing — the robot must sense and regulate force through sticking, sliding, and jamming, not just track a position.

Force and tactile feedback plus fast contact-mode solvers are the tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: insert a USB-A plug (tolerance ±0.3 mm) into a moving port (10 mm/s lateral drift).
  2. Contact mode detection: 6-axis F/T sensor at 1 kHz; mode = free (|F| < 0.5 N), jammed (Fz > 8 N and δz < 0.1 mm/s), sliding (stick: Fxy < μFz).
  3. Compliance controller (Adaptive Compliance Policy): diffusion policy outputs a 6D stiffness profile; jammed → drop Kz from 800 to 200 N/m, raise Kxy.
  4. Force-aware correction: ForceVLA2 mixture-of-experts adds a Δpose residual when |Fxy| > 2 N; steers plug back to axis.
  5. Outcome: insertion in 1.8 s average; without force control, 12% success; with it, 91% success; max force peaks at 6 N.
  6. Key number: compliance profile must switch within 20 ms of jam detection — beyond 50 ms the plug bends the port tab.
  • CVPRForceVLA2: Hybrid Force-Position Control with Force Awareness — a force-aware mixture-of-experts for contact-rich assembly
  • ROBOAdaptive Compliance Policy: Learning Compliance for Diffusion Control — learns spatiotemporal compliance profiles from demos
  • ROBOTactile-Driven Non-Prehensile Manipulation via Extrinsic Contact Mode Control — switches stick/slide via gradient optimization on tactile
  • CVPRContact-Aware Neural Dynamics — tactile co-training implicitly aligns sim and real contact

Deformable-object manipulation

robotics
the problem

A rigid object has 6 degrees of freedom; a shirt has effectively infinite. When a robot grabs a corner and pulls, the rest of the cloth moves in ways that depend on weave density, moisture, and folding history — none of which a camera can directly see. Predicting the shape of the cloth after an action, well enough to plan the next action, requires either a very good physical model or a lot of data.

the approaches

Cloth, rope, cables, and dough have effectively infinite degrees of freedom and change shape as you act — so the robot must predict how the object deforms under its moves.

Point-cloud dynamics, keypoints, and differentiable simulation model the state.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: flatten a bunched shirt on a table from a random crumpled start; 3 actions allowed.
  2. State representation: point cloud of 2,048 points from an RGBD overhead camera; projected to a 64×64 height map.
  3. Dynamics model (DeformPAM): a graph neural network predicts the next height map given a pick–place action (pick_x, pick_y, place_x, place_y); trained on 40,000 random-action rollouts.
  4. Action selection: CEM (cross-entropy method) optimises over 256 action candidates for 5 iterations; cost = area of height > 5 mm (bumpiness).
  5. Result after 3 actions: 74% of shirt area within 5 mm of flat; human baseline 91%; naive random policy 22%.
  6. Why 3 not converge to human: the GNN accumulates prediction error; by action 3, predicted vs real height map differs by 8 mm RMS.
  • ROBODeformPAM: Data-Efficient Long-Horizon Deformable Manipulation via Preference Alignment — diffusion over 3D point clouds + preference-based training
  • ROBOGeneral-Purpose Clothes Manipulation with Semantic Keypoints — sparse semantic keypoints unify fold/flatten/hang
  • ROBOLearning Object Properties via Differentiable Robot-Object Interaction — differentiable sim identifies elasticity/mass from proprioception
  • ROBOShared Control for Cable Routing with Tactile Sensing — tactile-driven authority sharing for contact-rich cable routing

Non-prehensile manipulation

robotics
the problem

Sometimes the fastest path to moving an object is to push it rather than grasp it — slide a book across a table, topple a block, pivot a box around a corner. But a pushed object can slip sideways, rotate unexpectedly, or jam against a wall. Planning a push sequence that reliably lands the object in the target pose requires modelling friction, the pusher's contact geometry, and often the obstacle layout.

the approaches

You don’t always grasp — sometimes you push, pivot, topple, or slide. Non-prehensile manipulation rearranges objects through external contact, planning contact sequences that respect friction.

MPPI and contact-mode control handle the uncertainty and mode switches.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: move a 0.4 kg box from pose A to pose B 40 cm away, around a wall obstacle; only a flat pusher arm is available (no grasp).
  2. Contact model: quasi-static limit surface (ellipsoidal approximation); friction μ=0.35 between box and table.
  3. Push graph (ReloPush style): discretise the table into 4 cm cells; build a traversability graph where each edge is a feasible straight push; A* on this graph finds a 3-push path.
  4. Push 1: push 18 cm north; predicted final pose error ±1.2 cm (model uncertainty).
  5. MPPI correction (Goal-Driven Pushing): 512-sample MPPI with the limit-surface model optimises push direction online; corrects 0.8 cm lateral drift after push 1.
  6. Result: box lands within 2.1 cm of target in 3 pushes; grasping the same box from the start position takes 4.7 s more due to approach planning.
  • ROBOReloPush: Multi-Object Rearrangement with a Nonholonomic Pusher — a push-traversability graph plans confined-space rearrangement
  • ROBODynamic Object-Goal Pushing with Mobile Manipulators via Constrained RL — constrained RL discovers push contacts reactively
  • ROBOGoal-Driven Robotic Pushing Under Uncertain Object Properties — MPPI handles uncertain mass/friction without preset trajectories
  • ROBODynamic On-Palm Manipulation via Controlled Sliding — contact-implicit MPC slides objects on the palm

Tool use

robotics
the problem

Grasping a tool is not enough — the robot must understand that the tool is an intermediary: the force the tool applies to the world depends on how the robot holds it, how the tool flexes, and how the tool contacts the work surface. A spatula that is gripped too hard bends; a peeler that is too fast tears the vegetable. The robot must perceive and control forces through the tool, not at its own fingers.

the approaches

Grasp a tool, then apply forces through it — a peeler, a spatula, a rope — adapting to the tool’s geometry and the contact it makes with the world.

Contact-based demos, hierarchical RL, and visual prompting teach tool skills.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: peel a cucumber with a Y-peeler (Robotic Hand Tool Use paper).
  2. Demonstration capture: a human peels 5 cucumbers; an optical tactile sensor (GelSight-style) on the tool tip records 3D contact map + normal force at 30 Hz.
  3. What gets copied: NOT the trajectory, but the contact sequence — approach angle 35°, contact force 1.2 N ± 0.3 N, stroke length 80 mm, peel-lift at end.
  4. Stiffness identification: a quick 3-push calibration estimates the peeler's effective stiffness at the tip (1,400 N/m); the controller uses this to convert Δforce to Δangle.
  5. Execution: a 6-DOF arm with force-torque wrist maintains 1.2 N contact force via admittance control; 12 strokes complete the peel in 28 s.
  6. Failure mode: if the cucumber is bumpy, the contact force spikes to 3.5 N and the peeler skips; fix is to slow to 30 mm/s when Fz variance > 0.4 N².
  • ROBORobotic Hand Tool Use with Contact-Based Demonstration: Cucumber Peeling — optical tactile captures tool stiffness, reproduces contact not trajectory
  • ROBOHierarchical RL for Articulated Tool Manipulation with a Multifingered Hand — a tool-affordance encoder conditions a hierarchical policy
  • ROBOImplicit Physics-aware Policy for Dynamic Manipulation via Soft Body Tools — online system-ID for manipulating with rope-like tools
  • ROBOMOKA: Open-World Manipulation through Mark-Based Visual Prompting — turns affordance reasoning into VLM question-answering for tool use

Pick-and-place & rearrangement

robotics
the problem

Pick-and-place sounds simple, but when there are 20 objects to move to specific goal poses, the order matters enormously. Picking the wrong object first can block the goal pose of a later one, forcing backtracking or re-grasps. Planning an efficient sequence — minimising travel, avoiding collisions, and unblocking the right objects first — is an NP-hard combinatorial problem that gets harder as the scene grows.

the approaches

The bread and butter: grasp, move, place — sequenced over many objects to minimize travel and avoid collisions. Simple to state, subtle to optimize at scale.

Lazy search, registration-as-policy, and 3D language policies push generalization.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scene: 12 objects in a bin; 6 have assigned target poses on a shelf; 6 are blockers with no assigned goal.
  2. Obstruction graph: compute which objects physically block which target poses (ray-cast from each target); build a directed dependency graph.
  3. ORLA* planning: lazy A* on the rearrangement graph with a learned heuristic (MLP trained on 50 k random scenes); open only the most promising nodes.
  4. Sequence found: move 3 blockers first (no assigned targets), then place the 6 goal objects in dependency order; 15 picks total.
  5. Execution time: 4.8 min for 15 picks; naive nearest-neighbour baseline needs 18 picks (3 backtrack re-grasps); ORLA* is 25% faster.
  6. Registration policy (Match Policy): rather than re-planning, cast action inference as aligning the current point cloud to a stored demo; runs in 80 ms per pick.
  • ROBOORLA*: Mobile-Manipulator Rearrangement with Lazy A* — lazy A* + learned heuristics for time-optimal rearrangement
  • ROBOMatch Policy: From Point-Cloud Registration to Manipulation — recasts action inference as registering target poses to demos
  • ROBOTowards Generalizable Vision-Language Manipulation: GemBench + 3D-LOTUS — scene-conditioned 3D policy generalizes across objects/poses
  • ROBOHook-Based Aerial Payload Grasping from a Moving Platform — trajectory optimization times an aerial pick-up

Affordance & task-oriented grasping

both
the problem

A knife affords two grasps: blade for handing to someone safely, handle for cutting. A force-closure-optimal grasp on the blade is mechanically good but task-wrong. The robot must reason from the task goal to the part of the object to grab, and from that part to the actual contact points — a chain from language semantics to geometry to contact physics that classical grasp planners skip entirely.

the approaches

The right grasp depends on the goal: hold a knife by the handle to cut, by the blade to hand over. Affordance grasping reasons about object parts and task to pick where and how to grip.

VLM in-context reasoning and part segmentation ground affordances by language.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Instruction: 'hand the scissors to the person' (implies: grasp the blade half, not the handle).
  2. VLM affordance reasoning (AffordGrasp): GPT-4V in-context prompt: 'Which part of scissors should I grip to hand safely?' → 'the blade, away from the cutting edge'.
  3. Part grounding: OWL-ViT open-vocabulary segmentation finds the blade region in the current RGB frame; outputs a 2D mask (84% IoU with human label).
  4. Grasp selection: GraspNet samples 256 grasps; filter to those whose contact points fall 90% within the blade mask; 18 pass.
  5. Final pick: highest-quality remaining grasp; gripper opens 52 mm, approaches from 70° to avoid the cutting edge.
  6. Task-aware result: 88% of grasps land on the correct part; without affordance reasoning, 43% grab the handle (wrong for safe handover).
  • ROBOAffordGrasp: In-Context Affordance Reasoning for Open-Vocabulary Grasping — a VLM infers the task and grounds part-level affordances
  • ROBORT-Affordance: Affordances as Intermediate Representations — predicts affordance keypoint plans from language, web-scale
  • CVPRAffordGen: Diverse Demonstrations via Affordance Correspondence — DINOv2 correspondences transfer grasps across novel objects
  • ROBOSemSegGrasp: Task-Oriented Grasping via Semantic Segmentation — decouples semantic part analysis from grasp detection

Cluttered-scene & amodal manipulation

robotics
the problem

In a real bin, objects stack on top of each other: you can see the top of a cup but its base is hidden behind a bottle. A robot that plans to grasp the cup may collide with the bottle it cannot see. Amodal reasoning — estimating the complete shape of an object from a partial view — and reasoning about which objects physically block which grasps are both required before the first pick.

the approaches

Real bins are messy: objects occlude each other and only part of each is visible. The robot must segment instances, complete hidden shape, and reason about what blocks what.

Boundary-refined segmentation, shape-completion confidence, and obstruction graphs help.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scene: a bin with 8 objects; camera sees 3 fully, 5 partially; target is a small spray bottle at the bottom.
  2. Segmentation (Boundary Refinement paper): Mask R-CNN with boundary-error refinement produces per-instance masks; estimates 4 boundary-error types (false-positive/negative on each side).
  3. Shape completion: a point-cloud completion network (ShapeFormer) completes each partial object; outputs per-point uncertainty (std of 10 samples) → high uncertainty → avoid those grasps.
  4. Obstruction graph: for each object, ray-cast the completed shape to identify which other objects it rests on; spray bottle is blocked by 2 objects.
  5. Clear-first sequence: remove the 2 blocking objects (easier grasps) first; then grasp spray bottle.
  6. Result: 82% task-success vs 54% without amodal reasoning; the 18% failures come from under-segmented instances where two objects are merged.
  • ROBOHigh-Quality Unknown Object Instance Segmentation via Boundary Refinement — estimates four boundary error types for robust clutter segmentation
  • ROBOMeasuring Uncertainty in Shape Completion to Improve Grasp Quality — per-point completion confidence steers grasp choice
  • CVPRObstruction Reasoning for Robotic Grasping — a graph identifies obstructing objects to clear first
  • ROBOAdaptive Grasping of Moving Objects in Dense Clutter — global-to-local detection + static-to-dynamic planning

Manipulation policies & imitation learning

both
the problem

Learning the whole pick-to-place skill from 50 human demonstrations sounds like enough, but the actions at each timestep are not unimodal: there are five equally good ways to approach a mug, and a network that averages them outputs a path down the middle that grabs nothing. Capturing the full distribution of valid actions and committing to one clean choice — without collapsing the modes — is the central statistical problem of imitation learning for manipulation.

the approaches

Learn the whole visuomotor policy from demonstrations — observations to continuous actions — handling the multimodality of valid actions and generalizing across tasks and embodiments.

Diffusion transformers, recovery behaviors, and model merging are the current tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Dataset: 50 human tele-operation demos of picking a mug and placing it on a coaster; 30 Hz, 7-DOF arm, wrist RGB.
  2. Architecture (Diffusion Transformer, DiT-B): action chunk length = 16 steps; diffusion with 100 DDPM noise levels; conditioning = last 2 RGB frames + robot proprioception.
  3. Training: 200 k gradient steps, batch 256, lr 1e-4; loss = MSE on the denoised action chunk.
  4. Inference: 100-step DDPM denoising at 10 Hz (real-time at 16-step chunk); picks one of 3 approach modes deterministically from the noise seed.
  5. Result: 84% success on seen mugs, 61% on novel objects; REACH recovery policy kicks in on OOD detections (cosine distance > 0.7 from training distribution).
  6. Model merging (MergeVLA): merge this mug policy with a trained 'pour water' policy via task-mask LoRA; combined policy handles both tasks without catastrophic forgetting.

  • CVPRREACH: Explicit Recovery Behavior for Diffusion Policies — OOD detection + retreat for robust action selection
  • CVPRMergeVLA: Cross-Skill Model Merging Toward a Generalist VLA — task-mask LoRA merges skills without forgetting
  • ROBORoCoDA: Counterfactual Data Augmentation for Robot Learning — causal invariance + SE(3) equivariance augments demos

Articulated-object manipulation

vision
the problem

A door, a drawer, and scissors all have moving parts, but the motion is constrained — a hinge, a prismatic joint, a pivot. A robot that does not know the joint type and axis will try to pull the door straight out and strip the hinge. Inferring the articulation model (joint type + axis + range of motion) from a single RGB image, then planning motion along that inferred joint, is the core problem.

the approaches

Doors, drawers, laptops, scissors — objects with moving parts. The robot must infer the joint (hinge or slider) and manipulate the part along it, ideally from a single view.

VLM knowledge, analytic part concepts, and Gaussian part models reconstruct the kinematics.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: one RGB frame of a closed laptop; no depth, no CAD model.
  2. VLM knowledge (SPARK): GPT-4V asked 'what is the joint type of a laptop lid?' → 'revolute, axis along the hinge, range 0°–135°'.
  3. Part segmentation: Grounded-SAM segments 'screen' and 'base' masks; 2D correspondence lifted to 3D via a monocular depth estimate.
  4. Articulation fitting: differentiable forward kinematics fits a revolute joint (axis + pivot) to the 3D part masks; 50-step Adam optimisation, final axis error 3.2°.
  5. URDF generation (SPARK output): a minimal URDF with two links and the fitted joint; used directly in MuJoCo for motion planning.
  6. Execution: a 6-DOF arm follows a screw-motion path along the inferred axis; opens the laptop to 90° in 2.1 s; success rate 74% (failure: depth error > 15 mm on glossy surfaces).

  • CVPRSPARK: Sim-ready Part-level Articulated Reconstruction with VLM Knowledge — differentiable forward kinematics + URDF refinement from one image
  • CVPRPart2GS: Part-aware Modeling of Articulated Objects with 3D Gaussians — part-level Gaussian decomposition for articulation
  • CVPRHoi!: A Multimodal Dataset for Force-Grounded Articulated Manipulation — 3048 sequences with synced RGB-D, pose, force, haptic

Hand-object interaction: reconstruction & synthesis

vision
the problem

To teach a robot from video, you need to recover how the human hand was gripping the object — its 3D pose and all 21 joint angles — from a monocular camera that sees only a 2D projection, with the hand frequently hidden behind the object. The reconstruction must also be physically plausible: fingers must not float in the air or penetrate the object surface.

the approaches

Recover, or generate, how a hand grips and moves an object — from monocular video, under occlusion, obeying physics (no floating, no penetration). This is the perception side that teaches robots how humans manipulate.

Foundation models, discrete hand tokens, and physics-aware diffusion drive it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 30-second monocular video of a person picking up and turning a mug; hand occluded ~40% of frames.
  2. Stage 1 — initial estimates: HaMeR hand-pose model + FoundationPose object-pose model run per-frame; gives noisy (x,y,z, 21 joints) estimates with floating fingers in 30% of frames.
  3. Stage 2 — contact reasoning (ArtHOI): MLLM prompted with rendered hand+object image: 'Is the index fingertip in contact with the mug handle?' → binary contact labels per finger per frame.
  4. Stage 3 — physics refinement: RL agent (PAD-Hand style) minimises: penetration depth + contact gap + joint-velocity smoothness, 200 optimisation steps per second of video.
  5. Stage 4 — mesh output: TokenHand VQ-VAE decodes the refined joint angles to a smooth mesh at 65 FPS; outputs MANO parameters for downstream retargeting.
  6. Result: mean per-joint error 8.3 mm (vs 14.1 mm baseline); zero frames with finger penetration > 2 mm after refinement.
  • CVPRArtHOI: Monocular 4D Reconstruction of Hand-Articulated-Object Interaction — multi-foundation-model + MLLM contact reasoning for 4D HOI
  • CVPRTokenHand: Discrete Tokens for Efficient Hand Mesh Reconstruction — VQ-VAE hand codebook, 65 FPS at SOTA accuracy
  • CVPRPAD-Hand: Physics-Aware Diffusion for Hand Motion Recovery — probabilistic Euler-Lagrange dynamics with variance maps
  • CVPRRecovering Physically Plausible Human-Object Interactions from Video — RL frame selection + physics simulation refine noisy HOI

Sim-to-real for manipulation

both
the problem

A policy trained in simulation for 10 billion steps can fail immediately on the real robot — not because the task is too hard, but because the real table has slightly different friction, the camera has a 5 ms delay, and the joint encoders add 0.5° noise. These small mismatches compound across a manipulation sequence and cause the policy to drift off course within the first 3 seconds. Closing this gap without per-task real-world fine-tuning is the open problem.

the approaches

Manipulation policies are mostly trained in simulation, where data is cheap and failures are free — then they must survive real friction, sensor noise, and delay. Closing that last gap is its own discipline.

Teacher-student distillation, delta actions, and tactile co-training bridge it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Policy: a pixel-to-action diffusion policy for door-opening, trained 100 M steps in IsaacGym.
  2. Sim domain randomisation: friction μ ∈ [0.3, 1.2], door-handle diameter ±2 mm, lighting ±40% intensity, camera pose ±1 cm / ±2°, sensor delay ∈ [0, 20 ms].
  3. Teacher-student transfer (VIRAL style): teacher has privileged state access (perfect pose); student sees only pixel observations; student learns delta-action residuals on top of teacher's actions.
  4. Stage-reset exploration: initialise rollouts from 10 stages of door-opening (GRPO method); each stage trains the policy on the hardest 20% of resets.
  5. Real evaluation: 83% success on the real door after zero real-robot fine-tuning (vs 41% without domain randomisation); mean episode time 4.6 s.
  6. Remaining gap: success drops to 61% on a door with a lever handle (never seen in sim); few-shot real adaptation (5 demos) recovers to 79%.
  • CVPROpening the Sim-to-Real Door for Humanoid Pixel-to-Action Transfer — stage-reset exploration + GRPO, 83% real door-opening
  • CVPRVIRAL: Visual Sim-to-Real at Scale for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation — teacher-student with delta actions, 91.5% real cycles
  • ROBODemoStart: Auto-Curriculum for Sim-to-Real with Multi-Fingered Robots — demo-initialized auto-curriculum RL for dexterous sim-to-real
  • ROBOLearning Dual-Arm Coordination for Grasping Large Flat Objects — zero-shot transferable dual-arm RL, sim-to-real validated

Part IV

Where it stands

Manipulating the real world as fluently as a person is far from solved. What is genuinely still hard:

Contact you can’t differentiate through. The physics jumps between stick, slip, and separation; smooth optimizers stall at the switches, and learning contact-rich skills stays sample-hungry.
Generalizing to every object. Even with foundation-model priors, grasping and manipulation still lean on large pre-training; true one-shot skill on arbitrary objects is unsolved.
The last 10% of sim-to-real. Policies plateau at 80–90% real success; unmodeled friction, delay, and calibration eat the rest, and closing it without per-task tuning is open.
Deformables at scale. Cloth, rope, and fluids have unbounded state and defeat generic models — each still needs a good deal of per-object engineering.
Grasp meets long-horizon reasoning. Choosing a grasp that serves the next step — and clearing what obstructs it — needs scene reasoning most systems don’t yet have.
Tactile that transfers. Touch sensors vary wildly and the tactile sim-to-real gap is larger than vision’s; policies rarely move between sensor types without retraining.
