Making training examples cheaper

Teaching robots by example is slow and costly. Four ways the field cuts that cost: better recording rigs, squeezing more from few demos, faking data in simulation, and refining a policy after the demos run out. The big picture — connecting the dots

The core fact is simple: teaching a robot by watching a person is extraordinarily expensive. Every robot that learns by imitation needs hundreds or thousands of human demonstrations, and collecting each one costs time, wages, equipment, and the risk of repetitive strain on the person demonstrating. The single bet running through almost every paper here is that you can move the cost somewhere cheaper than human time — move it to smarter recording rigs that give better data per human session, move it to algorithms that squeeze more learning from fewer examples, move it to computers in simulators that generate examples for free, or move it to the robot itself by letting it refine what it learned without asking for more human demos. The field is not trying to eliminate the cost entirely; it is just trying to move it away from the expensive part (expert humans repeating motions) to the parts you can automate or parallelize.

Four ways people are actually relocating the cost:

  • ① Better recording rigs. ARCap puts an augmented reality headset on the demonstrator and shows them live feedback about whether their motion is good enough — they fix bad takes instantly instead of re-recording later. How to Train Your Robots? systematically compares kinesthetic teaching, VR controllers, and spacemouse input to figure out which method produces data worth paying for.
  • ② Making one demonstration do more work. SPOT records the motion of the target object in 3D space rather than the robot's joint angles, so the same recording teaches any robot body — you can even learn from bare-hand videos. Human-Agent Joint Learning puts a trained helper agent in control with the human, gradually taking over more of the work so each hour of human time produces more training signal.
  • ③ Generating unlimited fake examples in simulation. Stonefish and OceanSim build realistic simulators for underwater robots, which are expensive to collect real examples for, so every robot learns mostly on simulated data. Domain Randomization for Object Detection in Manufacturing generates synthetic training images by varying appearance, lighting, and angle, then tests what random variations matter most.
  • ④ Letting the robot improve itself after demos end. FLaRe takes an initial policy learned from demonstrations and continues training it with trial-and-error learning driven by a sparse reward signal — just whether the task succeeded, with no detailed human guidance. ExploRLLM uses a large language model to suggest a good initial exploration strategy, so the robot's self-improvement phase learns faster.

The throughline: demonstration data is expensive, so every paper here pushes a different cost lever — recording quality, multi-robot reuse, simulation scale, or robot-driven refinement — to get a working policy without demanding unlimited human time. The 2025 work is testing which levers actually move far enough to matter in the real world, and whether any single one (or combination of them) can scale from tabletop tasks to the full range of things a general-purpose robot needs to do. Still open: whether object-centric demonstrations (like SPOT's recordings of object motion) can actually replace hand-tuning for radically different robot morphologies; whether simulators built automatically from real environments have enough fidelity to train robust policies without fine-tuning; and whether trial-and-error refinement (FLaRe, ExploRLLM) can converge reliably on real hardware in reasonable time without constant human supervision.

The problem — in depth

Every robot that learns to do something by watching people needs a large set of recorded examples. A person physically guides the robot through the task, or the person does the task themselves while sensors record every movement. Then those recordings are fed to a learning algorithm that builds a policy — a set of rules for what to do in each situation.

The core trouble is that this process is slow, expensive, and does not scale. A single arm task, like picking up a cup and placing it on a shelf, might need hundreds of good recordings before a robot can do it reliably. Getting those recordings means finding willing people, setting up equipment, running the session, checking the recordings, and throwing away the ones that are noisy or wrong. Scale that to dozens of tasks and multiple robot bodies, and the cost becomes the main thing blocking the whole field from moving forward.

Why is collecting examples so hard? Three reasons compound each other.

First, robots are clumsy to operate. Moving a robot arm by hand, or through a controller, is tiring and unintuitive. The person giving the demonstration does not move the way they would move their own hand, so the motion they produce is often jerky or imprecise. A learning algorithm trained on bad motion data learns bad habits.

Second, the person giving the demonstration has no feedback while they are doing it. They cannot see whether their movement was clean enough, or whether the sensor data it produced will actually be useful for training. They find out the data was bad only after the session ends and someone checks the files. That means repeating the whole session.

Third, examples collected from one robot body usually cannot directly teach a different robot body, and examples from a person's bare hands usually cannot teach a robot at all without extra conversion steps. So even if you have thousands of examples, you may not be able to reuse them across the robots you have.

This matters because it connects directly to the one thing every robot must do: take in a world it does not fully understand, decide what to do, and move a physical body to carry that action out — all quickly, cheaply, and without failing. The example-collection bottleneck slows all three parts. Without enough examples, the robot does not learn to sense the world well. It does not learn to make good decisions. And it does not learn the precise physical motions that actually work. Every other advance in robot learning — better algorithms, bigger models, faster computers — hits a ceiling if there are not enough good examples to learn from.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Better rigs for recording demonstrations

One approach is to make the act of recording a demonstration easier and higher quality — better tools so each example a person gives is more likely to be usable.

ARCap takes this direction directly: an augmented reality headset shows the person live visual signals while they are demonstrating. The signals tell them in the moment whether their motion is clean enough for training. This means the person can adjust on the fly instead of discovering the recording was bad afterward. The result is fewer wasted sessions and cleaner data from each one.

The paper "How to Train Your Robots?" takes a more analytical approach: the researchers recorded the same set of tasks using three different methods — moving the arm by hand, using a VR controller, and using a joystick — then trained and tested robot policies from each set. Understanding which method produces data that leads to better learning is itself a way to stop wasting effort on the wrong recording approach.

Diff-DAgger (from "Diff-Dagger: Uncertainty Estimation With Diffusion Policy for Robotic Manipulation") attacks a related problem: when to ask a human to step in and give more examples during live operation. Most systems ask too often, which is tiring, or too rarely, which means the robot stays stuck. Diff-DAgger measures how uncertain the robot's own internal process is at each moment and calls for a human only when that uncertainty is genuinely high. This means human effort is spent only where it is most needed.

2. Making one demonstration go further

A second approach keeps the number of demonstrations small and instead finds ways to squeeze more learning out of each one.

SPOT ("SE(3) Pose Trajectory Diffusion for Object-Centric Manipulation") makes demonstrations more portable. Instead of recording what the robot's joints did, it records how the target object moved in 3D space. That description of the task is body-independent — it can teach a different robot, or even learn from videos of a person's bare hands, with as few as eight demonstrations. This dramatically widens the pool of possible recordings.

The Human-Agent Joint Learning paper takes a related approach during collection itself. A trained helper agent shares control of the robot with the human operator, gradually taking over more of the work as it improves. The operator does less and less over time while still producing useful demonstrations. Each hour of human effort produces more training signal than it would if the person were working alone.

"From Imitation to Refinement" addresses what happens when the examples run out and the policy still is not good enough. Instead of collecting more demonstrations, it freezes the policy learned from the existing demonstrations and trains a second, lighter policy that watches the robot in action and makes small corrections. The corrections are learned through trial and error, not more human demonstrations. This extends the value of the original data.

3. Generating fake training data in simulation

A third direction bypasses real-world collection entirely: build a computer simulation that is realistic enough to generate useful training examples automatically, at almost no cost per example.

The Stonefish simulator and OceanSim both do this for underwater robots, a domain where real collection is especially expensive. Stonefish adds realistic sensor models — including cameras that report only changes rather than full frames — and automatic labeling of every simulated frame. OceanSim runs on graphics cards and models how underwater cameras and sonar physically work, creating realistic sensor data without ever getting a real robot wet.

"Domain Randomization for Object Detection in Manufacturing Applications" applies the same idea to factory robots. It generates synthetic training images by randomly varying material appearance, lighting, camera angle, and background — a technique called domain randomization. A detector trained only on these generated images then works on real factory scenes. The paper systematically tests which random variations matter most, giving future researchers a guide for building their own synthetic datasets.

"Learning to Swim" and AnyCar both use simulation to train control policies that transfer to real robots. The underwater vehicle paper trains in a fast parallel physics simulator with randomly varied conditions, then deploys the policy directly on a real vehicle with no extra tuning. AnyCar trains a single neural network on many different virtual vehicles with different physics, then fine-tunes it to a real vehicle using only a small number of real examples.

4. Refining policies after the examples run out

A fourth approach trains an initial policy from whatever demonstrations exist, then improves it further through trial and error — so the robot keeps getting better without needing more human recordings.

FLaRe takes a general robot policy trained by copying demonstrations and continues training it using reinforcement learning (trial-and-error learning) driven by sparse signals — just a signal for whether the task succeeded or not, with no detailed instructions. This extends the value of the original demonstrations by letting the robot practice on its own.

ExploRLLM uses a large language model to write an initial control program and simplify what the robot senses, giving the trial-and-error learner a far better starting point than random exploration. The result learns faster and transfers to a real robot without extra training.

Where it stands

The field has made real progress on all four fronts: better recording tools reduce the cost of each human session, portability tricks like object-centric representations let one demonstration teach more than one robot, simulation closes gaps in domains where real collection is dangerous or slow, and trial-and-error fine-tuning extends the life of the examples that do exist. What is still open is whether any of these approaches can scale to the full range of tasks a general robot needs — most systems are still tested on a narrow set of table-top tasks, and none have shown that a single example-collection strategy covers everything from precise assembly to outdoor navigation to helping people eat. The combination of cheaper recording and cheaper simulation is narrowing the gap, but the gap remains large.

ARCap: Collecting High-Quality Human Demonstrations for Robot Learning with Augmented Reality Feedback

ICRA 2025 · 7 citations · strategy: better recording rig

What it does

ARCap is a wearable data-collection rig that gives a human demonstrator live augmented-reality feedback — a virtual robot superimposed on the real scene — while they perform a manipulation task, so the data they record is kinematically valid and collision-free before it ever reaches the training pipeline. The strategy is better recording rig: the insight is that the cost of fixing bad demonstrations (re-recording, filtering, extra data) is higher than the cost of preventing them in real time. Compared to the closest prior system (DexCap, which has no live feedback), ARCap-trained policies achieve 70% vs 25% success on cluttered pick-and-place, and novice-collected data goes from 0% to 60% success.

The method, in detail

Inputs at collection time. A person wears a Meta Quest 3 headset, a Rokoko motion-capture glove on each hand, and a mounted Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera on their wrist. The Quest 3 controllers are fixed to the glove mounts so wrist 6-DOF pose is tracked. The RealSense captures colored point clouds of the scene. A laptop in a backpack runs the Python server.

Kinematic retargeting (the core loop). At each timestep the server reads wrist pose from the Quest controllers and finger joint angles from the Rokoko gloves, then runs an inverse-kinematics (IK) solver to find joint angles for the target robot (e.g. LEAP dexterous hand, or a parallel-jaw gripper). For dexterous hands the IK matches human fingertips to robot fingertip positions; a null-space IK term simultaneously keeps the robot out of self-collision. For parallel-jaw grippers, the aperture is derived from the thumb–index separation distance, mapped to the gripper's 1 Hz open/close speed limit. The resulting virtual robot model is rendered in the AR view, exactly overlaid on the real scene.

Three real-time quality signals.

  • *Scene visibility frame*: a rectangle in the AR display shows the robot's RGB-D camera field of view. If the demonstrator's hand drifts out of it, they see the frame go red — so objects stay in the robot's perceptual range.
  • *Speed warning*: if the human moves faster than the robot can actuate (joint speed limit exceeded), a yellow frame appears and the virtual robot visibly lags behind the hand, giving intuitive feedback to slow down.
  • *Collision warning*: the Quest 3's built-in scene-reconstruction mesh is used for real-time collision checking between the virtual robot body and the pre-scanned environment. A blue blinking frame + controller haptic vibration alert the user before a collision is captured as data.

Data pipeline. Recorded streams: colored point cloud from RealSense, virtual robot joint angles and end-effector poses, headset world pose, and glove finger data — all time-stamped and stored as .npz files per trajectory. Post-processing (convertdatawith_robot.py) applies point-cloud cropping, superimposes the virtual robot's own point cloud to reduce the visual domain gap at training time, and outputs files in robomimic-compatible HDF5 format.

Policy training. A diffusion policy with a PointNet encoder processes the cropped point cloud; the observation is the PointNet embedding concatenated with current joint angles. Action chunks are predicted. No large language model or external API is involved. The specific data-cost saving: because feedback prevents bad demonstrations at collection time, fewer total demos are needed — novice users produce usable data rather than requiring expert re-collection.

How to Train Your Robots? The Impact of Demonstration Modality on Imitation Learning

ICRA 2025 · 4 citations · strategy: squeeze more from few demos (mixed-modality reuse)

What it does

Collecting robot demonstrations is expensive partly because we do not know which recording method — physically guiding the robot by hand (kinesthetic teaching), using a VR headset controller, or using a 6-axis mouse called a spacemouse — produces the best downstream learning. This paper runs a controlled empirical study across all three methods on three tabletop tasks and measures both learning performance and human effort. The central punchline is that a small number of kinesthetic demos mixed into a larger teleoperation dataset outperforms using any single modality at the same total demo count, letting you get more policy quality from fewer total recordings.

Strategy: squeeze more from few demos (mixed-modality data reuse — the insight is that kinesthetic traces are cleaner but fatiguing, so you use a handful of them to anchor the dataset rather than collecting hundreds).

The method, in detail

Inputs. Each demonstration is a time-series of end-effector Cartesian poses and a binary gripper signal, recorded on a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda arm. Two RGB cameras (one wrist-mounted, one external) feed into the policy. The three collection modes differ in how a human produces those poses:

  • *Kinesthetic teaching* — the robot is put in gravity-compensated mode; the operator physically moves the arm and releases it, recording joint positions at each step.
  • *VR teleoperation* — an Oculus Quest controller tracks the operator's hand pose via IMU; the controller's 6-DoF motion is mapped to end-effector delta pose commands sent in real-time over a Cartesian impedance controller (Polymetis).
  • *Spacemouse teleoperation* — a 6-axis desk mouse outputs velocity commands along three translational and three rotational axes, scaled to end-effector delta poses.

Policy architecture. The authors use a Diffusion Policy (Chi et al., RSS 2023): a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) that takes stacked RGB frames encoded by a ResNet plus the robot's proprioceptive state, and outputs a chunk of 16 consecutive actions, of which 8 are executed before the next inference step. This action-chunking trick reduces inference calls but creates open-loop execution between steps. Training runs for 800 K gradient steps with MSE loss on denoised noise predictions.

Data quality measurement. The key analytic tool is K-nearest-neighbor (KNN) variance in proprioceptive space: for each timestep, find the K most similar robot states across all demonstrations and measure how spread the corresponding actions are. Low variance means the demos agree on what to do in each state — "cleaner" data for imitation learning. They also measure jerkiness (second derivative of actions) and state-space diversity. Kinesthetic demos consistently show lower action variance and lower jerkiness than both teleoperation modes, except for high-contact tasks where physical limits of kinesthetic teaching cause drop-outs.

The specific trick that saves data. The mixed-modality scheme: collect a *small* kinesthetic subset (e.g. 20–30 demos) — enough to capture smooth, consistent trajectories without operator fatigue — then blend it with a *larger* VR or spacemouse dataset. The blended set trains a single diffusion policy without any reweighting or separate heads. On the Flip Glass task, 100 mixed demos (20 kinesthetic + 80 VR) reach 75% success vs. 70% for 100 kinesthetic-only, while the kinesthetic-only condition requires far more physical effort per demo. The insight is that kinesthetic data anchors the action distribution in contact-free phases, while teleoperation data provides coverage at scale.

Tasks. Three real-robot tabletop tasks: *Open Drawer* (free-space reach, alignment, constrained linear pull), *Flip Glass* (pick, 180-degree rotation, place), *Push Sanitizer* (contact-force pressing). Each modality contributed 100 expert trajectories. A user study with 12 participants (6 expert, 6 novice) compared 5 demos per person per modality using NASA-TLX subjective load and practice-time-to-confidence metrics.

Reviewing it yourself

What to read first. Start with the arXiv HTML version (arxiv.org/html/2503.07017v1), Section III (Experimental Setup) and Section IV (Results). The paper is 8 pages and quite readable — it is primarily empirical, so the methods are described in prose rather than algorithm boxes. Then read Section II for the data quality metrics (KNN variance, jerkiness formulas).

How hard is the code to follow. The Diffusion Policy codebase (the effective implementation base) is moderately complex — it uses Hydra for all config management and PyTorch Lightning conventions. The core policy logic in diffusion_policy.py is clean and well-commented. The robot interface (Polymetis) is the steeper learning curve if you do not have Franka experience. Estimated reading time to understand the policy: 2–3 hours. Reproducing the data collection pipeline without the paper's own scripts: 1–2 days.

Run verdict

CPU-only box: cannot meaningfully run. Training 800 K steps of a DDPM with ResNet encoders on image sequences requires a GPU (the original work likely used a single A100 or similar). A forward pass for inference is technically possible on CPU but runs at ~1–2 Hz — far below the 20 Hz required for real-time robot control. Additionally, the experiment requires a physical Franka Emika Panda arm, Polymetis server, and either an Oculus Quest or spacemouse for data collection. There is no simulation fallback and no pre-collected dataset released publicly. A GPU workstation with Linux + Polymetis + Franka FCI license is the minimum viable setup.

Diff-DAgger: Uncertainty Estimation with Diffusion Policy for Robotic Manipulation

ICRA 2025 · 8 citations · strategy: refine the policy after demos run out (interactive DAgger loop that solicits expert corrections during live rollout)

What it does

Diff-DAgger is an interactive imitation learning system that lets a diffusion policy ask for expert help exactly when it is genuinely confused, rather than whenever its internal action proposals disagree. A standard diffusion policy is trained on a small seed dataset; during robot rollout the system monitors the policy's own denoising reconstruction error (the training loss evaluated on the current observation). When that error spikes above a threshold, the robot pauses and hands control to a human expert, appends the corrected trajectory to the dataset, and retrains. The key claim is that using denoising loss as the query signal correctly identifies real out-of-distribution states, whereas ensemble disagreement falsely fires at natural multimodal decision points (places where several valid actions exist and the ensemble branches).

Strategy: refine the policy after demos run out — a robot-gated DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) loop that acquires new expert demonstrations on-demand during deployment.

The method, in detail

Inputs. A seed set of offline demonstrations, a robot arm, and a human expert on standby. Observations are typically robot proprioception plus an image encoded by R3M (a frozen ResNet pretrained on human video).

Core policy: DDPM diffusion policy. The policy is a U-Net denoiser trained to predict noise added to action sequences (the DDPM objective). At inference it runs a reverse diffusion chain (64 denoising steps in the released config) starting from Gaussian noise to produce a smooth action chunk.

Uncertainty signal — denoising loss. This is the paper's key trick. During rollout, after the policy generates an action chunk, the system also evaluates the denoising mean-squared error on that (observation, generated action) pair — essentially asking: "how well does the trained denoiser reconstruct its own output from this observation?" High MSE means the observation is far from the training manifold. The code captures this as actiondict["diffusionloss"] returned alongside the action. No additional model (no ensemble, no separate classifier) is needed.

Why this beats ensemble disagreement. At a multimodal decision point (e.g., the robot can grasp left or right), an ensemble spreads probability mass across both modes; their disagreement is high even though the policy "knows" the state well. Denoising loss stays low in both modes because the denoiser can reconstruct either valid action cleanly. Only genuinely OOD (out-of-distribution) states, where no training trajectory covers the current configuration, produce high denoising loss.

The DAgger loop, step by step.

  • Collect numinitep full expert demonstrations to bootstrap the dataset D.
  • Roll out the current diffusion policy. At every timestep, compute denoising loss. If loss exceeds threshold τ, set query = True.
  • When query fires, the expert takes over (or the episode is flagged for expert re-labelling). Corrected (observation, expert-action) pairs are collected.
  • New trajectories are appended to the replay buffer: dataset.rb.extend(TensorDict(...)). Normalizer statistics (mean/std per observation/action dimension) are updated incrementally.
  • The diffusion policy is retrained from scratch (or fine-tuned) on D ∪ D_new using the standard DDPM noise-prediction loss.
  • Repeat from step 2 for the next rollout round.

Threshold τ. Treated as a hyperparameter tuned empirically. The paper sweeps τ and reports AUROC for failure prediction; τ is not derived analytically.

Data-saving mechanism. Because the query signal is accurate, the expert is interrupted far less often than with ensemble methods. The authors report a 7.8× reduction in wall-clock expert time for equivalent task completion rates. The policy converges with fewer total expert interactions because noisy, unnecessary queries (at multimodal branch points) are suppressed.

Simulator and tasks. Experiments use ManiSkill3 (a fork is bundled as mani_skill/ in the repo) for simulated tasks (push-T; stacking; plugging). Real-world experiments also appear in the paper. The Vulkan graphics driver is required by ManiSkill3 for GPU-accelerated rendering.

Results. Compared to Ensemble-DAgger and behavioral cloning baselines: +39 % failure prediction accuracy, +20.6 % task completion, 7.8× faster in wall-clock expert time.

SPOT: SE(3) Pose Trajectory Diffusion for Object-Centric Manipulation

ICRA 2025 · 5 citations · strategy: squeeze more from few demos (object-centric representation makes 8 iPhone demos sufficient)

What it does

SPOT replaces robot-specific action sequences with SE(3) object pose trajectories — 6D rigid-body transforms of the manipulated object relative to a target object — as the intermediate task representation. A diffusion policy is conditioned on these pose trajectories instead of on raw sensor readings or joint angles, which decouples the policy completely from robot embodiment. Because the representation is embodiment-agnostic, demonstrations can come from either a teleoperated robot arm or from a person's bare hands filmed on an iPhone (no action labels needed), and the policy still achieves 100% success on tested tasks with only 8 real-world demos.

This is the "squeeze more from few demos" strategy: the object-centric representation eliminates embodiment-specific noise, so the policy generalizes from far fewer examples than pixel- or joint-space methods require.

The method, in detail

Inputs. At training time SPOT consumes RGBD video clips of task demonstrations. For real-world demos, these are 8 short recordings taken with an iPhone 12 Pro (RGB + LiDAR). For simulation benchmarks (RLBench), 100 episodes per task are used. Each clip can be either action-labeled robot teleoperation or completely action-free human hand video.

Step 1 — Object mesh acquisition. A 3D mesh of the manipulated object is scanned once using the iPhone LiDAR sensor (BundleSDF in simulation). This mesh is needed by FoundationPose in subsequent steps.

Step 2 — 6D pose extraction (FoundationPose). FoundationPose, a zero-shot 6D pose estimator and tracker, runs on every frame of each demo clip. It produces, for each frame t, a 4x4 homogeneous transform Ta (pose of the source/manipulated object in camera frame) and Tb (pose of the target object or surface). YOLO-World provides the 2D bounding box detection needed to initialize FoundationPose on the first frame.

Step 3 — Canonical pose trajectory. The raw per-frame poses are converted to a canonical relative pose: tau = {T^ba}0^l, i.e., the source object's pose expressed in the target object's coordinate frame. This normalization makes the trajectory invariant to where in the scene the demonstration was recorded. Keyframe selection then filters the dense stream: a frame is kept when the relative velocity drops to zero (grasp/release moment) or when translation or rotation displacement exceeds a threshold, yielding a compact trajectory of ~10-30 keyframes per task.

Step 4 — Diffusion policy on SE(3). A lightweight 3-layer MLP with LayerNorm encodes the current object pose (6D) into a 64-dimensional feature. A DDIM diffusion model (100 noise steps during training, 10 during inference) learns to denoise the next-pose distribution conditioned on that feature. The denoising loss is standard MSE over predicted noise: L = MSE(epsilonk, epsilontheta(Tt, T{t+1}^0 + epsilon_k, k)). A separate binary MLP classifier predicts task-end state (sigmoid threshold 0.95) to decide when to open the gripper.

Multi-task extension. For multi-task RLBench training, task descriptions are encoded with a frozen CLIP encoder; the sentence embedding is concatenated with the object-pose feature before the diffusion backbone. Multi-stage tasks additionally receive a one-hot stage encoding.

Step 5 — Action conversion at deployment. During robot execution, FoundationPose tracks object poses in real time from a live RGBD camera. The diffusion policy predicts the next SE(3) waypoint for the object. That object-space waypoint is converted to an end-effector Cartesian target via a known hand-eye calibration: A{t:t+h} = T^objEE · T{t:t+h}, where T^objEE = T^camEE · T^objcam. A Cartesian impedance controller then moves the arm to each waypoint.

The specific trick that saves data. By representing tasks entirely in object-relative SE(3) space, the policy never sees robot joint angles, pixel coordinates, or scene-specific visual features. The same 8-demo dataset simultaneously covers action-less human hand data and robot arm data because the representation collapses both to the same trajectory format. This is in contrast to visuomotor policies (e.g., Diffusion Policy, DP3) that must learn the mapping from raw images to joint commands and therefore need many more demonstrations to cover appearance variation.

Human-Agent Joint Learning for Efficient Robot Manipulation Skill Acquisition

ICRA 2025 · 4 citations · strategy: better recording rig (shared control to ease teleoperation and collect cleaner data)

What it does

Teleoperating a dexterous robot arm is exhausting: the operator must track high-dimensional joint states continuously while the robot kinematics fight against human intuition. HAJL solves this by splitting the robot's end-effector command between the human and a diffusion-model agent that gradually takes over the easy parts, reducing operator fatigue and producing cleaner trajectories. The human can dial a control-ratio knob to hand off more or less of the motion to the agent as it improves, and both the demonstrations and the agent policy are collected and updated in the same loop. Strategy: better recording rig — specifically, a shared-control teleoperation system that lowers the effort barrier so fewer, higher-quality demos can be gathered in less wall-clock time.

The method, in detail

Inputs. The operator uses a hand-pose estimation device (a glove or camera-based tracker); the system reads the human's intended end-effector delta-action a^h ∈ ℝ^m plus the current robot state s ∈ ℝ^n (joint positions, gripper state, vision-derived object pose).

The diffusion blending core. Rather than a classical weighted average, HAJL blends human and agent commands inside the denoising chain of a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM). The human action is injected as a noisy starting point: a^k = a^h + ε^k, where ε^k is Gaussian noise at denoising timestep k. The learned noise-prediction network ε_θ(a^k, s, k) then denoises this toward the policy's preferred action. After K denoising steps the output a^s is the fully agent-driven action; stopping at step k < K yields an interpolation.

Control-ratio γ. The operator's contribution is controlled by γ = k/K: at γ = 1 the output is pure agent, at γ = 0 the output is pure human. Two scheduling rules are provided: (1) manual adjustment by the operator in real time; (2) automatic cosine alignment, γ = ½(1 + cos θ), where θ is the angle between the previous human delta and the shared output — if human and agent already agree, the agent takes over more.

Training loss. Standard DDPM noise-prediction MSE: L = E[‖ε − εθ(ai + ε, s_i, k)‖²₂], trained on the growing mixed dataset of human-only and shared-control demonstrations.

The iterative loop — where data savings come from. (i) Collect a small seed set (~40 simulation demos, ~20 real-world demos) of pure human teleoperation. (ii) Pre-train ε_θ on that seed. (iii) Resume data collection with shared control active: the agent handles the routine parts of the motion while the human corrects edge cases, producing 30 sim / 10–20 real additional demos. (iv) Retrain on the enlarged dataset. Repeat. Because the agent absorbs cognitive load, operators succeed more often and faster, so each session yields more usable demonstrations per hour.

Simulation platform. Six tasks are tested across two simulation environments (exact engine not named in the paper, but references to rigid-body kinematics and gripper/dexterous-hand morphologies are consistent with MuJoCo or a similar physics engine). Tasks include Pick-and-Place, Articulated-Manipulation (door and drawer opening), Push-cube, and Tool-Use (hammering), each run with both a parallel gripper and a multi-finger dexterous hand.

Real robot. A gripper-equipped robot arm on a tabletop; hand pose estimation provides the human command stream via a vision-based tracker (references cite Dex-Retargeting and MediaPipe-style estimators).

Key numbers. Shared control raises collection success rate by ~30 % and doubles collection speed (samples/hour) versus unaided teleoperation, while downstream imitation-learning task success is comparable to or better than pure human data.

From Imitation to Refinement — Residual RL for Precise Assembly

ICRA 2025 · 9 citations · strategy: refine the policy after demos run out

What it does

Behavior cloning (BC) for precise assembly tasks — plugging pegs, mating furniture joints — hits a performance ceiling that more demonstration data cannot fix. The ceiling comes from two compounding problems: the policy was trained on static offline data so it drifts when small errors accumulate (distribution shift), and it executes long "chunks" of pre-planned actions open-loop so it cannot react to those errors mid-chunk. ResiP (Residual for Precise Manipulation) freezes the BC policy entirely and trains a second, small neural network on top of it using reinforcement learning; this network sees the current robot state plus the BC chunk currently playing and outputs a corrective delta that is added to each BC action in real time, restoring closed-loop reactivity without discarding the long-horizon planner that BC provides.

Strategy: refine the policy after demos run out (residual RL layered on a frozen BC base).

The method, in detail

Inputs to the system

  • A pre-trained, frozen diffusion-based BC policy (U-Net, 66 M parameters, predicts chunks of 8 actions, trained on 50 teleoperated demonstrations).
  • A simulation environment (Isaac Gym) that can run 1024 parallel rollouts.
  • A sparse binary reward: +1 when a part-pair is fully assembled, 0 otherwise.

Step 1 — BC pre-training (offline)

Demonstrations are collected via SpaceMouse teleoperation and stored as .pkl files. A preprocessing script (src/dataprocessing/processpickles.py) converts them to flat .zarr arrays. A Diffusion Policy is trained with DDPM (100 denoising steps at training time, 4 DDIM steps at inference) on proprioceptive state observations; it outputs an action chunk of length 8 which the robot plays open-loop.

Step 2 — Residual policy definition

The residual policy is a small Gaussian MLP. At each timestep during a chunk, its input is the concatenation of the current robot state and the BC action for that timestep: sres = [st, abaset]. Its output arest is a corrective delta. The final executed action is:


a_t = a_base_t + α · a_res_t

where α ≤ 1 clips the correction magnitude. This keeps the residual policy from overriding the BC planner entirely; it can only nudge, not steer.

Step 3 — PPO fine-tuning in simulation (online)

The frozen BC policy rolls out chunks in Isaac Gym. At each step inside a chunk, the residual MLP also runs and its correction is applied. The combined trajectory is evaluated against the sparse assembly reward. PPO (discount γ = 0.999, GAE λ = 0.95, clip ε = 0.2, target KL = 0.1) trains only the residual MLP. Because 1024 environments run in parallel, enough completions appear even under a sparse reward for a useful gradient signal. The number of simulation steps varies by task difficulty: 200 for the 0.2 mm-clearance peg-in-hole, 1000 for longer tasks like lamp assembly.

The specific trick that saves data

Fifty demonstrations are enough for BC to learn the global shape of the task (where to reach, which parts to grasp, sequencing). Simulation-based residual RL then corrects the last-mile precision errors that more demos would not fix anyway, because distribution shift is structural rather than data-quantity limited. No new human demonstrations are needed during the RL phase.

Results

On the 0.2 mm-clearance peg-in-hole task (FurnitureBench / Factory): BC alone achieves 5% success; ResiP achieves 99%. On five other assembly tasks, ResiP improves over BC by 20–40 percentage points. Real-world transfer uses 10–40 real demonstrations for BC and then the same simulation-trained residual policy is transferred zero-shot.

What it does

Stonefish is an open-source C++ simulator for marine robots (AUVs and ROVs) that was extended in this paper with new ML-oriented capabilities: three new vision sensors (event-based camera, thermal camera, optical flow camera), visual light communication between agents, tethered vehicle support, a reworked thruster and hydrodynamics model, improved sonar accuracy, and an automated annotation tool that produces pixel-level ground-truth labels alongside synthetic imagery. The core idea is that real underwater data is prohibitively expensive and dangerous to collect, so you generate unlimited labelled training data in simulation and transfer learned models to real hardware. This is the generate data in simulation strategy — the simulator itself is the contribution, lowering the cost of labelled data to essentially zero for underwater ML.

The method, in detail

Inputs. Users define a simulated world (vehicles, terrain, lighting, water properties) in XML. The simulator ingests this description and runs a real-time physics loop.

Physics engine. The core is Bullet Physics, extended with custom hydrodynamic forces. Version 1.4/1.5 reimplemented the hydrodynamics layer: drag, added-mass effects, and directional buoyancy are computed from the actual surface mesh geometry of each body, not from hand-tuned coefficients. Thruster models now include nonlinear response curves, command saturation, deadbands, and response delays that match real underwater thrusters. Tether dynamics (tension, drag, cable entanglement) are modelled for ROVs connected to a surface vessel.

Rendering pipeline. Built on OpenGL 4.3. The renderer models wavelength-dependent light absorption and scattering in water, producing physically-plausible underwater imagery for training color cameras and — new in v1.5 — the three ML-targeted vision sensors:

  • Event-based camera (EventBasedCamera.h): simulates a neuromorphic (dynamic vision) sensor that fires asynchronous per-pixel events when pixel brightness changes by a threshold. This gives high-temporal-resolution training data without motion blur.
  • Thermal camera (ThermalCamera.h): renders infrared signatures from object temperature properties, enabling heat-contrast detection tasks.
  • Optical flow camera (OpticalFlowCamera.h): computes dense per-pixel motion vectors from consecutive frames, providing ground-truth flow without any post-processing.
  • Segmentation camera (SegmentationCamera.h): renders semantic instance masks, one integer label per pixel.

Automated annotation tool. The paper introduces a pipeline that simultaneously captures RGB, depth, segmentation, and event streams from the same simulated scene. Because all labels (bounding boxes, instance masks, pixel flow, depth) are derived algebraically from the simulation state, they are exact — no human annotator required. This is the specific trick that eliminates the annotation cost that dominates real underwater dataset creation.

Sonar. Forward-looking sonar (FLS), mechanical scanning imaging sonar (MSIS), side-scan sonar (SSS), and multibeam sonar are all simulated with acoustic absorption and scattering in the water column, enabling training of sonar-based perception models.

Visual light communication (VLC). A new comms module lets multiple simulated agents exchange data via optical wireless links, enabling multi-robot coordination research.

Companion dataset. The paper produced eStonefish-scenes, a synthetic event-based optical flow dataset generated with Stonefish. Scenes contain event streams, grayscale frames, and ground-truth optical flow stored as HDF5 files under the eWiz format (events.hdf5, gray.hdf5, flow.hdf5). This dataset validates that Stonefish-generated data can train models that transfer to real hardware.

Sim-to-real transfer. Evaluation compares Stonefish-trained object detectors and segmentation models against real-world baselines, showing the synthetic data closes a meaningful portion of the domain gap for underwater scenes.

OceanSim: A GPU-Accelerated Underwater Robot Perception Simulation Framework

IROS 2025 · 6 citations · Strategy: generate data in simulation (sim-to-real)

What it does

OceanSim is an open-source underwater robot simulator built on NVIDIA Isaac Sim that generates physics-accurate synthetic sensor data — RGB camera imagery and imaging sonar scans — at real-time speed by running the entire sensor pipeline on the GPU. The core problem it solves is the high cost and logistical difficulty of collecting annotated underwater robot data: real-world trials require ships, tanks, and divers, so training data is scarce. OceanSim replaces that with a simulator that renders underwater imagery at 28–32 frames per second and sonar at real-time rates, making it cheap to generate large labelled datasets for training perception models. The strategy is: generate data in simulation — fake it with a high-fidelity simulator, then transfer learned models to a real robot.

The method, in detail

Inputs. A USD (Universal Scene Description) 3D scene file — either a photogrammetry scan of a real environment or a hand-built scene — plus a robot model with sensors attached. The user configures water-column parameters (light attenuation coefficient βattn per RGB channel, backscatter coefficient βbs, veiling luminance B∞) and sonar parameters (acoustic attenuation α, acoustic reflectance Ar per material, beam pattern, noise model).

Camera image formation model. For each pixel the rendered image follows:

Ic = J · exp(−βattn,c · d) + B∞,c · (1 − exp(−β_bs,c · d))

where J is the clear-water radiance from the scene (computed by Isaac Sim's GPU ray tracer), d is the pixel's depth, βattn,c is the per-channel signal attenuation in water, and βbs,c is the backscatter (veiling luminance) coefficient. The first term makes objects dim and lose colour with distance; the second term adds the characteristic blue-green haze from suspended particles. Parameters are tuned interactively in a GUI with real-time preview, letting users match a specific water body.

Imaging sonar model. Sonar (a 2D fan-beam device that returns a range-azimuth intensity image) is the hardest sensor to simulate because it is purely acoustic. OceanSim uses NVIDIA Warp and Omniverse Replicator to ray-trace the scene on the GPU and compute per-hit intensity:

Isonar = Ar · (−vin/|vin| · vn/|vn|) · exp(−α · d)

where vin is the incident ray direction, vn is the surface normal at the hit, A_r is the acoustic reflectance of the material (tunable per semantic label), and α is the water-column acoustic attenuation. The dot product captures the angle-of-incidence effect (surfaces facing away from the sonar return low signal). Speckle noise is Rayleigh-distributed with beam-pattern-dependent gain, plus multiplicative Gaussian noise. The 3D hit points are then binned into a 2D range × azimuth polar grid to produce the sonar image a real device would output.

Additional sensors. DVL (Doppler Velocity Log) with adaptive rate and beam dropout; barometer; IMU (from Isaac Sim's native models).

The data-cheapening trick. The entire rendering pipeline (ray-tracing, intensity computation, binning, noise) runs in parallel on the GPU via NVIDIA Warp. This gives 28–32 FPS sonar rendering vs 1.5–3.8 FPS in HoloOcean (the previous best open tool), with zero octree pre-computation overhead (HoloOcean needs 317–502 seconds of cache build time per scene). Fast rendering means a researcher can generate thousands of labelled sonar-image frames in minutes rather than hours, making simulation a practical substitute for real dives.

Sim-to-real validation approach. The authors built a digital twin of the University of Michigan Marine Hydrodynamics Laboratory (MHL) towing tank using photogrammetry from a stereo camera survey. They placed the same rock platform in both real and virtual environments, captured synchronized camera+sonar pairs, and compared them. Colour accuracy is measured with RGB angular error (mean deviation in degrees between rendered and real colour patches): OceanSim scores 7.88°/13.78° across two viewpoints vs HoloOcean's 19.01°/16.72° — roughly 2× more accurate colour reproduction.

Domain Randomization for Object Detection in Manufacturing Applications Using Synthetic Data: A Comprehensive Study

ICRA 2025 · 5 citations · strategy: generate data in simulation

What it does

The paper shows you can train competitive industrial object detectors using only synthetic images — no real annotated training data — by carefully randomizing a Blender rendering pipeline across object textures, lighting, camera pose, background, and post-processing. The authors introduce a new dataset of 15 manufacturing parts (SIP15-OD), run a structured ablation to identify which randomization factors matter most (PBR material rendering and distractors dominate), and train YOLOv8 detectors that reach 94–99% mAP@50 on held-out real images. Strategy: generate data in simulation (sim-to-real via domain randomization; no demonstrations involved).

The method, in detail

Inputs. CAD models of industrial parts (supplied by Scania as OBJ/FBX files), three texture libraries (random RGB colors; Flickr 8K image textures; CG Texture PBR material library with metalness and roughness maps), a background image library (BG-20K), and a config JSON that controls every randomization range.

Core mechanism: fully scriptable Blender pipeline.

  • Scene initialization. A ground plane (z=0) and four vertical walls are created. Each wall and ground surface is assigned a random background image from BG-20K. Six primitive 3D meshes (cubes, spheres, cones) are scattered without gravity constraints as visual distractors — these turned out to be one of the two most important factors in ablation.
  • Object placement. The number of part instances is sampled uniformly (0 to the full count). Each instance is placed at a random 3D position and rotation, with a non-overlap constraint enforced. Gravity is simulated by zeroing the lowest z-coordinate of each object so parts rest on the floor.
  • Texture assignment. Each object is independently assigned one of three texture types: (a) flat RGB with random hue/saturation/value, (b) an image texture from Flickr 8K wrapped onto the mesh UV, or (c) a PBR material from CG Texture with randomized metalness and roughness. PBR with the Cycles path-tracer engine was found to be the single largest performance driver.
  • Camera sampling. The camera position is sampled in spherical coordinates (radius r, polar angle θ, azimuthal angle φ) and converted to Cartesian. Radius is computed from camera sensor parameters and the area of the object cluster. The focus point is the center of mass of the scene, with a random XYZ shift. Focal length is randomized to vary apparent zoom.
  • Illumination. Area lights are placed; count is proportional to scene size. Each light has an independently randomized RGB color and intensity (energy).
  • Rendering. Either Cycles (path tracing, slower, more realistic specular/metallic appearance) or Eevee (rasterization, fast). The paper conclusively shows Cycles+PBR outperforms Eevee by a large margin; this is the specific trick that closes the sim-to-real gap for shiny metal parts.
  • Post-processing. Salt-and-pepper noise and Gaussian blur are applied at randomized strength and probability, mimicking sensor noise and motion blur.
  • Annotation. Segmentation masks and YOLO-format bounding boxes are derived from 2D pixel coordinates in the Blender camera viewport; masks are saved alongside images automatically.

Training. YOLOv8 (pre-trained on COCO) is fine-tuned on the synthetic set for 500 epochs, batch 80, image size 720, IoU 0.6. Rendering runs across 10 parallel Blender instances (1–3 seconds/image); generating 4000 images + training on a single A100 takes ~4 hours total.

What saves labeling cost. Real images require manual bounding-box annotation per image. Here, annotation is generated automatically by the renderer, so the only human labor is preparing CAD models — which manufacturing companies already have — and running the Blender script. Zero real annotated images are needed for training.

Ablation structure. The authors systematically turn factors on and off: material type (RGB vs. image texture vs. PBR) × renderer (Cycles vs. Eevee) × distractors on/off × post-processing on/off × dataset size (500/1000/2000/4000 images). PBR+Cycles and distractors were the two dominant factors; post-processing gave a smaller boost; more than ~2000 images yielded diminishing returns.

Learning to Swim: Reinforcement Learning for 6-DOF Control of Thruster-Driven Autonomous Underwater Vehicles

ICRA 2025 · 9 citations · strategy: generate data in simulation (sim-to-real, domain randomization)

What it does

Training a PID controller for an autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) requires hand-tuning for every new payload or hull configuration; this paper replaces it with a PPO reinforcement learning policy trained entirely inside a GPU-parallelized simulator of underwater hydrodynamics, then deployed zero-shot to a real AUV with no real-world data collected at all.

The core data-savings trick is domain randomization: the simulator randomizes buoyancy offset and vehicle volume during training so the resulting policy tolerates the physical variations that would otherwise demand repeated real-world re-tuning.

This is firmly the "generate data in simulation" strategy — no human demonstrations are collected; all training experience is synthetic.

The method, in detail

Inputs at runtime: A 17-dimensional observation vector: 3D position offset (desired minus current position, expressed in the vehicle's own coordinate frame), desired orientation as a quaternion (4D), current orientation as a quaternion (4D), linear velocity (3D), angular velocity (3D).

Action space: Six continuous thruster commands, normalized to [−1, 1], then scaled to PWM (pulse-width modulation) values that drive the physical motor controllers directly. There is no intermediate force decomposition — the network learns the thruster mixing itself.

Simulator: A custom environment built on NVIDIA Isaac Lab (version 2.2.0, which sits on top of Isaac Sim 4.5.0). Isaac Lab uses GPU-based parallel simulation; 2048 independent underwater vehicle environments run simultaneously on one GPU. Each episode lasts 3 simulated seconds. The hydrodynamic model (rigidbodyhydrodynamics.py) applies simplified Morison-equation drag and an explicit buoyancy force at the center of buoyancy (CoB). The thruster model (thruster_dynamics.py) converts PWM commands to rotor velocities to thrust forces, capturing first-order lag.

Domain randomization (the specific data-saving trick): At the start of every episode, resetdomain samples:

  • CoB-to-CoM (center of mass) offset: a random vector drawn uniformly from a sphere of radius 0.05 m. This mimics adding new pressure housings or shifting payload.
  • Vehicle volume: uniform draw from [0.0197, 0.0257] m³. This changes the net buoyancy force magnitude.

Mass is held fixed at 22.701 kg. The paper also tests "Small" and "Large" randomization regimes (radii up to 0.5 m, volume range up to ±1.5 L) to characterize sensitivity.

RL algorithm: PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) via the RSL-RL library, an on-policy algorithm that collects rollouts across all 2048 environments in parallel, computes advantages with GAE, and updates a shared policy network. Training converges in approximately 400 iterations; wall-clock time is 10–20 minutes on an NVIDIA A6000 GPU using ~11.4 GB VRAM.

Policy network: A 2-layer MLP taking the 17-D observation and outputting 6 thruster commands. Specific hidden-layer sizes are not reported in the paper.

Reward function (three weighted Gaussian terms):

  • Position accuracy: exp(−‖offset‖²) × 0.2
  • Orientation accuracy: exp(−quaterror) × 0.5, where quaterror is the angle between desired and current quaternion
  • Energy penalty: exp(−‖actions‖²) × 0.2 (encourages low thruster effort)

Sim-to-real transfer: Policy is deployed zero-shot — no real-world fine-tuning — onto a CUREE AUV running on a Jetson Orin NX at ~20 Hz. State estimation uses an EKF fusing an AprilTag camera, a DVL (Doppler velocity log), and an IMU. One small engineering patch for stability: the desired quaternion is smoothed with SLERP (spherical linear interpolation) to handle unmodeled motor lag.

AnyCar to Anywhere: Learning Universal Dynamics Model for Agile and Adaptive Mobility

ICRA 2025 · 6 citations · Strategy: generate data in simulation (multi-simulator synthetic pre-training + few-shot real fine-tuning)

What it does

AnyCar trains a single transformer-based forward dynamics model across data from four different simulators — each with different physics engines and vehicle parameter distributions — so the learned model generalizes across wheeled robot morphologies without per-robot re-tuning. At deployment, the model acts as the internal predictor inside a sampling-based model-predictive controller called MPPI (Model Predictive Path Integral control), which rolls out thousands of candidate action sequences through the learned model and picks the best one online. When the zero-shot transferred model is not precise enough, a small batch of real trajectories collected on the target vehicle is used to fine-tune the model in minutes. This is a generate data in simulation strategy: the core training-data cost is eliminated by running diverse simulators instead of collecting real-world demonstrations.

The method, in detail

Inputs. The dynamics model takes as input a short history window of vehicle states (position, heading, velocity, acceleration, steering angle) and a proposed action (throttle, steering command). It predicts the next vehicle state one step ahead.

Multi-simulator data generation. Training data comes from four simulators: Assetto Corsa (a racing-game-derived vehicle dynamics engine), IsaacSim (NVIDIA GPU-accelerated robot sim), MuJoCo, and a Numeric DBM (a parametric bicycle-model simulator). Each simulator exposes different physics fidelity and vehicle-parameter ranges, so the aggregate dataset covers a wide distribution of vehicle masses, wheelbases, tire stiffnesses, and friction coefficients without the researcher ever touching a real robot. Data collection scripts (isaacsimcollectdata.py, parallelmujococollectdata.py) run autonomously inside each sim, and collectdataonpolicy.py can collect data by running MPPI inside the sim so the distribution of visited states matches what the deployed controller will actually see.

Model architecture. The core model is a transformer (implemented in JAX) defined in carfoundation/jaxmodels.py. The transformer processes a sequence of past (state, action) pairs and predicts the next state residual. Using a transformer rather than a fixed-structure bicycle model allows the network to implicitly identify which vehicle is being controlled from the recent trajectory context — a form of in-context system identification. The architecture is trained with a supervised prediction loss (mean-squared error on next-state prediction) over shuffled mini-batches from all four simulators.

Robustness augmentation (the sim-to-real trick). During pre-training, inputs are corrupted with three techniques: random masking of input channels, additive noise injection, and adversarial input perturbation ("attacking the inputs"). This is not standard domain randomization of visual textures; it is sensor-level noise augmentation applied to the state history, which teaches the model to make accurate predictions even when the on-robot state estimator is noisy or has missing channels. This single augmentation strategy accounts for a major fraction of the sim-to-real gap in state-based (non-visual) dynamics prediction.

MPPI controller. At runtime, MPPI samples K candidate action sequences (typically thousands) by perturbing a nominal plan with Gaussian noise, rolls each sequence forward through the learned dynamics model for a horizon H, evaluates a cost (tracking error + speed objective + safety penalty), and takes a temperature-weighted average of the samples as the control output. Because the dynamics model is fast (a compact transformer forward pass in JAX on GPU), thousands of rollouts run in real time. MPPI is model-based: it does not need any additional training, it just needs a good model, which is what the pre-training provides.

Few-shot fine-tuning (the data-efficiency lever). After zero-shot transfer from simulation to a new real vehicle, the user drives the robot for a short collection session — on the order of a few minutes of trajectories — and runs trainfinetunejax.py to fine-tune the pre-trained transformer on this small real dataset. Because the pre-trained model already has good priors, fine-tuning converges with far less data than training from scratch. The paper reports that zero-shot transfer alone already beats specialist models tuned specifically for each vehicle by up to 54% on agility metrics, and fine-tuning narrows remaining gaps further.

What saves data. The simulation pre-training means zero real robot data is needed for the bulk of training. The few-shot fine-tuning step needs only a handful of real trajectories — not hundreds of human demonstrations. This is purely a sim-to-real + fine-tuning data strategy with no teleoperation, no motion-capture rig, and no LLM in the loop.

FLaRe: Achieving Masterful and Adaptive Robot Policies with Large-Scale Reinforcement Learning Fine-Tuning

ICRA 2025 (Best Paper Finalist) · 6 citations · strategy: refine the policy after demos run out

What it does

FLaRe takes a robot policy that was pre-trained by imitating large-scale human demonstrations (behavior cloning, abbreviated BC), and then fine-tunes it with reinforcement learning (RL) using only a sparse binary reward — no extra human demonstrations required. The pre-trained policy reaches a performance ceiling because BC is offline and cannot correct its own mistakes during execution; FLaRe breaks through that ceiling by letting the robot practice in simulation with RL. The strategy is: refine the policy after demos run out — squeezing more capability from a fixed demonstration set by spending compute, not human labor.

The method, in detail

Inputs. The method starts from a frozen SPOC checkpoint (a transformer-based BC policy pre-trained on the SPOC CHORES benchmark across four manipulation/navigation tasks: ObjectNav, PickUp, Fetch, RoomVisit). The robot runs in AI2THOR — a photorealistic indoor simulator with 150,000 procedurally generated houses (ProcTHOR) and 800,000+ annotated 3D Objaverse objects. The action space has 20 discrete choices (base movement, arm control, gripper rotation, pick/drop, done).

Pre-trained architecture (SPOC). Two Intel RealSense RGB cameras (384×224 pixels each) feed into a frozen DINOv2 ViT-S vision backbone, which outputs patch-level feature vectors. A 3-layer, 512-hidden-dim, 8-head transformer state encoder aggregates those features along with a T5 text embedding of the language instruction and a history of past actions. A causal Llama-2-style transformer decoder (3 layers, same dims) autoregressively outputs the action. A KV-cache trick reduces inference cost from quadratic to linear in sequence length.

RL fine-tuning. The algorithm is PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), an on-policy policy gradient method. The reward is binary and sparse: 1 if the language instruction is successfully completed, 0 otherwise. Navigation tasks run for 20 million environment steps; manipulation tasks for 50 million steps.

The specific trick that saves data — four gradient stabilization moves. BC-pre-trained policies are fragile to naive RL fine-tuning: any one of the following omissions collapses the success rate to 0% in ablations.

  • On-policy algorithm. PPO instead of an off-policy method like SAC, because off-policy updates diverge from the behavior distribution the pre-trained features were built on.
  • Reduced learning rate. 2×10⁻⁵ instead of the 2×10⁻⁴ used when training from scratch — a 10× reduction that prevents rapid overwriting of the BC-learned features.
  • Disabled entropy bonus. The standard PPO entropy regularization term is zeroed out (weight = 0.0); it destabilizes the already-sensible action distribution inherited from BC.
  • Separate actor and critic networks. Rather than sharing a trunk (as is typical in PPO), actor and critic are initialized as independent networks from the same SPOC checkpoint. This prevents critic gradients — which optimize a value estimate, not robot behavior — from corrupting the policy's representations. The DINOv2 vision backbone is kept frozen throughout, which also provides sim-to-real-transferable visual features.

Domain randomization (random color jitter, random crops, posterization) is applied during RL training to keep the frozen DINOv2 features consistent with what will be seen on a real Stretch RE-1 robot.

Adaptation. After fine-tuning on seen tasks, FLaRe can rapidly adapt to unseen task types (relational attributes, affordance reasoning) and new robot bodies (LocoBot) with less than one day of additional RL — no new demonstrations needed.

Results. 79.5% average success rate on long-horizon mobile manipulation in unseen apartments; +23.6% over prior state-of-the-art in simulation and +30.7% on a real Stretch RE-1, zero-shot sim-to-real.

ExploRLLM: Guiding Exploration in Reinforcement Learning with Large Language Models

ICRA 2025 · 6 citations · strategy: exploration / residual-RL

arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.09583

Project: https://explorllm.github.io

Authors: Runyu Ma, Jelle Luijkx, Zlatan Ajanovic, Jens Kober (TU Delft / RWTH Aachen)

Code: promised at project site; no public GitHub URL confirmed as of mid-2026.

What it does

ExploRLLM makes RL for tabletop robot manipulation converge faster and with fewer samples by using a large language model (GPT-4) to write executable Python policy code that immediately gives the agent a reasonable starting behavior, and by having a vision-language model collapse the raw RGB-D camera image into a small, object-centric observation of bounding-box positions and image crops. A residual RL agent (SAC) then runs on top — it learns only the small correction offset needed to fix the places where the LLM's physical intuition is wrong (e.g., where exactly to grip a letter). Trained policies transfer zero-shot from simulation to a real Franka Panda arm without any fine-tuning.

The method, in detail

Step 1 — Command parsing with GPT-4.

The natural-language task ("pick the red letter O and place it in the blue bowl") is fed to GPT-4 with a few-shot prompt that maps it into a structured template: {pickobject: "letter O", placetarget: "blue bowl"}. This template tags the objects that matter for the current task and discards the rest of the scene.

Step 2 — Object detection with ViLD.

A Vision-Language Distillation (ViLD) model takes the current overhead RGB-D image and the tagged object labels and outputs bounding boxes with center positions P_vlm. During simulation training, ground-truth simulator positions are used instead (with added Gaussian noise, σ = half the crop radius, to simulate VLM error). At test time on the real robot the actual ViLD detections are used.

Step 3 — Compact observation construction.

The reformulated observation ŝᵗ is assembled from three parts: (a) the interpreted command vector l̃ (a fixed-dim encoding of the template), (b) the set of detected object center positions Pvlm, and (c) RGB-D image patches Ivlm cut out around each detected bounding box. This replaces the full 640×480 RGB-D image with a handful of small crops — the observation is no longer spatially confused by irrelevant background.

Step 4 — LLM policy code generation (two levels).

*High-level policy:* GPT-4 is prompted once (few-shot) with the list of available robot motion primitives (pick, place) and a custom API for querying geometric relationships. Output: a short Python function that, given the parsed template and detected positions, returns a pick-or-place action selecting which object and which target position to use. This implements the task's commonsense sequencing logic (pick before place, pick the right object).

*Low-level affordance policy:* GPT-4-with-Vision is prompted with example images and language descriptions of the robot workspace. Output: Python code (using OpenCV and NumPy) that produces a 2D probability heatmap over an image patch — this heatmap indicates where on the object the gripper should make contact. Multiple code candidates are generated; the one with the highest empirical success rate in simulation is kept. The heatmap peak is converted to a 2D pixel coordinate and back-projected to a workspace position.

Step 5 — Residual action space.

The raw action space is {prim, P} — pick/place primitive plus a 2D top-down workspace position. ExploRLLM reformulates this as ã = {prim, i, P_res}:

  • prim: binary (0=pick, 1=place)
  • i: index into the detected object list (discrete, ≤3 objects in tested tasks)
  • P_res: a continuous 2D residual offset

The executed position is P̃ = Pvlm[i] + Pres. The RL agent never has to discover object locations from scratch — it just corrects the LLM's per-pixel prediction by a small delta.

Step 6 — SAC with LLM-guided exploration.

Training uses Soft Actor-Critic (stable-baselines3) with a modified rollout collection loop. A 20,000-step warm-up phase runs with no LLM guidance to populate the replay buffer. After warm-up, with probability ε (swept over {0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9}; best was ε=0.2) the agent executes the LLM-generated action instead of its own — this acts like an ε-greedy bonus that biases exploration toward high-value regions from the start. The SAC network uses: two CNN layers to process the image patches → concatenation with position features and gripper state → one self-attention layer → two-layer MLP heads for actor and critic. Short-horizon tasks train for 25k steps; long-horizon for 75k steps.

Step 7 — Sim-to-real transfer.

The simulated tabletop environment matches the real setup (UR5e / Franka Panda geometry, top-down RGB-D camera). No domain randomization beyond the bounding-box noise injection during training. At deployment the EAGERx framework mediates robot control; the learned policy runs directly on real camera frames processed by ViLD. No additional fine-tuning is applied.

The pipeline — step-by-step trace through the actual code

Phase 1 — Dataset construction

RLBench path:

envrlbenchperact/data/collectzarrrlbench_peract.py

The script replays PerAct pre-generated demos inside CoppeliaSim. For each frame, it reads the 7-DoF world-frame pose of both the grasp object and the target object (position xyz + quaternion xyzw). It then calls getrelpose(targetobjpose, graspobjpose) from utils/poseutils.py to express the grasp object's pose *in the coordinate frame of the target object*. That relative SE(3) pose (shape [7]) is the state. The next-frame version is statenext. The action is calculateaction(state, statenext) — a delta: translation difference plus the relative rotation quaternion Qp = QW * Qch.inverse. Progress is a scalar in [0,1] tracking task completion. Everything is packed into a zarr archive under keys state, statenext, action, progress, progressbinary, variation, task_stage.

Real-world path:

envreal/data/preparepose.py → runs FoundationPoseWrapper.createestimator() → calls estimater.register(K, rgb, depth, obmask) for the first frame then estimater.trackone(rgb, depth, K) for subsequent frames on both the grasp object and the target object from iPhone RGBD video. Results saved as .npy. Then envreal/data/collectzarrreal.py does the same getrelpose conversion and writes identical zarr schema.

Phase 2 — Training

tools/train_dp3.py, class TrainDP3Workspace

  • Hydra instantiates SimpleDP3 from config/simpledp3.yaml (policy.target_).
  • Dataset: RLBenchBaseDataset._getitem calls self.sampler.samplesequence(idx) to get a window of horizon=4 consecutive frames. sampletodata() returns obs['agentpos'] (shape [4, 7]) and action (shape [4, 8], the 7 delta-pose values plus the binary progress scalar).

Optional data augmentation: addrandomnoiseto_pos applies a small random rotation (Rodrigues formula) + translation jitter to all poses in a window. Symmetric augmentation applies a fixed-axis rotation for tasks like placing cups that are rotationally symmetric.

  • Loss: SimpleDP3.compute_loss(batch)

- custom_normalize: translates only the xyz part through LinearNormalizer; the quaternion [qx,qy,qz,qw] is separately L2-normalized via F.normalize (unit quaternion constraint).

- Encodes observation via obsencoder = DP3Encoder (a PointNet that handles point clouds or a simple MLP when only agentpos is present).

- Randomly samples a diffusion timestep t and adds noise: noisytrajectory = noisescheduler.add_noise(trajectory, noise, t).

- Passes noisy_trajectory through ConditionalUnet1D, conditioning via FiLM modulation on the observation features (scale + bias per channel).

- Computes MSE loss on the noise/sample prediction (DDIM scheduler, predictiontype: sample). When useprogress=True, the last channel (progress/gripper) uses binary cross-entropy with a 0.1 weight.

Phase 3 — Inference / evaluation

tools/evaldp3.py → TrainDP3Workspace.eval() creates a FoundationPoseWrapper and an RLBenchRunner, loads the checkpoint, then calls envrunner.run(policy).

RLBenchRunner.run():

  • For each test episode, resets the RLBench env to the demo initial state.
  • Instantiates RLBenchDP3Policy(env, policy, usefp, enablestage).
  • Steps through a stage machine in RLBenchDP3Policy.get_action(obs):

"reach" stage: Replays the first two waypoints from the recorded demo to bring the gripper to the grasp position. Calls env.starttracking() which initializes FoundationPoseWrapper.createestimator() (if use_fp=True).

"grasp" stage: Records the transform Tobjtogripper = getrelpose(graspobjpose, gripperpose) — the rigid offset from object to gripper, used for closed-loop control.

"move" stage — the core loop:

1. updatehistorybuffer(obs): Reads the current object pose. If usefp=True, this comes from CustomRLBenchEnv.getposeest() which calls poseestimator.trackone(rgb, depth, K) and converts the result to world frame via fpframetoworldframe(fpmat, R). Otherwise, it reads the simulator ground-truth. Calls getrelpose(targetobjpose, graspobjpose) to get the current state relative to the target.

2. getsubgoal(obs): Builds obsdict['agentpos'] from the history buffer and calls subgoalpolicy.predictaction(obsdictinput).

3. SimpleDP3.predictaction(obsdict):

- Normalizes observation via custom_normalize.

- Encodes observation: nobsfeatures = self.obsencoder(thisnobs), shape [B, obsfeaturedim] → becomes globalcond.

- Runs conditionalsample(): starts from Gaussian noise of shape [B, horizon, actiondim], iterates DDIM denoising steps, at each step calling model(sample=trajectory, timestep=t, globalcond=globalcond).

- Slices the output: action = actionpred[:, nobssteps-1 : nobssteps-1+naction_steps], returns shape [B, 3, 8].

4. Back in getsubgoal: takes pred[0] — the first predicted step — and splits into selectaction = selectpred[:7] (delta SE(3)) and selectprogress = select_pred[7].

5. Converts the predicted delta back to an absolute object pose:

subgoalrelativetotarget = calculategoalpose(objposerelativetotarget, selectaction)

(adds translation, composes quaternions: Qp * Qch).

6. Converts from target frame back to world frame:

subgoalinworld = relativetotargettoworld(subgoalrelativetotarget, targetobj_pose)

(matrix multiplication: TWA = TWB @ T_BA).

7. Converts from object pose to gripper pose using Tobjto_gripper.

8. Returns this as a 7-DoF end-effector pose action to RLBench's EndEffectorPoseViaPlanning action mode, which solves IK and moves the arm.

"leave" stage: When select_progress > 0.9, opens gripper and lifts.

Where the core idea lives — the SE(3) representation and the diffusion model

SE(3) representation

File: utils/pose_utils.py


def get_rel_pose(pose1, pose2):
    # pose1 = target object, pose2 = grasp object
    pos1, quat1 = np.array(pose1[:3]), np.array(pose1[3:])
    pos2, quat2 = np.array(pose2[:3]), np.array(pose2[3:])
    mat1 = T.quat2mat(quat1)
    mat2 = T.quat2mat(quat2)
    pos, mat = compute_rel_transform(pos1, mat1, pos2, mat2)
    quat = T.mat2quat(mat)
    return np.concatenate([pos, quat])

This is the representation: the grasp object's SE(3) pose in the coordinate frame of the target object. A 7-vector [x, y, z, qx, qy, qz, qw]. Because it is relative to the target, it is invariant to the absolute position of the scene, the camera, and crucially the robot embodiment. A human hand doing a pour and a robot arm doing the same pour produce the same trajectory in this space.

The action between two consecutive states is:


def calculate_action(current_pose, goal_pose):
    # translation delta + relative rotation (left-multiply convention)
    Qp = QW * Qch.inverse   # relative rotation quaternion
    a_x, a_y, a_z = x2-x1, y2-y1, z2-z1
    return [a_x, a_y, a_z, a_qx, a_qy, a_qz, a_qw]

File: diffusionpolicy3d/policy/simpledp3.py, function customnormalize


pose_ori = pose[key][:, :, 3:7]
pose[key] = pose[key][:, :, :3]          # strip quat
pose_ori = F.normalize(pose_ori, dim=2)  # unit quaternion
npose = normalizer.normalize(pose)       # normalize xyz only
npose[key] = torch.concat([npose[key], pose_ori], dim=-1)

This is how the quaternion is handled differently from the translation: the xyz part goes through LinearNormalizer (min-max scaling to [-1,1]), while the quaternion is L2-normalized to lie on the unit sphere. Both are kept in the same 7-dim action vector. The separate treatment is needed because ordinary min-max normalization would destroy the quaternion constraint.

The diffusion model

File: diffusionpolicy3d/model/diffusion/simpleconditionalunet1d.py, class ConditionalUnet1D

The model is a 1-D temporal UNet over the action horizon (length 4 in default config). The conditioning mechanism is FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation):


# In ConditionalResidualBlock1D.forward():
embed = self.cond_encoder(cond)
embed = embed.reshape(embed.shape[0], 2, self.out_channels, 1)
scale = embed[:, 0, ...]
bias  = embed[:, 1, ...]
out = scale * out + bias

The observation features (the encoded current object pose history, optionally plus CLIP language embedding and stage one-hot) are broadcast into every residual block of the UNet as scale/bias modulations. Down-dims are (128, 256, 384) by default.

Residual RL (From Imitation to Refinement) — code walkthrough

What the repo is / how you'd run it

Paper: "From Imitation to Refinement — Residual RL for Precise Visual Assembly" (Ankile et al., arXiv 2407.16677). The idea is that behavior cloning from ~50 human demonstrations gives a solid but imprecise base policy; RL then adds a thin correction layer on top, recovering closed-loop precision without collecting more demonstrations.

Simulator: FurnitureBench running on top of NVIDIA IsaacGym. Tasks are furniture assembly pieces (oneleg, lamp, roundtable, mugrack, factorypeg_hole). The sim runs up to 1024 parallel environments on a single GPU using IsaacGym's GPU-pipeline physics.

Two-stage workflow:

Stage 1 — behavior cloning (BC) pre-training:


python -m src.train.bc +experiment=state/diff_unet task=one_leg randomness=low dryrun=false

Stage 2 — residual RL fine-tuning (the main contribution), pointing at the saved BC checkpoint:


python -m src.train.residual_ppo \
    base_policy.wt_path=/path/to/actor_chkpt.pt \
    env.task=one_leg env.randomness=low \
    num_env_steps=700 \
    debug=false

Configuration is managed by Hydra. The root config for residual RL is src/config/baseresidualrl.yaml; actor architecture details live in src/config/actor/residualdiffusion.yaml (or residualmlp.yaml). Key knobs: numenvs=1024, updateepochs=50, discount=0.999, clipcoef=0.2, residuall1/l2 regularizers. WandB is used for experiment tracking and weight storage; basepolicy.wandbid or basepolicy.wtpath must be supplied to locate the frozen base.

    base_naction = agent.base_action_normalized(next_obs)

    # Step B: build the residual policy's input = [normalized_state, base_action]
    next_nobs = agent.process_obs(next_obs)
    next_residual_nobs = torch.cat([next_nobs, base_naction], dim=-1)

    # Step C: residual policy samples a correction + its value estimate
    with torch.no_grad():

            residual_policy.get_action_and_value(next_residual_nobs)
        )

    # Step D: combine base + scaled residual -> actual action sent to sim
    residual_naction = residual_naction_samp if not eval_mode else naction_mean
    naction = base_naction + residual_naction * residual_policy.action_scale

    action = agent.normalizer(naction, "action", forward=False)
    next_obs, reward, next_done, truncated, info = env.step(action)

The stored actions[step] is the *residual* action (residualnaction), not the combined action. The stored obs[step] is the *augmented* observation (nextresidualnobs). This is important for the PPO update: the policy re-evaluates its log-probability over (augmentedobs, residual_action) pairs.

Where the core idea lives — base + residual combination

File: src/train/residual_ppo.py, lines 353–368 (the rollout inner loop).

The exact lines:

residualpolicy.actionscale = 0.1 (from src/config/actor/residual_diffusion.yaml). This hard cap means the residual can shift the action by at most ±10% of the normalized action range — deliberately limiting how far the RL correction can move from the demonstration-derived base.

The augmented observation [normalizedstate, baseaction] means the residual policy always *sees* what the base intends to do, not just where the robot is. This is why the residual can learn targeted corrections (e.g., "the base is about to insert but is 2 mm left; move right") rather than trying to rediscover the whole task structure from scratch.

Why only the residual gets gradients:

baseactionnormalized() is decorated @torch.nograd() in both ResidualDiffusionPolicy and ResidualMlpPolicy (e.g., residualdiffusion.py line 157). The base inference is additionally wrapped in with torch.nograd(): in the rollout loop. The optimizers only hold references to agent.actorparameters / agent.criticparameters, which are filtered to residualpolicy parameters exclusively. No mechanism in PyTorch's autograd connects loss.backward() to the base model weights.

The corresponding inference-time combination lives in ResidualDiffusionPolicy.action() (lines 111–143 of src/behavior/residual_diffusion.py):


residual = self.residual_policy.get_action(residual_nobs)  # scaled by action_scale inside get_action
naction = base_naction + residual
return self.normalizer(naction, "action", forward=False)

ResidualPolicy.get_action() (src/models/residual.py line 161):


def get_action(self, nobs: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
    return self.actor_mean(nobs) * self.action_scale

So the scale is applied identically at train time (via residualnaction * residualpolicy.actionscale in the PPO loop) and at inference time (via getaction).

What you'd need to run it

Hard requirements:

  • NVIDIA GPU with CUDA support — IsaacGym Preview 4 is CUDA-only; there is no CPU physics backend.
  • Linux (Ubuntu 18.04 / 20.04 tested). IsaacGym does not run on Windows or macOS.
  • The custom FurnitureBench fork (included as a git submodule under furniture-bench/). It must be installed with pip install -e furniture-bench/.
  • IsaacGym Preview 4 (downloaded separately from NVIDIA's developer portal, or from the project's S3 bucket). Install with pip install -e isaacgym/python.
  • Python 3.8 (pinned in the README's conda environment).
  • A Conda environment with the packages in requirements.txt plus pip install -e . for the project itself.
  • WandB account (or --wandb.mode=disabled / debug=true) for tracking and weight storage.
  • Demo data: download from https://iai-robust-rearrangement.s3.us-east-2.amazonaws.com/ using scripts/downloaddata.py, set DATADIR_PROCESSED env var.
  • Pre-trained BC checkpoints are available at the same S3 bucket under checkpoints/bc/<task>/<randomness>/actor_chkpt.pt.

CPU-only verdict: Not runnable. IsaacGym's simulation and rendering pipeline is fully GPU-resident; there is no fallback path. Even a single-environment test would fail at import time without a CUDA device.

GPU memory: With numenvs=1024 (the default), expect 20–40 GB VRAM for oneleg. The repo's SLURM scripts use A100-80GB nodes. A smaller num_envs (e.g., 64 or 128) should work on a 16 GB card at the cost of higher gradient variance.

Read-it-in-this-order

A practitioner who wants to understand the full system should read files in this order:

  • README.md — installation, data download commands, BC + RL launch commands; gives the end-to-end picture in 5 minutes.
  • src/config/baseresidualrl.yaml — every tunable hyperparameter in one place; anchors the reading of the code.
  • src/config/actor/residualdiffusion.yaml — actionscale=0.1, hidden sizes, init logstd; defines the residual policy's architectural footprint.
  • src/models/residual.py — ResidualPolicy class: buildmlp, getactionandvalue, getvalue, getaction; the RL-trained component in isolation.
  • src/behavior/base.py — Actor base class; understand LinearNormalizer, obshorizon, actionhorizon, and the observation preprocessing shared by all policies.
  • src/behavior/diffusion.py — DiffusionPolicy: DDPM/DDIM inference via normalizedaction(); how the base policy produces a chunk of actions for an observation window.
  • src/behavior/residualdiffusion.py — ResidualDiffusionPolicy: how the base and residual are wired together; loadbasestatedict, baseactionnormalized, processobs, action, actorparameters / critic_parameters properties.
  • src/gym/_init.py — getrlenv(): how FurnitureRLSimEnv is instantiated with numenvs, ctrlmode="diffik", actiontype="pos".

  • notebooks/14asresidualvizvideos.ipynb — visualizes the magnitude and direction of residual corrections over a rollout; the best way to build intuition for what the RL head actually learns to do.
