01 · the loop

The self-driving loop, on a hard clock

Autonomous driving is the same sense-decide-act loop as any robot, but under brutal constraints: it runs on a computer bolted in the trunk, it must react before the world moves on, and a mistake can kill.

The loop has five beats — sense (cameras, LiDAR, radar), perceive (what and where), predict (where everything goes next), plan (a safe trajectory), act (steer and brake) — then look again. Every stage lives inside a real-time budget, which is why so much of the field is about doing more with less compute.

the actual math — one level deeper

The loop: oₜ (sensors) → perception → prediction of other agents → plan τ → control uₜ → the world advances → repeat.

Each stage has a latency budget; total loop time must stay under the reaction deadline (often ~100 ms) or the plan is stale on arrival.

02 · BEV

Bird’s-eye view: the invented map

Here is the field’s signature move. A planner reasons about a top-down world — lanes, gaps, distances — but cameras deliver perspective images from all around the car. So you lift those multi-camera features into a single bird’s-eye-view grid: the scene as seen from above.

The key insight is that BEV is not something a camera outputs; it is an invented intermediate representation, chosen because it makes everything downstream easier. Detection, tracking, prediction, and planning can all share one common top-down frame.

the actual math — one level deeper

Lift multi-camera image features to a shared top-down grid: for each BEV cell, gather the image features along its projection ray (depth-splat or attention).

BEV(x,y) = Σ_cameras attend( image-features, ray(x,y) ). Detection, forecasting, and planning then operate on this common grid.

03 · fusion

Fuse sensors, each blind differently

No single sensor is enough, and each fails in its own way. A camera is rich in color and meaning but guesses depth. LiDAR measures exact 3D distance but is colorless and sparse far off. Radar sees velocity directly and pierces rain and fog, but is coarse.

So you fuse them, and the weaknesses cancel: color from the camera, depth from LiDAR, motion-through-weather from radar. Redundancy and diversity are what make perception reliable enough to trust with a life.

the actual math — one level deeper

Fuse modalities by projecting each into the shared BEV/3D frame and combining features: f = fuse( φ_cam, φ_lidar, φ_radar ).

Redundancy means no single sensor failure (glare, rain, sparsity) collapses perception; alignment across modalities is the hard part.

04 · end-to-end

Modular cascade vs end-to-end (vs VLA)

How you wire the loop is the field’s central debate. The modular way builds separate blocks — detect, track, predict, plan — each interpretable, but each handoff throws information away and small errors compound down the chain.

The end-to-end way trains one network from sensors straight to a trajectory: it keeps all the information and often performs better, but it’s a black box you can’t interrogate after a crash. The current frontier — driving VLAs — tries to get both: reason about the scene in language (what’s happening, what’s risky) and act on that reasoning, so the decision is grounded and readable.

the actual math — one level deeper

Modular: p(plan) = plan ∘ predict ∘ track ∘ detect — each block trained separately; errors compound multiplicatively down the chain.

End-to-end: one network πθ(sensors) → trajectory, trained on the final objective — no information lost between stages, but no interpretable intermediates.

VLA: emit a reasoning trace z, then the trajectory: p(z|o)·p(τ|o,z) — readable middle ground.

05 · the long tail

The long tail, and closed-loop truth

The last problem is the hardest. Almost all driving is trivially easy — empty highway, clear lanes — and the risk concentrates in a thin, endless tail of rare events: a sudden cut-in, a jaywalker, road debris, a child chasing a ball. You can never collect enough of them.

Worse, the metric lies. Score a model on logged data (open-loop) and it looks great, because it’s graded on frames where nothing went wrong. The honest test is closed-loop — let the policy actually drive, especially through the tail — which is why simulation, rare-event generation, and closed-loop benchmarks matter so much.

the actual math — one level deeper

Event frequency follows a heavy-tailed distribution: common scenes dominate the data, rare-but-dangerous events populate the tail.

Open-loop metrics average over logged frames (mostly easy) and hide tail failures; closed-loop evaluation compounds errors over time and exposes them.

Coverage of the tail — via simulation, generative rare-event synthesis, and targeted collection — is the binding constraint on safety.

01

BEV perception & detection

both
the problem

A camera delivers a perspective image — objects look smaller far away, and you can't tell depth from a single pixel. But a planner needs a top-down map: how far is that car, which lane is it in, what space is free? The core problem is lifting 2D image pixels from several cameras into a single top-down grid where every cell has known real-world coordinates. That grid is called the bird's-eye view, and it is not something a camera outputs naturally — it has to be constructed by projecting each image feature along its 3D ray into the correct top-down cell.

the approaches

Lift multi-camera (and LiDAR) features into a shared top-down bird’s-eye grid where objects, lanes, and free space all live in one frame the planner can use directly.

The recurring work is doing it from cheap cameras, under bad light, and generalizing across camera rigs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Setup: six surround cameras around a car; BEV grid is 100×100 cells, each 0.5 m × 0.5 m, covering 50 m in every direction.
  2. Feature extraction: each camera image is passed through a backbone (e.g. ResNet-50) producing a 32×32 feature map.
  3. Lift: for each BEV cell at (x, y), compute its 3D world coordinate, project it into each camera image, and bilinearly sample the feature at that pixel location — one feature vector per camera per cell.
  4. Fuse: features from all cameras that can see a cell are averaged (or attended over); occluded cells get only the cameras that face them.
  5. Detect: a detection head runs on the 100×100 BEV feature grid, outputting bounding boxes in top-down coordinates — no 2D-to-3D guessing needed downstream.
  6. Result: BEV-CAR achieves 38.4 mAP on nuScenes val from cameras only; every subsequent planning step operates in the same 50 m square.

    BEV-CAR — context-aware ray rasterization improves monocular BEV segmentation + occlusion handling

    A camera sees the world in perspective: objects look smaller far away, depth is guessed, and lanes twist in distorted perspective. A planner, however, needs a top-down map where every cell has real-world coordinates — how far is that car, which lane is it in, is this space free? BEV-CAR lifts camera features into a shared bird's-eye-view grid, one cell per 0.5 m of real ground, so detection, prediction, and planning can operate in a common frame.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: Six surround cameras feed their images through a backbone (e.g. ResNet-50), producing a dense feature map per camera. For each cell in the top-down BEV grid, the method computes its 3D world coordinate, projects that coordinate back into each camera image to find the pixel location, and bilinearly samples the feature vector at that pixel. Features from all cameras that can see that cell (or see it partially) are aggregated — averaged or attended over — with learned handling of occlusion (cells hidden behind the car's own body, or blocked by nearby objects). A detection head then runs directly on the fused 100×100 BEV feature grid, outputting bounding boxes in top-down coordinates — no 2D-to-3D guessing needed downstream. Context-aware ray rasterization improves the projection: it learns which camera rays are most reliable and weights them accordingly, boosting occlusion handling and ambiguous boundary cases.

  • ROBO
    Retinex-BEVFormer — Retinex enhancement makes multi-view BEV detection robust in low light

    Multi-view BEV detection fails dramatically under low light or glare: cameras deliver muddy, low-contrast images, and features extracted from them become unreliable. Retinex-BEVFormer applies Retinex enhancement to each camera image before feature extraction, decomposing the image into reflectance (surface properties) and illumination, then suppressing the illumination channel to reveal the true surface details hidden by poor lighting.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: The Retinex algorithm factorizes each image into two components: reflectance R (the true color of objects) and illumination I, such that image = R × I. In low light, both R and I are hard to recover because pixels are dim. The method estimates I by smoothing across space (using a guided filter), then divides out I to recover a high-contrast R that reveals lane markings, vehicle edges, and pedestrians even when the input was nearly black. This enhanced image is then passed to the standard BEV backbone and lifting pipeline. The key insight: reflectance is stable across lighting conditions, while illumination is the source of distribution shift. By recovering reflectance, the model's learned features — trained on well-lit data — now work on poorly-lit data, because the underlying scene structure is the same.

  • CVPR
    CoIn3D — configuration-invariant multi-camera 3D detection generalizes across camera setups

    Different car models have camera rigs of different sizes, mounting positions, and focal lengths. A 3D detector trained on one rig (say, six 1280×960 cameras on a Tesla) fails when deployed on a different rig (say, four 640×480 cameras on a truck). CoIn3D learns to detect in 3D in a configuration-invariant way, warping features from different camera setups into a canonical 3D space so the detector sees a consistent 3D layout regardless of which physical cameras provided the data.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: Instead of projecting all cameras into a single bird's-eye grid (which assumes a fixed camera rig), CoIn3D works in 3D space directly. Each camera image is backprojected into 3D using its intrinsic and extrinsic matrices. Features from all cameras are lifted into a canonical 3D feature volume (a voxel grid, or a continuous 3D point cloud representation), where the relative camera positions are absorbed into the projection matrices. A 3D detection head then operates on this canonical volume. Because the final detection operates on 3D geometry, not 2D pixel coordinates, the same detector head works for any camera rig — the difference between rigs is only in how features enter the 3D volume, not in what the detector does once they are there. This factorization lets a single model generalize to new configurations without retraining.

  • ROBO
    Dur360BEV — single-camera 360° BEV, dropping the complex multi-camera rig

    Multi-camera BEV detection requires complex intrinsic/extrinsic calibration and synchronization across six cameras; it is expensive to integrate and brittle on recalibration. Dur360BEV constructs a full 360° bird's-eye-view from a single monocular camera, using deep features and geometric priors to infer the scene structure and object positions from one image alone, eliminating the multi-camera rig entirely.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: A single camera delivers a perspective image with a fixed field of view (e.g. 70°), covering only what is in front. Dur360BEV uses a depth-prediction network to estimate per-pixel depth, then transforms each pixel into 3D world coordinates using the camera's intrinsic matrix. The resulting sparse 3D point set is projected onto the BEV grid to produce a 360° overhead view from one perspective. To handle the unobserved regions (behind, to the sides far away), the method trains a context encoder that reads the visible BEV region and predicts features for unseen areas using semantic priors (e.g., 'there are likely lanes ahead because the car is on a road'). The key operation: depth-to-3D lifting + projection + learned hallucination of occluded regions. This is cheaper to deploy (one camera, one LiDAR mount point removed) but trades accuracy for simplicity — the hallucinated regions are probabilistic estimates, not true geometry.

3D occupancy & 4D forecasting

both
the problem

A bounding box says a car is 4.5 m × 1.8 m at this location. But a mattress fallen on the highway has no standard size. A child half-hidden by a parked car fits no box template. The core problem is representing what space is occupied without assuming shape. The answer is a 3D voxel grid: divide the world into small cubes, predict which are filled and with what. Then forecast that grid forward in time so the planner knows not just where things are but where they will be — a particularly critical gap for planning safe merges.

the approaches

Instead of boxes, predict a dense grid of which 3D cells are filled — and forecast that grid into the future, separating static structure from moving agents.

Occupancy captures odd shapes and unknown objects that box detectors miss, and forecasting it gives the planner foresight.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Setup: voxel grid is 200×200×16 cells at 0.4 m resolution — 200×200 covers 80 m × 80 m around the car, 16 layers cover 0–6.4 m height.
  2. Input: six camera images; no LiDAR (ShelfOcc uses a foundation model for supervision instead).
  3. Occupancy prediction: a BEV encoder lifts camera features to the 3D grid; each voxel gets a class probability — empty, vehicle, pedestrian, bicycle, static.
  4. 4D step: a recurrent head reads the occupancy grid at t=0 and outputs predicted grids at t+0.5 s, t+1.0 s, t+1.5 s; each cell gets a predicted class and a flow vector showing where it moves.
  5. Planner input: the t+1.0 s predicted grid flags any voxel on the ego's intended path as occupied → planner brakes or re-routes.
  6. Result: ShelfOcc scores 39.8 RayIoU on nuScenes without any LiDAR at train time — better than earlier methods that used it.
  • CVPR
    ShelfOcc — foundation-model 3D supervision for camera-only occupancy, no LiDAR needed

    A bounding box says a car is exactly 4.5 m × 1.8 m, but a fallen mattress has no standard size, and a child half-hidden behind a parked car fits no template. ShelfOcc predicts a dense 3D occupancy grid — which voxels are filled — without assuming any shape, and crucially does so from cameras alone by using a foundation model to generate dense 3D supervision, cutting the need for expensive LiDAR annotation.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: A 3D voxel grid discretizes space into small cubes (0.4 m × 0.4 m × 0.4 m in a 200×200×16 volume, covering 80 m × 80 m and 0 to 6.4 m height). Six camera images are passed through a backbone to extract features, then lifted to the 3D grid via the same BEV projection technique (ray-based attention or depth-splatting). Each voxel is classified: empty, vehicle, pedestrian, bicycle, or static (walls, trees). To learn from camera-only data without 3D annotations, ShelfOcc uses a pre-trained foundation model (e.g., a large vision-language model or a 3D scene understanding model) to generate weak 3D labels from the images — the model 'imagines' what the 3D world must look like given the camera views. These weak labels supervise the occupancy head, letting it learn without any expensive human 3D annotation. This approach captures odd shapes (fallen debris, partially-occluded pedestrians) that box detectors miss.

  • CVPR
    Gau-Occ — geometry-completed Gaussians for smooth multi-modal 3D occupancy

    Occupancy grids are inherently discrete: a voxel is either filled or empty. But the edge of an object is blurry in camera images and should be blurry in the 3D occupancy estimate too — a hard grid boundary is both physically wrong and hard to optimize. Gau-Occ represents occupancy as a set of 3D Gaussians, whose overlapping covariances naturally model multi-modal, soft occupancy, and completes geometric structure that cameras alone cannot see.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: Instead of a binary per-voxel occupancy, Gau-Occ places a set of 3D Gaussians (each defined by mean position and covariance) to represent the occupied region. Each Gaussian covers a small volume, and overlapping Gaussians produce soft occupancy probabilities at any 3D point — the probability that a ray from a camera will hit an object smoothly transitions from 1 (inside an object) to 0 (in free space). A neural network predicts the positions, covariances, and occupancy confidences of these Gaussians given camera images and geometric constraints. The key operation: differentiable rendering of Gaussian occupancy back to camera images. The predicted Gaussians are rendered as occupancy observations per camera, and those renderings are compared to features extracted from the actual camera images. Gradients flow backward to refine Gaussian positions and covariances until the rendered views match the camera views. This forces the Gaussians to explain the 3D geometry consistent with all camera observations, filling in occluded regions coherently.

  • CVPR
    GenieDrive — 4D-occupancy-guided video generation; models how actions change occupancy

    Once the planner decides to accelerate, turn, or brake, the occupancy grid in front will change. A typical predictor only forecasts static futures, but the car's actions affect what the car will see. GenieDrive generates 4D occupancy (3D grid over time) conditioned on the car's planned action sequence, creating a mental simulation of how the predicted future world depends on what the car does.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: Given the current 3D occupancy grid at time t=0 and a candidate trajectory (a sequence of steering angles and accelerations over 5 seconds), a generative model produces predicted occupancy grids at future timesteps t+0.5s, t+1s, etc. The generation is conditioned on two things: (1) the initial occupancy, which encodes what is present now, and (2) the action sequence, which acts as a control input. The model learns to predict how static elements (parked cars, lanes) persist and how dynamic elements (pedestrians, other vehicles) move in response to their own policies. The actions matter because they affect the ego car's viewpoint — turning left reveals new areas, accelerating brings distant objects closer — and also affect predictions of how other agents react (if ego accelerates, a following car might accelerate too). The key innovation: actions as conditioning variables in a video-generation model. The planner can now sample several action sequences, generate the resulting predicted futures, and pick the action that leads to the safest predicted world.

  • CVPR
    QueryOcc — continuous 4D query-based self-supervision for semantic occupancy

    Occupancy labels for 3D voxel grids are expensive to annotate — a human must label a cube in 3D space. QueryOcc avoids this by using self-supervision: it forces the current frame's occupancy prediction to be consistent with observed sensor data (cameras, LiDAR), without human labels.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: The method operates on pairs of camera observations at time t and t+δt (e.g., 0.5 seconds apart). At time t, a network predicts occupancy for the voxel grid. At time t+δt, the same voxel grid is projected into the new camera views using the vehicle's odometry (how much it has moved). Now, voxels that were predicted as occupied at t should still be occupied at t+δt (with some allowance for tracked objects that move). The self-supervised loss: consistency between occupancy predictions across frames under geometric transformation. If a voxel was predicted occupied and visible in both frames, its feature should be consistent. If it was occupied in t but now occluded by another object in t+δt, the loss ignores it. This forces the model to learn coherent 3D occupancy that is stable over time, without any manual 3D bounding-box or occupancy annotations. The term 'semantic' comes from the optional use of 2D segmentation masks (road, vehicle, pedestrian) to guide which voxels should be which class.

  • ROBO
    OCCUQ — efficient uncertainty on occupancy so planning can be risk-aware

    An occupancy prediction is never certain. A voxel might be filled with a pedestrian (high confidence) or might be empty (medium confidence). A planner that ignores this uncertainty risks colliding with a high-confidence prediction that turns out to be wrong. OCCUQ quantifies uncertainty on each voxel's occupancy, letting the planner make risk-aware decisions — staying conservative around uncertain obstacles.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: Instead of outputting a single occupancy probability per voxel, OCCUQ outputs both a mean occupancy and a variance (uncertainty). The mean comes from the standard occupancy prediction; the variance is learned from data by predicting aleatoric uncertainty (inherent noise in the world, e.g., a flickering reflection) and epistemic uncertainty (model confusion from limited training data). The key operation: Bayesian prediction with learned uncertainty. The planner can then compute a risk-aware cost: a voxel with mean occupancy 0.3 and high uncertainty (variance 0.4) might be treated more conservatively than a voxel with mean 0.3 and low uncertainty (variance 0.05), because the uncertain one could easily be fully occupied. Planning then becomes a constrained optimization: choose the trajectory that stays below a per-decision collision risk budget, trading off agility (staying in lane) and safety (avoiding uncertain obstacles).

3D detection & multi-object tracking

robotics
the problem

Detecting objects in one frame is not enough: the planner needs to know which car from the last frame is the same car now, how fast it is moving, and whether it is getting closer. The core problem is association — matching detections across frames so each agent keeps a continuous identity over time. This sounds easy until occlusion, crowding, or sudden maneuvers break the standard assumption that things move at constant velocity. A mistaken swap of identities makes a decelerating car look like an approaching one.

the approaches

Find every vehicle, pedestrian, and cyclist and keep a consistent identity for each across time, through clutter, occlusion, and irregular motion.

The hard parts are long-range detection, cross-modal resolution gaps, and association when motion breaks the usual assumptions.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Frame 1: LiDAR point cloud processed by a 3D detector → three bounding boxes: car A at (12 m, 0 m), car B at (8 m, 2 m), pedestrian C at (5 m, -1 m). Each gets a new track ID.
  2. Frame 2 detection: three new boxes appear — car at (11.2 m, 0 m), car at (7.6 m, 2.1 m), pedestrian at (4.8 m, -0.9 m). No IDs yet.
  3. Association: compute 3D IoU between frame-1 and frame-2 boxes; Hungarian matching assigns frame-2 car to ID A (IoU 0.72), frame-2 car to ID B (IoU 0.68), frame-2 pedestrian to ID C (IoU 0.61).
  4. Track state update: Kalman filter updates each track's position and velocity — car A velocity estimated at 2.2 m/s away; car B at 1.1 m/s lateral.
  5. New object: a fourth detection in frame 2 has no match (IoU < 0.3) → assigned new ID D; kept tentative until confirmed in frame 3.
  6. Planner output: IDs A and B are flagged as moving away; ID D as unknown — planner stays conservative until D is confirmed.
  • ROBO
    ConTrack3D — contrastive association stays robust when constant-velocity assumptions break

    Matching detections across frames (tracking) assumes objects move smoothly with roughly constant velocity. But a car turning a sharp corner breaks this assumption dramatically: its velocity vector rotates 90° in one second. A simple Euclidean distance or Kalman filter match fails, creating a track swap. ConTrack3D uses contrastive learning to match detections reliably even when motion is irregular and highly non-linear.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: A contrastive loss works by pushing similar things together and pulling dissimilar things apart in an embedding space. ConTrack3D embeds each detection (from frame t) into a learned feature space, and separately embeds detections from frame t+1. The embedding captures what the object looks like (appearance), where it is (3D position), and how it is moving (velocity and acceleration), compressed into a dense vector. For a ground-truth match (the same car in frame t and t+1), the embeddings should be close; for wrong matches, they should be far. The loss: 1 / (1 + distance of correct match) + log(1 + exp(distance of wrong match)) — minimize correct, maximize incorrect. Once trained, at inference time, for each frame-t detection, the closest frame-t+1 detection in embedding space is the likely match — even if the motion is highly non-constant, because the learned embedding has seen thousands of non-linear motions during training and learned to cluster them sensibly.

  • ROBO
    Stands on Shoulders of Giants — lifts abundant 2D detections to 3D to cut expensive LiDAR labeling

    LiDAR 3D bounding-box annotations are expensive — a human must carefully label each vehicle's 3D position and size. But 2D bounding boxes (on images) are 100× cheaper and abundant. Stands on Shoulders of Giants lifts abundant 2D detections to 3D by using depth estimates from monocular images, drastically cutting the need for expensive LiDAR annotation.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: A modern 2D detector (trained on ImageNet or COCO scale) outputs tight 2D bounding boxes in image space for every vehicle and pedestrian. Separately, a monocular depth-prediction network (trained on camera-only video) outputs a depth value per pixel. Within each 2D box, the method samples the predicted depths and fits a 3D bounding box by assuming standard object sizes (cars are ~4 m long, pedestrians ~1.8 m tall) and solving for 3D position and orientation that is consistent with the depth samples inside the box. The key operation: pseudo-label generation from 2D + depth. These pseudo-labeled 3D boxes, generated automatically from cheap 2D labels, train a 3D detector to output real 3D boxes directly from LiDAR or camera features. A small amount of expensive hand-annotated 3D boxes (say 10% of the training set) provides ground truth to fine-tune and correct systematic errors in the pseudo-labels. This hybrid approach cuts annotation cost from massive to manageable while retaining reasonable 3D detection accuracy.

  • ROBO
    SaViD — adaptive camera-LiDAR fusion bridges the resolution gap at long range

    LiDAR sees nearby objects in high resolution (dense 3D points) but thins out fast beyond 50 m, where detection becomes sparse and unreliable. Cameras see far objects clearly (high-res pixels at 100+ m) but cannot measure depth directly. SaViD fuses camera and LiDAR adaptively, using camera features at long range where LiDAR is sparse, and LiDAR depth at close range where it is dense, bridging the resolution gap.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: Both modalities are projected into a shared BEV grid. The LiDAR point cloud, sparse in the far field, produces a depth map with gaps — some cells have LiDAR depth, others are empty. The camera images provide dense features at all ranges. For each BEV cell, compute a 'confidence' in the LiDAR depth based on point density: high confidence if many points hit the cell, low confidence if few. The fusion operation: weighted average confidence × LiDAR_depth + (1 - confidence) × camera_depth. Cells with strong LiDAR returns trust LiDAR depth; cells with weak or no LiDAR returns fall back to camera depth estimates derived from stereo or a monocular depth network. The planner then has a complete, high-confidence depth map at all ranges. The adaptive weighting ensures that the strengths of each sensor (LiDAR's true 3D, camera's long-range clarity) combine coherently, with no artificial gaps or trust boundaries.

  • ROBO
    LGDD — native 4D-radar detection with noise-robust proposal filtering

    4D radar (range, azimuth, elevation, and Doppler velocity) is underutilized in autonomous driving — most stacks focus on LiDAR and cameras. Yet radar directly measures moving targets' velocities and cuts through heavy rain and fog. LGDD applies 4D radar to 3D detection natively, with noise-reliable proposal filtering to handle radar's low angular resolution.

    how it actually works

    The machinery: 4D radar outputs a sparse set of detections, each with position (x, y, z), velocity (vx, vy, vz), and confidence. Unlike LiDAR's dense point cloud, radar gives only ~100 detections per sweep (sparse), and the angular resolution is coarse — two nearby vehicles can appear as one radar blob. LGDD feeds these 4D radar detections into a 3D object detector as proposals (candidate regions to refine). The proposals start coarse (each radar detection gets a large 3D region of uncertainty), and a refinement head uses a backbone of LiDAR or camera features to sharpen the proposal into a tight box. The key innovation: noise-reliable filtering that removes spurious radar detections without hand-tuned thresholds. The method learns a scoring function that takes a radar detection and its supporting features (e.g., is there a LiDAR point nearby? does camera show an object here?) and outputs whether the detection is likely real or noise. This learnable gating replaces brittle rules like 'keep detections with confidence > 0.7'.

04

Motion & trajectory forecasting

both
the problem

A car turning left and a car going straight look identical for the first half-second — same position, same speed. Waiting for certainty is too late. The core problem is that every agent's future is genuinely multimodal: at an intersection a vehicle might go straight (probability 0.55), turn left (0.30), or turn right (0.15). If the forecaster outputs a single averaged path, it lands in the middle of none of those — the worst possible prediction. The planner must receive the full distribution of futures, weighted by probability, so it can plan conservatively around the dangerous possibilities even if they are unlikely.

the approaches

End-to-end planning

both
the problem

The modular stack — detect, then track, then predict, then plan — throws information away at each handoff. The detector collapses a rich image feature into a bounding box. the approaches

Planning, MPC & safety-critical control

robotics
the problem

Given a perception output and predicted agent futures, the planner must produce a trajectory the car can physically follow, that avoids all obstacles, stays in the lane, and arrives at the goal. This sounds like search — enumerate candidate trajectories and pick the best. The catch is that 'best' must also mean 'safe': a trajectory that clips a lane boundary at 3% probability is not a small matter when 3% × 10 decisions/minute × 8 hours/day = 144 near-misses per day. Model Predictive Control formalizes this: project the car's dynamics forward in time, evaluate a cost that rewards progress and penalizes constraint violations, and solve for the trajectory that minimizes cost under hard constraints.

the approaches

  4. Constraint check: 'Guaranteed Reach-Avoid' neural reachability certifies 8 of 30 trajectories as provably collision-free under agent uncertainty; remaining 22 are pruned.
  5. Risk budget: 'Online Risk-Bounded Planning' allocates a per-decision risk budget of 0.01; the selected trajectory has a collision probability of 0.008 — within budget.

Closed-loop simulation & benchmarks

both
the problem

The standard way to score a driving model is to replay a log — play back a recorded drive, ask the model what it would have done, and compare to the human. This is called open-loop evaluation. The problem is that it grades the model only on situations where nothing went wrong, because the log was recorded by a human who survived. If the model makes a small error — brakes slightly late — the log keeps going without consequence, so the score looks fine. Real driving is closed-loop: errors accumulate, and a 2% error at step 1 puts the car in a new position that makes step 2 harder. The field needs simulators where the policy actually drives and agents react to it.

Because open-loop scores on logged data flatter models, the field builds closed-loop simulators and benchmarks (NAVSIM, nuPlan) where the policy actually drives and its errors compound.

A model trained on a million hours of driving in sunny cities will encounter exactly one unusual situation it has never seen: a car fire blocking the road with emergency vehicles approaching from three sides. In that moment its perception might still output plausible-looking detections — because it was trained to always output something — while having no reliable signal from the out-of-distribution input. The car that doesn't know it's confused is more dangerous than the car that stops. The core problem is detecting when the system is outside its trained competence, so it can either trigger a fallback (slow down, hand off to a human) or synthesize training data for the gap.

the approaches

Catch perception failures and out-of-distribution situations at run time, and synthesize the rare edge cases needed to test coverage — the difference between a system that fails loudly and one that fails silently.

Foundation models are increasingly used both to monitor and to generate the hard cases.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Normal driving: SPARQ monitors the Q-value of the planner's action head alongside a perception-failure score. Q-value for current action: 0.87 (high confidence). Perception score: 0.12 (low failure likelihood).
  2. Unseen scene arrives: emergency vehicles with flashing lights; detector's feature uncertainty (measured by Monte Carlo dropout, 10 samples) spikes from 0.08 to 0.61.
  3. SPARQ alert: Q-network sees perception-failure score 0.61 × plan-safety score 0.34 → joint risk 0.87 → threshold 0.6 exceeded. Alert raised.
  4. Fallback: car decelerates to 20 km/h and turns on hazard lights; human override requested.
  5. OOD synthesis (offline): 'Generating OOD Scenarios Using LLMs' uses the failure description to branch-synthesize 200 new sim scenarios with emergency vehicles; model retrained on those cases.
  6. Outcome: SPARQ achieves 90% recall on perception failures in the SPARQ benchmark — 23 points above a standalone rule-based monitor — without increasing false-alert rate.

    System-Level Safety Monitoring and Recovery (SPARQ) — a Q-network jointly reasons over perception failures + plan safety, 90% recall

    A self-driving car can fail in two ways: perception mistakes (missing a pedestrian) and planning mistakes (choosing an unsafe trajectory). Existing safety monitors check only perception. This work trains a Q-network that jointly reasons over perception failure scores and plan safety, detecting the compounded risk when either component is weak. SPARQ achieves 90% failure recall by considering the system holistically.

    how it actually works

    Problem: Monitoring perception alone misses scenarios where perception is correct but planning is wrong, and vice versa.

    Mechanism: A Q-network is trained to estimate the probability of imminent collision. Its input is a multi-modal state: (1) perception-uncertainty features (confidence scores for detected objects, scene segmentation), (2) plan-safety features (planned trajectory, nearby obstacles, predicted collision time). The network learns to weight these inputs—high perception uncertainty makes even a conservative plan risky, while perfect perception allows more aggressive plans. The output is a safety score. A threshold on this score triggers intervention (alert, takeover, or automatic maneuver).

    Why it works: Multiplicative effects appear when perception and planning both degrade—a 70% confident detection in a 70% confident plan compounds to ~50% actual safety. The Q-network learns these interactions. Training on naturalistic failure data ensures the network recognizes real failure modes. The 90% recall means the system catches most failures before impact, trading false alarms for safety.

    Generating OOD Scenarios Using LLMs — LLM branching-tree synthesis of out-of-distribution test scenarios

    Testing self-driving cars on rare, dangerous scenarios is crucial, but most test suites are unbalanced—common scenarios dominate, rare edge cases are missed. This work uses an LLM to generate a tree of scenario variations, branching on rarity and difficulty, producing out-of-distribution test cases that challenge the system. LLM-driven scenario generation finds edge cases without manual curation.

    how it actually works

    Problem: Manually creating rare scenarios is tedious; uniform random generation misses interesting out-of-distribution cases.

    Mechanism: An LLM is given a base scenario and instructions to branch. For example, base: "Car stops at a red light." The LLM expands: "Branch A: pedestrian jaywalks; Branch B: traffic light flickers; Branch C: preceding car reverses." Each branch is further elaborated. A tree forms, with leaves representing specific scenarios. The LLM is guided to prefer branches that represent out-of-distribution situations (low frequency in the training data) or high difficulty (large deviations from nominal behavior). Scenario specifications are generated for each leaf and executed in simulation.

    Why it works: LLMs are trained on human-written text, so they encode human intuitions about what scenarios are realistic and interesting. Branching explores the space of variations systematically. Guidance toward OOD cases ensures the test suite includes edge cases the learned system has not seen. The tree structure makes dependencies clear—if variant A fails, the human can try variant B to isolate the cause. The result is a richer, more challenging test set than hand-authored suites.

    Validity Learning on Failures — an annotation-free failure objective mitigates planning distribution shift

    A learned planner trained on expert data works well when conditions match training, but when the world changes (e.g., roads become icy), the planner fails due to distribution shift. This work trains a validity predictor that learns which situations the planner will fail on, without needing labeled failure data. By learning an annotation-free failure objective, the planner avoids distribution shift automatically.

    how it actually works

    Problem: Distribution shift between training and deployment causes planner failures, but labeling new failures for retraining is expensive.

    Mechanism: A validity model is trained alongside the planner to output a confidence score for each planned trajectory. During offline training, the validity model learns from success examples in the expert data—high score when the plan matches expert behavior, low score otherwise. This is done without explicit failure labels. At deployment, a trajectory is executed only if validity is high. When validity is low, the planner is queried again or defaults to conservative behavior. The validity model is updated occasionally on new deployment data, learning to recognize situations where the planner's distribution has shifted.

    Why it works: The validity model learns that expert behavior is valid, inferring implicitly that non-expert behavior is not. At deployment, it flags trajectories that deviate from learned validity patterns, which often signals distribution shift. By refusing invalid plans, the system avoids compounding errors. Occasional updates on deployment data let the validity model adapt to new environments without retraining the full planner. The annotation-free approach is practical—no expert labels of failures are needed.

    Foundation Models for Rapid Autonomy Validation — generative coverage cuts the sim-miles to find rare events

    Finding rare failure cases through simulation requires millions of miles of simulated driving, which is expensive. This work uses a generative foundation model to synthesize diverse scenarios and concentrate sim-miles on rare, hard-to-find edge cases. Coverage-aware generation cuts the simulation budget by orders of magnitude.

    how it actually works

    Problem: Random scenario sampling requires billions of simulated miles to find one rare failure; sim-time is the bottleneck.

    Mechanism: A foundation model (e.g., a diffusion model trained on real driving data) generates scenario variations: object positions, velocities, weather, lighting. The model is guided by a coverage objective—the system tracks which regions of scenario-space have been explored (e.g., via clustering in scenario embeddings). The model generates scenarios in unexplored regions. Each generated scenario is run in closed-loop; failures are recorded. As coverage increases, generated scenarios become increasingly rare and difficult, concentrating compute on the hard tail of the distribution.

    Why it works: Foundation models capture the manifold of plausible scenarios learned from real data, avoiding generating nonsensical scenarios that waste sim-time. Coverage guidance ensures the search is systematic, not random. By prioritizing rare cases, the system finds failures faster. The trade-off is clear: spend human effort fine-tuning the generative model, save massive sim-time on random sampling. This inverts the usual cost structure—generation is cheap, simulation is expensive.

Efficient onboard perception & data

robotics
the problem

The model that tops the nuScenes leaderboard runs on a server with eight GPUs and takes 200 ms per frame. The car's onboard computer is a 15W embedded chip with a single GPU and a 100 ms deadline — total, for the entire loop, including actuation. Accuracy that misses the deadline is worse than useless: a stale perception output fed to the planner gives a false sense of safety. The core problem is closing the gap between what's achievable at research scale and what fits the onboard budget — in compute, memory, and power — while retaining enough accuracy that the car remains safe.

the approaches

Squeeze perception into the car’s real-time, power-limited computer — via compression, distillation, and label-efficient learning — and curate which data is even worth training on.

Accuracy that misses the latency budget or the annotation budget doesn’t ship.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Starting point: BEVFormer-Large, 69M parameters, 210 ms/frame on an NVIDIA Orin (the target chip). Loop budget: 100 ms. Gap: 110 ms.
  2. Distillation: KDMOS-style knowledge distillation trains a 9M-parameter student to mimic the teacher's BEV feature maps — not just the output boxes — using L2 loss on intermediate features.
  3. Compression: 'Real-Time LiDAR Compression' encodes the point cloud at 60:1 before transmission to the perception module — reduces memory bandwidth by 40%.
  4. Label efficiency: 'Label-Efficient LiDAR Panoptic Segmentation' lifts sparse 2D pseudo-labels to 3D, cutting the annotation budget from 50,000 to 3,200 hand-labeled frames while retaining +10.6 PQ.
  5. Result: student runs at 34 ms/frame on Orin (67% of budget), mAP 41.3 vs teacher's 47.8 — a 6.5-point cost for a 6× speed gain.
  6. Data selection: 'Scaling-Aware Data Selection' ranks the remaining training corpus by per-sample utility; keeping the top 30% of samples by utility achieves 98% of full-corpus performance — saving 3 weeks of training compute.
  • ROBO
    Real-Time LiDAR Point Cloud Compression — 40–80× compression on resource-constrained robots, task accuracy kept

    LiDAR sensors generate massive point clouds that overwhelm onboard compute and communication bandwidth on resource-constrained robots. This work applies learned compression that reduces point cloud size by 40–80 times while preserving accuracy on downstream perception tasks. Compression shrinks data, not information.

    how it actually works

    Problem: LiDAR point clouds are high-dimensional and bandwidth-intensive; transmitting or storing them in full is impractical.

    Mechanism: A learned compressor network encodes the point cloud into a compact latent representation, then decodes it back. The encoder removes spatial redundancy—nearby points are merged, distant points are subsampled according to learned importance. The decoder upsamples and refines the point cloud using learned geometric priors. Training uses two losses: reconstruction loss (decoded cloud should look like input) and task loss (a downstream detector trained on decoded clouds should perform as well as on original clouds). The trade-off between reconstruction fidelity and task-relevant fidelity is learned.

    Why it works: Reconstruction-only compression often removes information needed for perception. Task-aware training biases compression to preserve features (edges, plane normals) that matter for detection. The network learns that distant clutter is less informative than nearby structures, achieving spatial non-uniformity. Learned compression outperforms fixed subsampling or geometric quantization because it adapts to the specific task. At 40–80 times compression, data fits in constrained memory and communication pipelines.

  • ROBO
    KDMOS — knowledge distillation for motion segmentation, 7.69× fewer parameters

    Segmenting which parts of a LiDAR scene are moving (motion segmentation) is essential for planning, but the networks that do this are large and slow on robots with limited memory. This work applies knowledge distillation, training a tiny student network to mimic a large teacher, cutting parameters by 7.69 times. Distillation shrinks motion segmentation without accuracy loss.

    how it actually works

    Problem: State-of-the-art motion segmentation networks are too large for embedded robots.

    Mechanism: A large teacher network is pre-trained to high accuracy on motion segmentation (e.g., distinguishing moving cars from static road). A small student network is then trained with a combined loss: (1) supervised loss on ground truth labels (standard cross-entropy), (2) distillation loss that penalizes divergence from the teacher's predictions. The teacher's soft predictions (probabilities before argmax) are much more informative than hard labels; the student learns to match the teacher's belief distribution. During deployment, only the student is used.

    Why it works: The teacher has learned rich feature hierarchies that capture motion patterns. The distillation loss transfers that knowledge to the student without requiring the student to relearn from raw pixels. The student learns at a higher level of abstraction (imitate the teacher) than it would from labels alone. Parameter reduction comes from removing channels, layers, or both; the student is optimized for efficiency. At 7.69× fewer parameters, the student still achieves high accuracy because it is guided by the teacher's knowledge.

    Label-Efficient LiDAR Panoptic Segmentation — lifts sparse 2D pseudo-labels to 3D, +10.6 PQ with minimal annotation

    Fully annotating LiDAR point clouds in 3D is expensive and slow. This work uses sparse 2D pseudo-labels from images (e.g., a few clicks per frame) to automatically lift 3D supervision, training a panoptic segmenter with minimal annotation. The method achieves +10.6 points of quality improvement with a tiny fraction of labeled data.

    how it actually works

    Problem: 3D panoptic segmentation (instance + semantic segmentation) requires dense 3D labels; manual annotation is prohibitive.

    Mechanism: A human labels sparse 2D regions in camera images (e.g., the car instance is here, the road is there). These sparse 2D labels are reprojected to 3D using the LiDAR-camera calibration and depth estimates. The 3D projection is sparse and noisy. A learning algorithm then propagates these sparse 3D labels to the full point cloud using geometric and temporal consistency. Consecutive frames are aligned; points moving together are likely the same instance. A graph-based method or weak supervision refines the sparse labels into full 3D panoptic annotations.

    Why it works: 2D annotation is much faster than 3D—humans can label an image in seconds. Reprojection connects 2D evidence to 3D geometry. Propagation exploits the structure of driving scenes—the road stays the road, instances move coherently. Weak supervision (geometric constraints, temporal consistency) acts as a form of semi-supervised learning, turning sparse labels into dense ones. The +10.6 PQ gain comes from this combination: seed labels are quick, propagation is automatic, consistency constraints are free (encoded in the data).

  • CVPR
    Scaling-Aware Data Selection for E2E Driving — selects high-utility samples balancing scale and quality

    End-to-end learned driving systems improve with more data, but collecting and storing all available data is wasteful—some samples teach more than others. This work builds a selection algorithm that identifies high-utility samples balancing both scale diversity (coverage of driving scenarios) and quality (accuracy of labels). Intelligent selection cuts data requirements by training on fewer, better samples.

    how it actually works

    Problem: Collecting all available driving data is expensive; training on a random subset wastes compute on low-value samples.

    Mechanism: The system maintains a candidate pool of unlabeled or weakly-labeled samples. A scoring function evaluates each sample on two axes: (1) scale score—how different is this sample from the training set? (measured via embedding distance or diversity metrics), (2) quality score—how accurate and reliable is the label? (measured via label confidence, inter-annotator agreement, or consistency checks). High-value samples score well on both dimensions. A selection algorithm greedily picks samples that maximize a weighted combination of scale and quality. Selected samples are added to the training set; the model is retrained.

    Why it works: Random sampling catches everything equally; selective sampling focuses on the most informative examples. Scale awareness ensures the dataset covers diverse driving scenarios—corner cases, different weather, different road types. Quality awareness avoids learning from mislabeled or ambiguous samples. The weighted combination allows tuning whether to prioritize breadth or precision. Iterative selection lets the algorithm adapt as the model improves—early on, scale matters more; later, quality refinement matters more. The result is a parsimonious dataset that trains better than a larger random dataset.

Part IV

Where it stands

Driving is the highest-stakes application of the whole loop. What is genuinely still hard:

The long tail. Rare, high-stakes events are endless and under-represented; collecting or even simulating enough of them is unsolved.
Open-loop metrics lie. A model can top logged-data leaderboards and still drive badly; closed-loop, reactive evaluation is essential and still maturing.
Accuracy vs the clock. The best perception often can’t fit the onboard real-time budget; efficiency is a first-class constraint, not an afterthought.
Interpretability vs performance. End-to-end wins on performance but resists the explanations safety and liability demand; VLAs help but are slow.
Sim-to-real & weather. Simulators miss friction, tire slip, and sensor noise; rain/snow/glare still degrade perception sharply.
Certifying safety. Provable guarantees exist for narrow cases; certifying a learned, end-to-end driver at scale remains open.
