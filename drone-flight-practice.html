Delivery drones — the real problem

A quadrotor has four spinning propellers and six degrees of freedom to control, so every stage — from sensing which way is up to threading a smooth, motor-feasible path — is a fight against underactuation, drift, and lag.

1

Know which way is up — attitude estimation

Our demo: fusing a drifting gyro with a noisy accelerometer, the complementary filter cuts attitude RMSE to 0.024 rad, versus 0.38 rad for gyro-only integration.

Who actually hits it

Every autopilot needs this: open-source flight stacks PX4 and ArduPilot both estimate attitude before anything else, and each ships a lightweight quaternion complementary filter alongside their full EKF (PX4's EKF2, ArduPilot's EKF3).

What it costs when it fails

A raw gyro's small bias integrates into unbounded attitude drift within seconds, and a bare accelerometer reads every bump as a false tilt; trusting either alone leaves the controller thinking level is level when it isn't, which ends in loss of control.

How it's solved in production

The complementary filter high-passes the gyro (smooth, but drifting) and low-passes the accelerometer's gravity direction (noisy, but drift-free) so each covers the other's weakness; production stacks upgrade this to an Extended Kalman Filter that also fuses GPS, magnetometer, and barometer to reject bad measurements.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses clean simulated noise; real IMUs add vibration from the motors, temperature-dependent bias, and magnetic interference, and outdoors the accelerometer's 'gravity' reference is corrupted by the drone's own acceleration during maneuvers.

2

Plan a flyable path — trajectory generation

Our demo: a smooth spline through the waypoints holds velocity jumps to 0.006 m/s at each corner, while straight line-segments jump 2.5 m/s — a demand for near-infinite acceleration.

Who actually hits it

Trajectory generators feed the controller a reference to follow; the field-defining approach is the minimum-snap method of Mellinger and Kumar (ICRA 2011), still the reference technique for planning aggressive quadrotor flight.

What it costs when it fails

Routing through waypoints with straight segments jumps the velocity at every corner, which demands infinite acceleration no motor can deliver; the drone can't actually track such a path and either overshoots the corner or stalls out.

How it's solved in production

Fit smooth polynomial trajectories that minimize snap (the fourth derivative of position); because a quadrotor's position, velocity, acceleration, jerk, and snap map directly to feasible thrust and body rates, a snap-smooth path is one the underactuated drone can physically fly.

Toy demo → real system

Our spline just smooths corners; a real planner must also respect thrust and motor limits, dodge obstacles and no-fly zones, and re-plan online as wind, other aircraft, and sensor updates change what's flyable.

3

Tilt to move — the underactuated quad

Our demo: to reach a 5 m sideways goal the drone must tilt up to 31.5 degrees and lets thrust push it there (final 4.94 m); commanded to stay perfectly flat it goes nowhere (0.0 m).

Who actually hits it

This is the defining constraint of every multirotor: four upward-pointing propellers give only four control inputs for six degrees of freedom, so position and attitude are physically coupled — you cannot command them independently.

What it costs when it fails

A quadrotor is underactuated — thrust only ever points along the body's up-axis, so a drone held flat can hover but can never translate sideways; getting the tilt-then-thrust sequence wrong means the drone drifts off course or, over-tilted, loses the lift that keeps it airborne.

How it's solved in production

Cascaded control: an outer position loop computes the acceleration the drone needs, converts it into the tilt (roll and pitch setpoints) that points thrust the right way, and an inner attitude loop drives the body to that tilt — the standard architecture in PX4 and ArduPilot.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo commands a clean tilt in a vacuum; real flight adds aerodynamic drag, rotor wash, and gusts that push back on the tilt, plus a slung payload whose swing shifts the center of mass and fights the very attitude the controller is trying to hold.

4

Mix the motors — control allocation

Our demo: the mixer matrix turns a commanded 0.5 N·m roll torque into four feasible rotor thrusts that achieve exactly 0.5 N·m, while an even thrust split across all four rotors produces 0.0 N·m of torque.

Who actually hits it

Every multirotor autopilot has a mixer (control allocation) stage; PX4 and ArduPilot both convert the controller's desired thrust-and-torque command into individual motor outputs, and the mixer's geometry is what makes an X-quad, a hexacopter, or a Y6 fly the same control law.

What it costs when it fails

Split thrust evenly and you get lift but zero torque, so the drone can't roll, pitch, or yaw; get the mixing wrong and commanded torques don't match achieved torques, and when a maneuver demands more than a motor can give, that rotor saturates and the drone loses authority on that axis and can flip.

How it's solved in production

The mixer matrix (built from rotor positions and spin directions) maps the desired thrust-and-torque wrench to the four rotor thrusts by inverting the geometry; production allocators add saturation handling — prioritizing attitude over thrust so the drone keeps control authority when it hits the motors' limits.

Toy demo → real system

Our mixer assumes ideal, instantly-responding rotors; real motors have spin-up lag, thrust that isn't perfectly linear in command, battery voltage sag that lowers the ceiling as the pack drains, and no redundancy — lose one motor on a quad and the mixer can no longer produce every torque it needs.

5

Follow without lag — feedforward tracking

Our demo: adding the reference trajectory's own acceleration as feedforward drops tracking RMSE on a 3.0 rad/s reference to 0.0139 m, versus 0.122 m for feedback alone — roughly a 9x improvement.

Who actually hits it

Anyone flying fast or aggressive trajectories relies on this; feedforward is standard in the Mellinger-Kumar-style controllers used for precise, aggressive quadrotor flight and in the position controllers of PX4 and ArduPilot.

What it costs when it fails

Pure feedback only reacts after an error appears, so on a fast reference the drone always lags behind — and lag on a delivery drone means overshooting the drop point, missing a tight aerial corridor, or drifting into an obstacle.

How it's solved in production

Feed the trajectory's own known acceleration forward to the motors so they're already pushing where the path is going, and let feedback correct only the residual error; because the smooth planned trajectory hands you that acceleration for free, feedforward turns a laggy tracker into a tight one.

Toy demo → real system

Our feedforward assumes a perfect model of the drone; real feedforward is only as good as the mass, thrust, and drag estimates it's built on, and wind gusts, a variable payload, and battery-dependent thrust all shift those numbers mid-flight, leaving feedback to absorb what the model got wrong.
