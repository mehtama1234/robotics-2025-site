Driving at the limit

An autonomous race car finding the limit does five things through every corner: pick the fastest line, spend its grip inside the friction circle, brake at the last metre, hold the tyres at their slip peak, and balance the brakes so nothing locks. Five stages of pure vehicle dynamics — each a plain rule, run and measured, with the failure it prevents.

Each stage is one thing the car must get right to carry the most speed a corner will allow: the line, the grip budget, the braking point, the tyre's grip peak, and the brake balance.

1

Find the racing line

Navigation

A corner can be driven a thousand ways, but only one is fastest. Follow the middle of the track and you hug the tightest radius through the whole turn — and the tighter the radius, the lower the speed the tyres can hold. The lap is lost in the corners, and hugging the center line throws that time away.

Take the minimum-curvature racing line, not the center line; opening the corner over the full track width raises the minimum radius, so a higher friction-limited corner speed and a faster segment.
corner speed — center line76 km/h
corner speed — racing line147 km/h
time saved0.73 s

Through the same corner, the center line's minimum radius is 30 m — good for 76 km/h. Opening it over the 12 m track width raises the racing-line radius to 114 m — 147 km/h — and clears the corner zone 0.73 s sooner.

2

Respect the friction circle

Control & MPC

The tyres have one budget of grip, shared between braking and cornering. A naive controller treats them as independent — brake at 100% AND turn at 100% at the same time — but grip adds as a vector, not a sum. The two full demands together ask for more grip than exists, and the car slides straight off.

Respect the friction circle; braking and cornering at 100% at once demands ~141% of grip and slides off — trade braking for cornering so the combined demand stays on the circle.
grip demand — brake + turn 100%141% (off track)
grip demand — friction circle100% (gripped)
grip limit1.5 g

With grip capped at 1.5 g, braking and cornering both at 100% demands 141% of the limit — off track (true). Trading braking for cornering holds the demand at 100% — on the circle, gripped.

3

Brake at the last metre

Control & MPC

Slowing from a straight-line top speed to the speed a corner will hold takes a fixed distance. Lift too early and you shed speed you didn't need to, crawling into the corner and bleeding time down the following straight. Lift too late and you can't slow enough — you arrive too fast, run wide, and miss the apex.

Brake at the last metre; the optimal braking point arrives exactly at corner speed at the apex — brake late and you carry too much speed and run wide, brake early and you crawl and lose time.
apex overshoot — brake too late20 m past apex
apex overshoot — optimal point0 m
time lost — brake too early0.30 s

Braking from 80 m/s to a 30 m/s corner needs 187 m. Braking 20 m too late still carries 39 m/s at the apex and overshoots 20 m past it; the optimal point overshoots 0 m. Braking early loses 0.30 s.

4

Find the grip peak

Control & MPC

Steering harder feels like it should always add grip — turn the wheel more, corner harder. But a tyre's lateral force follows a curve: it climbs with slip angle, peaks, then falls. Past the peak, more steering gives LESS grip, and the car pushes wide in understeer exactly when the driver is asking for more.

Control to the peak slip angle, not maximum steering; lateral tire force peaks near ~7 deg slip and falls beyond it, so more steering past the peak actually loses grip and pushes wide.
lateral grip — at peak slip1.50 g
lateral grip — over-steered1.19 g
peak slip angle8 deg

At the peak slip angle of 8 deg the tyre makes 1.50 g of cornering force. Steering on to 16 deg — well past the peak — drops it to 1.19 g, losing 20% of the grip while turning the wheel harder.

5

Balance the brakes

Hardware

Hard braking throws the car's weight forward onto the front tyres and lightens the rear. A brake system that pushes equal force front and rear ignores this — the now-lightly-loaded rear tyres get more brake force than their reduced grip can hold, they lock, and the car snaps into a spin at the worst possible moment.

Bias brake force forward to match the load transfer; braking lightens the rear axle, so an equal front/rear split overloads and locks the rear — matching the bias to the dynamic loads keeps both axles gripped.
rear brake demand — equal bias1.53× (locks)
rear brake demand — load-matched1.00×
load lifted off rear1698 N

Under braking, load transfer lifts 1698 N off the rear axle (down to 3207 N). An equal front/rear split then demands 1.53× the rear's remaining grip — it locks (true). Matching the bias to the dynamic loads holds the rear at 1.00× — gripped.
