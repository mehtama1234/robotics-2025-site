CoRL 2024: teaching a robot instead of programming it

The other big robotics conferences — ICRA, IROS — cover the whole field: sensors, control theory, mechanisms, the works. CoRL is narrower on purpose. Almost every one of these 264 papers is about the same move: instead of a person writing the rules for how a robot should act, the robot learns the behavior from data. Read the whole stack at once and one problem sits underneath everything.

The problem underneath everything

A robot skill is a function: camera pixels (and maybe a touch signal or a sentence) go in, motor commands come out. Writing that function by hand works for a bolt on an assembly line and falls apart the moment the world varies — a new object, a different light, a cup slightly rotated. There are too many situations to enumerate.

So the field's bet is: don't write the function, fit it to examples. Collect data of the skill being done well, and let a neural network find the mapping. That turns robotics into a data problem, and it splits immediately into two questions that organize the whole conference: where does the lesson come from, and how do you keep it working when the robot sees something the data never showed.

Two ways to get the lesson in

There are only two honest ways to teach a skill from data, and the two largest themes here are exactly those two.

  • Show it. A person teleoperates the robot through the task a few dozen times; the network copies the mapping from what-it-saw to what-the-human-did. This is imitation learning, and it is the most direct path — no reward to design, the demonstrations are the answer. Its weakness is equally direct: the robot only ever saw the human's successes, so the first time it drifts into a state the human never visited, it has no idea what to do and the error compounds.
  • Let it try. Give the robot a score for success and let it discover the behavior by trial and error. This is reinforcement learning, and it can find moves no human demonstrated. Its weakness is that trial-and-error on a real robot is slow and breaks hardware, so almost all of it happens in simulation — which raises the gap problem below.

A striking amount of 2024's work is about blending the two: show the robot roughly what to do, then let a little trial-and-error polish it; or use a scoring model to filter which demonstrations to trust.

Why hands and contact are the hard part

Look at where the papers pile up — dexterous manipulation, grasping, contact-rich tasks — and you see the field has converged on its hardest case: touching things. Moving through empty air is easy; the math is smooth. The instant a finger touches an object, the physics turns into a cliff — contact starts and stops abruptly, forces spike, a millimeter of error flips success to failure. Learning handles this better than hand-written rules precisely because the network can absorb that messy, discontinuous relationship from examples instead of someone having to model it. That's why manipulation, not walking or flying, is where learning-for-robots is being fought out.

The gap that keeps coming back

Two themes — trained in simulation, working for real and diffusion policies — are really two answers to failure modes of the approach above.

Simulation gives you unlimited, safe, cheap trial-and-error, but a policy trained there has secretly learned the simulator's quirks, and the real world doesn't share them. Closing that sim-to-real gap — by randomizing the simulator until reality looks like just another variation, or by correcting the policy with a little real data — is a theme unto itself.

Diffusion policies address a subtler failure: a good task often has several right answers (grab the mug left or right), and a network trained to output one average answer splits the difference and grabs nothing. Borrowing the idea behind image generators, a diffusion policy learns to sketch a whole spread of plausible action sequences and commit to one — so multiple valid options stop cancelling each other out.

Words and big pretrained models arrive

The freshest shift is the language-and-foundation-model cluster. Instead of training each skill from scratch, these papers bolt a large model — one already pretrained on internet images and text — onto the robot, so a plain-language instruction ("put the block in the bowl") can drive actions, and so the robot inherits common-sense knowledge about objects it was never shown in the lab. It's the same efficiency bet the rest of the conference makes, pushed one level up: don't just reuse demonstrations, reuse a model that already learned about the world.

What's small tells you where the field is

The tail is as informative as the head. Safety and verification, world models, dataset curation, multi-robot — all real, all small. That's the honest state of things: the field is still mostly working on getting one robot to reliably do one manipulation skill it learned. Proving that skill is safe, having the robot imagine consequences before acting, building the shared datasets that would let everyone train faster, coordinating fleets — those are the next problems, visible on the horizon but not yet where the mass of the work is.

The whole conference is one wager stated fourteen different ways: a robot that learns its skills from data will handle the messy real world better than one whose every move was written by hand. 2024 is the year that wager got most of its evidence from hands touching objects.
