RSS 2024: where the learning wave meets the math that guarantees

RSS — Robotics: Science and Systems — accepts only about a hundred and thirty papers a year, single-track, no parallel sessions. Everyone in the room sees every talk. That selectivity gives the venue a particular flavor: it takes the newest learning results seriously, but it still expects the why — the analysis, the proof, the guarantee. Read the 134 papers together and the whole conference is one argument between two ways of building a robot.

The two ways to build a robot

There have always been two answers to "how should a robot decide what to do."

  • Write the math. Model the robot's dynamics, write down the physics as equations, and solve for the optimal action in real time. This is classical robotics — optimal control, motion planning, dynamics. Its gift is guarantees: you can prove the robot stays in bounds, respects a constraint, won't tip over. Its cost is that you must model everything, and the real world is richer than any model.
  • Learn from data. Skip the equations; fit the behavior to examples or to a reward. This is the wave that took over CoRL. Its gift is flexibility — it handles messiness no equation captures. Its cost is that a learned policy comes with no proof; it works until it meets something its data never showed.

CoRL is almost entirely the second camp. What makes RSS distinct is that both camps are here, in force, in the same room — and a striking number of papers are explicitly trying to marry them.

Reading the themes as that argument

Line the themes up and you can see the two camps and the bridge between them.

On the learning side sit the three largest clusters: learning to manipulate from many kinds of data, using large language models to turn words into robot actions, and learning to walk and fly. This is the flexible, data-hungry approach, and it dominates by paper count — the wave is real.

On the classical side sit two clusters that CoRL barely has: fast, precise control of many joints and planning moves that obey the rules. These papers are about squeezing a provably-good answer out of the equations quickly enough to run on a real robot — solving contact-rich planning by turning a nasty problem into one a computer can solve exactly, or computing control for a high-joint-count body in milliseconds. This is where RSS keeps the rigor the field could otherwise lose.

The bridge is the interesting part. Over and over, a paper will learn the messy part — what the world looks like, what a person intends, how an object behaves on contact — and hand it to a classical solver that gives the guarantee. Learning supplies the model; math supplies the proof. That handoff is arguably the through-line of the whole conference.

Two problems that only show up when robots leave the lab

Two smaller themes point at where robotics is actually heading. Working safely alongside people is about the moment a robot stops being alone in a cage: now it has to predict what a person will do, communicate its own intent, and share control without hurting anyone — a problem that is half perception and half social. Whole systems out in the messy real world — farms with no GPS, off-road driving, surgery, retail — is about the unglamorous truth that a robot is only as good as its weakest integrated part. A brilliant grasping algorithm means nothing if the localization drifts in a greenhouse.

These themes are small because they are hard and honest: they are what is left over after the algorithm works in the lab.

The through-line

CoRL asks can the robot learn this? RSS asks a second question on top: and can you prove it will hold? 2024's RSS is the field trying to keep both answers — the flexibility of learning and the guarantees of math — in the same machine at the same time.

That is the tension worth watching. The learning wave gives robots abilities no equation could hand-write. The classical core gives the guarantees you need before you'll let a machine near a person or a scalpel. The best 2024 work refuses to choose, and spends its effort on the seam where the two meet.
