01 · the problem

The stakes and the constraints

Every other robotics domain works in open space. Medical robotics works inside a living, moving patient, through an opening that may be a few millimetres wide. That is not just a size constraint: the forces must be right because tissue tears, the geometry changes because the body breathes, and the view is partial because a wide incision is the injury you are trying to avoid.

This is why medical robotics is not just miniaturized manipulation. The cost of a mistake is irreversible, the workspace deforms, specular highlights confuse vision, and every action must be planned around what the imaging shows at that instant. The rest of this explainer traces how the field answers those four hard constraints — sensing, actuation, autonomy, and learning — one at a time.

the actual math — one level deeper

The workspace is a compact subset W ⊂ R³ with a moving, deformable boundary; every planned trajectory must satisfy tissue force limits f ≤ f_max at all times.

Contact forces are viscoelastic: F(t) = kδ(t) + cδ′(t) where δ is tissue deformation — stiffness k and damping c vary by tissue type and hydration state.

02 · seeing inside

A camera in a tube

Perception in open environments is hard; perception through an endoscope is a different problem. The field of view is a narrow cone. The walls reflect light in bright, moving specular spots that confuse texture matching. The lumen itself deforms with every breath and heartbeat. And there is no GPS, no prior map, no static background.

Reconstructing a useful 3D model from that stream requires simultaneous localization and mapping designed for this setting: monocular depth cues, photometric models that handle specular surfaces, and temporal filtering against breathing motion. When that reconstruction works, it gives the surgeon — and an autonomous system — a stable picture of space inside a constantly moving tube.

the actual math — one level deeper

Endoscopic SLAM: minimize reprojection error over a deformable map — {T_i, D_j} = argmin Σ_{i,j} ρ( ||p_j − π(T_i, D_j)||² ) subject to a deformation prior.

Specular reflection is Lambertian + Phong: L = k_d (n·l) + k_s (r·v)^n_s; detecting and masking specular pixels is a prerequisite for stable feature tracking.

03 · the surgeon's hands

Scaling and steadying motion

A surgeon's hand moves naturally at centimetre scale and with the tiny, involuntary oscillations of tremor. A teleoperated instrument maps that motion to sub-millimetre movement at the tip — typically a 3:1 to 10:1 reduction — while a tremor filter on the signal path removes the high-frequency component that does not correspond to intent.

The kinematic constraint that makes port-based surgery possible is the remote center of motion: the arm is built, or controlled, so the shaft always pivots around a fixed point in space at the incision. This means the port opening does not tear as the surgeon moves. Every degree of freedom at the console becomes a degree of freedom at the instrument tip, with no force on the wound edge.

the actual math — one level deeper

Motion scaling: x_instrument = x_console / s, where s ∈ [3,10] reduces tremor by a factor of s while also reducing bandwidth — the surgeon feels less force but also less noise.

Remote center of motion (RCM): a kinematic constraint Φ(q) = p_port − f(q) = 0 fixes the insertion point; enforced via constrained Jacobian J_c q′ = 0.

04 · soft & continuum

Robots that bend to fit

The body's internal channels — the bronchi, the colon, the vasculature — are winding curves, not straight lines. A traditional serial robot arm, with a fixed number of rigid links and revolute joints, cannot follow them without applying large lateral forces that damage tissue. Continuum robots replace links with a flexible backbone that bends along its whole length, so the shape of the robot matches the path it takes.

Controlling that shape is the hard part. The relationship between the actuator input (cable tension, pneumatic pressure, magnetic field) and the tip position is highly nonlinear and changes as the robot interacts with tissue. Learned models, Cosserat-rod mechanics, and vision-based estimation all contribute to steering the tip reliably to where the surgeon directs it.

the actual math — one level deeper

Cosserat rod model: d/ds(n) = −f, d/ds(m) = −e_3 × n − l; arc-length s, internal force n, moment m, distributed load f/l. Boundary conditions set by cable tensions at the base.

Tip-position control solves the nonlinear BVP numerically; learned corrections Δx = πθ(q, x_desired) compensate for hysteresis and model error.

05 · toward autonomy

Assisting, then acting

Full autonomy in surgery is nowhere near deployment, and the reason is not technical capability alone: it is verification. Before a robot action can be accepted, the system must confirm that the instrument is where imaging says it is, that the motion will not breach a structure marked off-limits, and that the surgeon can stop it in time. This is why autonomy in medical robotics is built as a ladder: one supervised subtask proven safe, then extended.

The tools that make each rung possible are the same across levels: real-time segmentation that knows where nerves, vessels, and tumour margins are; a safety envelope derived from that segmentation; and a planning layer that searches for motions inside the envelope only. The higher rungs add learned phase recognition — what step of the operation is this? — so the system knows which autonomy mode is appropriate right now.

the actual math — one level deeper

Safety envelope as a control barrier function (CBF): h(x) ≥ 0 defines safe states; CBF constraint h′(x) + α(h(x)) ≥ 0 guarantees forward invariance.

Phase recognition as a sequence model: p(phase_t | obs_{1:t}) from a recurrent or attention-based classifier over image + kinematics — gating which autonomous subtask is active.

Surgical teleoperation & interfaces

robotics
the problem

Mapping a surgeon's gross hand motion—tremor and all—to sub-millimetre instrument displacement through a fixed port, while preserving force feedback, within 5 ms round-trip latency.

the approaches

The surgeon's console must translate intent into instrument motion — scaling, filtering, and adding haptic or visual feedback without introducing dangerous latency.

Sensory gloves, augmented-reality overlays, and learned user models extend the interface beyond a joystick toward something closer to direct touch.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scale factor set to 5:1; hand moves 20 mm, tip moves 4 mm
  2. Tremor filter: bandpass blocks >6 Hz (involuntary) passes <3 Hz (intentional)
  3. RCM constraint: Jacobian null-space projection keeps port displacement < 0.2 mm
  4. Latency budget: sense→compute→actuate must fit in 5 ms or surgeon perceives lag
  5. Force feedback scaled same ratio: 0.1 N at tip → 0.5 N felt at console
  • ROBO
    Sensory Glove-Based Surgical Robot User Interface — glove captures finger intent; mapped to instrument motion with haptic feedback

    A surgeon's hands have tremor and natural coarseness. A sensory glove maps gross finger motions to scaled-down instrument displacements, filtering out involuntary oscillation while preserving intent. Haptic feedback closes the loop, letting the surgeon feel tissue resistance through the console.

    how it actually works

    The glove's flex sensors capture finger joint angles at ~100 Hz. A scaling layer reduces hand displacement by a fixed factor (typically 3:1 to 10:1), so a 20 mm hand motion becomes 2–6 mm at the instrument tip. A bandpass filter removes frequencies >6 Hz (tremor) before sending the signal to the actuators. Haptic motors at the console reproduce scaled forces: 0.1 N at the tip becomes 0.5–1 N felt at the glove, creating the illusion of direct contact. The kinematic chain (glove → scaler → tremor filter → actuator → instrument) must complete within 5 ms or the surgeon perceives lag.

    Steering an endoscope by hand is unintuitive when you cannot see what you are steering toward. Augmented-reality overlays project a target region onto the live endoscopic image, and the surgeon navigates the scope toward that target while watching the overlay move with the instrument. Visual guidance replaces mental geometry.

    how it actually works

    A preoperative 3D model or intraoperative segmentation defines a target anatomy (e.g., the gallbladder in a cholecystectomy). The system tracks endoscope pose using SLAM or electromagnetic tracking, then projects the target region onto the live video stream as a transparent overlay or reticle. As the surgeon moves the scope, the overlay updates in real time, showing whether the tip is pointing toward or away from the target. A distance metric (e.g., 3D point-to-plane error from tip to target surface) provides numeric feedback. The surgeon steers iteratively, using the visual guide to reduce error faster than freehand navigation would allow.

    Tissue must be positioned correctly for the next cut—not too deep, not pulling away—and doing this manually wastes time. A learned policy observes the surgical field and autonomously repositions tissue for the optimal cutting angle and depth, treating positioning as a subtask the robot can own.

    how it actually works

    The system is trained end-to-end on video of dissection tasks, learning to associate the current tissue configuration (imaged) with the command that best repositions the tissue for the next step. Inputs are the endoscopic video and the current phase of the procedure. The learned policy outputs a motion command: direction, speed, and hold duration. Before executing, a safety layer checks that the motion stays within a segmented safe zone (away from critical structures). The robot applies the motion, watches the result, and confirms via segmentation that the tissue is now in the desired position. Error signals (position not where expected, or motion into a forbidden zone) trigger backoff and re-planning.

    A magnetic micro-robot moves through blood vessels under external magnetic field control, but the surgeon's manual input is slow and error-prone. Deep reinforcement learning shares steering: the learned policy handles routine course corrections, while the surgeon retains high-level waypoint commands. The surgeon sets a target; the RL agent does the fine-grained steering.

    how it actually works

The surgeon provides high-level commands (target vessel segment, go left/right at the next bifurcation) via a console interface. A trained RL agent observes the robot's position (tracked by fluoroscopy or ultrasound) and the vessel geometry ahead, and issues continuous low-level steering actions (coil currents) to follow the intended path. The RL policy is trained in simulation on thousands of procedurally generated vessel trees, learning to balance responsiveness with stability. Sim-to-real transfer uses domain randomization (vessel diameter ±20%, friction ±30%). The surgeon can override at any time by issuing a new high-level command; the policy adapts immediately.

Endoscopy & laparoscopy navigation

robotics
the problem

Building a metrically consistent 3D map of a deforming, specular lumen from a monocular endoscope camera, with no static background and only 1–2 ms per frame to spare.

the approaches

Building a stable 3D reconstruction of the lumen from a monocular endoscope — specular, wet, deforming — requires SLAM tuned for this setting, not ported from an outdoor camera.

Gaussian splatting and dense depth networks have recently brought reconstruction quality close to what surgical planning needs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Specular mask: pixels with L > 240/255 excluded before feature matching
  2. Feature points: SIFT/ORB keypoints tracked across 5 frames using Lucas-Kanade
  3. Deformation prior: as-rigid-as-possible energy with λ = 0.8 keeps local rigidity
  4. Depth scale recovered from known instrument diameter: 8 mm trocar shaft
  5. Map: 2D Gaussians splat; per-frame refit in ~12 ms on RTX 3090
  • ROBO
    Advancing Dense Endoscopic Reconstruction with Gaussian Splatting (Endo-2DTAM) — 2D-Gaussian-SLAM reconstructs a deformable colon lumen from monocular endoscopic video

    Endoscopes see a narrow, deforming, specular-filled view of the lumen. Extracting a stable 3D reconstruction is SLAM on hard mode: 2D Gaussians splat onto a deformable surface, tracking features through wet, moving walls. The result is a usable map of the surgical cavity that guides planning and tracking.

    how it actually works

    The algorithm processes the monocular endoscope video frame-by-frame. High-intensity pixels (specular reflections, brightness > 240/255) are masked before feature extraction. SIFT or ORB keypoints are tracked across 5-frame windows using Lucas-Kanade, building a sparse point cloud. Depth is recovered by exploiting the known instrument diameter (an 8 mm trocar shaft in the view) as a photometric scale anchor. As new frames arrive, the system maintains 2D Gaussian splats anchored to the sparse points; each splat covers a local surface region. An as-rigid-as-possible deformation prior (λ ≈ 0.8) regularizes the surface to stay locally rigid across frames, fighting breathing and peristalsis. On modern hardware (RTX 3090), per-frame refit takes ~12 ms. The output is a continuously updated 3D mesh of the lumen, stable enough for surgical planning.

  • ROBO
    Bilateral Structural Intensity-Depth Registration for Flexible Endoscope — coupled RGB and depth alignment compensates endoscope flex

    An endoscope that flexes distorts both the RGB image and any depth estimate. Joint registration of intensity and depth channels compensates for flex, using spatial coupling to keep the two modalities aligned. The result is a coherent perception of space despite the instrument bending.

    how it actually works

    The system processes synchronized RGB and depth frames from the endoscope. Flex (or hysteresis) in the insertion tube causes a time-varying misalignment between the RGB optical center and the depth sensor optical center. An iterative registration algorithm solves for a per-frame transformation that aligns edges in the intensity image with edges in the depth image. The key constraint is bilateral consistency: intensity features must be explained by depth discontinuities, and depth discontinuities must be consistent with intensity gradients. Optimization alternates between estimating the flex transformation and updating a spatial deformation model of the lumen. This coupled approach outperforms registering RGB and depth independently, because the flex is systematic but time-varying—enforcing bilateral agreement makes the estimate more stable against noise.

  • ROBO
    Remote: Real-Time Endoscope Ego-Motion Estimation — fast monocular ego-motion for tool-in-lumen localization

    Knowing where the endoscope is and where it is moving is essential for surgical navigation and tool tracking. Fast monocular ego-motion estimation computes camera motion in real-time from the endoscope video alone, without relying on external tracking, giving the surgeon a live picture of the instrument's position inside the lumen.

    how it actually works

    The system uses optical flow and keypoint tracking on the monocular endoscope video. Consecutive frames are aligned via a two-point RANSAC algorithm that estimates the camera rotation and translation. Specular highlights are masked beforehand to avoid spurious matches. The method exploits temporal coherence: in a smooth endoscope insertion, the motion between frames is small (less than 5 mm translocation, less than 5 degrees rotation). A Kalman filter fuses frame-to-frame estimates, predicting the next ego-motion and correcting for tracking outliers. The entire pipeline (optical flow, RANSAC, Kalman update) runs in less than 1 ms per frame on CPU, allowing real-time streaming at 30 fps. Output is a smooth 6-DOF pose (x,y,z, roll,pitch,yaw) of the endoscope in the lumen, which downstream modules use for tool-in-lumen localization and augmented-reality overlay positioning.

  • ROBO
    SLAM Assisted 3D Tracking for Laparoscopic Surgery — SLAM-derived map priors stabilize instrument tracking under deformation

    Surgical instruments deform and drift in the laparoscopic view. SLAM builds a map of the surgical cavity once, then uses that fixed map to stabilize and predict instrument positions, fighting the visual drift that plagues frame-to-frame tracking alone.

    how it actually works

    The system runs two parallel threads. The first is a real-time SLAM backend: sparse keypoint features are matched across the video stream, and a bundle-adjustment step maintains a metrically accurate 3D map of the tissue. Because the tissue itself deforms, the SLAM map is treated as semi-static: it gets a local update every 10–20 frames but is not globally optimized every frame (to avoid over-correcting for deformation). The second thread is instrument tracking. The instrument (e.g., a grasper or dissector) is tracked frame-to-frame via template matching or Hough circles (for the instrument shaft). Without map context, this accumulates drift. The innovation is to project the instrument's 3D position into the SLAM map: if the instrument's tracked position projects to an invalid region (inside tissue or far from any keypoint), the track is corrected to the nearest valid position in the SLAM map. This map-informed correction reduces tracking drift by ~70% compared to frame-to-frame tracking alone.

Steerable needles

robotics
the problem

Guiding a bevel-tip needle along a curved path through heterogeneous tissue to a point-target ≤2 mm in diameter, with no visual feedback beyond ultrasound slices.

the approaches

A bevel-tip needle bends as it advances through tissue; control that bending and you can steer around obstacles to reach deep targets without open surgery.

Resolution-optimal planning and MPC make the path precise enough for lung biopsy and vascular access.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Bevel offset: 1 mm bevel generates curvature radius of 50 mm in liver tissue
  2. Duty-cycle spinning: spin at 30 RPM during insertion straightens the path, stop to bend
  3. Obstacle: vessel at 18 mm depth; planned path arcs 8° to clear by 3 mm margin
  4. MPC horizon: 10 steps, 1 mm per step; replan every 2 mm of insertion
  5. Final error: tip lands within 1.4 mm of target in phantom study (n=20)
  • ROBO
    Steerable Tape-Spring Needle for Autonomous Sharp Turns — tape-spring mechanism enables tight-radius turns unreachable by bevel-tip designs

    Bevel-tip needles bend as they advance, but the radius of curvature is limited by tissue stiffness. A tape-spring mechanism pre-bends the needle, enabling tighter-radius turns that can thread around obstacles bevel-only designs cannot reach.

    how it actually works

    A tape-spring is a thin, curved metal ribbon that naturally wants to curl. Wrapped helically around a flexible tube (the needle shaft), it stores elastic energy and tries to coil the shaft into a tight helix. As the needle advances, friction from the tissue releases this stored energy, and the needle tip follows a curved path. Unlike a passive bevel tip (which generates curvature only from tissue stiffness), the tape-spring actively bends the needle. By tuning the initial curvature and spring stiffness, the designer can achieve a needle curvature radius of 30–50 mm, vs. 80–150 mm for bevel-only designs. The control law is spinning duty-cycle: spin the needle to go straight (friction prevents the spring from coiling), stop spinning to allow the spring to curve. Closed-loop steering via ultrasound feedback confirms that turns meet the planned angle. This trades mechanical complexity for access to targets (e.g., lung nodules) that require sharper bends than classical needle steering offers.

  • ROBO
    Resolution-Optimal Motion Planning for Needle Steering in Lung — plans a path through lung that meets accuracy requirements with fewest control steps

    Steering a needle through lung to a biopsy target requires a path that is accurate (tip lands near the target) with the fewest control steps (minimize insertions and spin-stop cycles). Resolution-optimal planning solves for the path that meets accuracy needs with minimum control effort. Fewer steps mean less tissue trauma.

    how it actually works

    The system takes as input a 3D ultrasound map of the lung, the needle's kinematics (curvature radius as a function of spin/stop commands, tissue stiffness estimates), and a target position within ±2 mm. A motion-planning algorithm searches over sequences of control steps (spin-N-steps, stop-M-steps, repeat). Each sequence is simulated forward using the needle kinematics model: given tissue stiffness, the curvature is deterministic; the cumulative trajectory is computed via numerical integration of the Cosserat rod model. Simulated trajectories are scored on (1) distance of final tip position to the target (must be < 2 mm) and (2) number of control steps (minimized). A* or RRT* finds the Pareto-optimal plan: the one with fewest steps that still hits the accuracy requirement. The planner runs offline before insertion; during insertion, an MPC layer updates the model using real deflection feedback and re-plans if drift exceeds a threshold.

  • ROBO
    Model Predictive Control for 3D Steerable Needles — MPC tracks planned paths in real tissue using online model updates

    A needle path plan must adapt as the needle advances through heterogeneous tissue with varying stiffness. MPC tracks the planned path by predicting the next few steps ahead and correcting the control input when actual deflection differs from the model. Real-time replanning keeps accuracy under 2 mm.

    how it actually works

    MPC maintains an internal model of needle kinematics: curvature as a function of tissue stiffness k, bevel angle, and spindle speed. At each control cycle (every 1 mm of insertion), the controller observes the actual needle position (via ultrasound or fluoroscopy). If position deviates from the planned path by more than ±0.5 mm, the controller solves a finite-horizon optimization problem: what sequence of spin/stop commands over the next 10 steps (10 mm) brings the needle back onto the planned path? The optimization uses the current tissue stiffness estimate (updated online from observed deflection) and a prediction of stiffness ahead (interpolated from the US scan). The first command in the optimized sequence is executed, then the cycle repeats. This online adaptation handles the fact that tissue stiffness varies (fatty vs. fibrous lung varies by ±30%); the MPC corrects within 1–2 control steps. Over a full needle insertion (~50 mm), the accumulated error remains less than 1.4 mm on average in phantom studies (n=20).

  • ROBO
    Ultrasound-Guided Robotic Blood Drawing — image-guided autonomy localizes and punctures a vein without operator input

    Puncturing a vein for blood draw requires finding the vein (typically 3 mm diameter at depth 10–20 mm) and hitting it without collateral damage. A robot integrates ultrasound imaging and learned autonomy to locate the vein and autonomously execute the puncture, removing operator tremor from the insertion.

    how it actually works

    The system acquires a 2D ultrasound image showing the target vein in cross-section. A convolutional network segments the vein lumen (< 1 mm localization error in the image plane). The robot arm is commanded to move the ultrasound probe until the vein is centered in the image. Once the vein is centered, a syringe or butterfly needle attached to the arm is commanded to advance along the ultrasound beam axis (out-of-plane from the imaging perspective). The needle's in-plane position is held constant via a visual-servoing loop: image-plane error is minimal because the needle tip is already centered. Depth into tissue is controlled by integrating encoder readings on the needle advancement motor. A learned depth model (trained on phantoms and ex vivo tissue) predicts how deep the needle must travel to reach the vein, accounting for skin compressibility and tissue layer thickness. Once the needle depth reaches the predicted value, the arm issues a small suction pulse to confirm vein puncture (sudden blood backflow); if successful, automated sampling begins.

Continuum & soft medical robots

robotics
the problem

Computing the cable tensions needed to steer a tendon-driven flexible backbone to a desired tip pose, when stiffness varies with temperature and hysteresis makes the tension–shape map irreversible.

the approaches

A flexible backbone bends continuously rather than at discrete joints, letting the robot follow winding anatomy. Tendon tension, pneumatics, or magnetic fields steer the shape.

Hysteresis in tendon drives and nonlinear stiffness in soft bodies are the control challenges the field keeps returning to.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Cosserat rod: arc length s ∈ [0, 150 mm]; 3 cables, 120° apart
  2. Stiffness k varies ±18% with hydration; identified online every 50 mm insertion
  3. Hysteresis model: Prandtl-Ishlinskii with 12 play operators reduces error by 60%
  4. Tip position error: 1.8 mm RMS after learned correction vs 5.1 mm open-loop
  5. FEM timestep: 4 ms (quasi-static, 3 Newton iterations per step)
  • ROBO
    Accelerated Quasi-Static FEM for Continuum Robots — fast FEM simulation enables real-time model-based control of soft bodies

    A tendon-driven continuum robot's shape is determined by cable tensions, but computing that shape requires solving a nonlinear system. Fast quasi-static FEM evaluates the robot shape in real-time, enabling model-based closed-loop control instead of slow open-loop tuning. The 4 ms feedback loop closes 250 times per second.

    how it actually works

    The continuum robot is modeled as a Cosserat rod: a one-dimensional elastic beam parametrized by arc length s ∈ [0, L]. The rod obeys differential equations: d(n)/ds = −f (internal force balance) and d(m)/ds = −e_3 × n − l (moment balance), where n is internal force, m is internal moment, and l is the distributed load. Boundary conditions (base position and orientation) are set by the robot's root joint; the cable attachment points at the base (typically 3 cables, 120° apart) define the loads l. The FEM discretizes the rod into ~20–50 elements. At each control cycle, given cable tensions T_1, T_2, T_3, the system solves the nonlinear BVP using Newton iteration (~3 iterations per cycle). Each iteration takes ~1 ms on a modern CPU (or < 0.5 ms with GPU acceleration). The result is the rod's 3D configuration: positions and angles at every point along the length. The tip position is extracted and fed to the controller; if it deviates from the setpoint, cable tensions are adjusted and the cycle repeats.

  • ROBO
    On the Benefits of Hysteresis in Tendon-Driven Continuum Robots — characterizes how hysteresis helps or hurts position tracking; design implications

    Tendons that drive continuum robots do not respond linearly to tension: pulling harder does not always increase stiffness proportionally because of hysteresis and friction. Characterizing how hysteresis helps or hurts tracking accuracy shows that some hysteresis is beneficial (it damps oscillations) while excessive hysteresis breaks precise control. Design must balance the trade-off.

    how it actually works

    The relationship between cable tension T and the resulting tip deflection x is nonlinear and history-dependent (hysteresis). Hysteresis models (Prandtl-Ishlinskii or Duhem models) are fit to experimental tension-deflection cycles: increasing T then decreasing T traces different paths. The magnitude of hysteresis is quantified by the area enclosed in the cycle. Experiments vary the cable diameter, material (nylon vs. Kevlar), and lubrication, producing hysteresis loops with different characteristics. For each design, tracking performance is measured: set the robot tip to a desired position trajectory, and compute RMSE error. Results show that zero hysteresis is unattainable and actually leads to instability (oscillations). Moderate hysteresis (loop width ±3% of cable tension) provides damping, reducing overshoot. Excessive hysteresis (>10%) makes the robot slow to respond and adds static position error. Optimal hysteresis for a given task is design-dependent: a fast task (< 50 mm/s motion) tolerates more hysteresis; a precise task requires < 5% hysteresis. This guides material and lubrication choices during design.

  • ROBO
    Variable-Stiffness Nasotracheal Intubation Robot — stiffness tuning navigates the nasal passage without tissue trauma

    Threading a tube through the nasal passage without tearing the tissue requires the tube to be flexible (to follow the curve) but stiff (to not buckle). A variable-stiffness mechanism allows the robot to adapt stiffness in real-time: soft while advancing, stiff while correcting angle. Tissue trauma drops 80% compared to fixed-stiffness designs.

    how it actually works

    The intubation tube is a thin silicone catheter wrapped with a segmented cable-reinforced sleeve. The sleeve segments are connected by flexible joints filled with pressurizable chambers (or shape-memory alloy wires). When the pressure is low (or current is off), the sleeve is floppy, and the catheter flexes freely to follow the nasal geometry. As the tip encounters resistance (force sensor feedback > 0.5 N), the controller increases chamber pressure (or SMA current), stiffening the local segment. This allows the robot to correct its angle against tissue resistance without tearing. The stiffness profile is graded from tip to base: distal segments are kept soft to conform, while proximal segments are stiffer to provide support. A force-sensitive feedback loop monitors tissue contact and modulates stiffness: if the catheter is advancing smoothly (low force), stiffness is minimal; if resistance spikes (tissue pinched or curve too tight), stiffness increases by 50–100% to help the catheter push through the obstruction. The result is a tube that behaves like a soft probe when gliding freely but hardens to penetrate dense regions, replicating the manual dexterity of an experienced operator.

  • ROBO

robotics
the problem

Actuating an untethered magnetic robot or capsule through a fluid-filled cavity using only an external rotating magnetic field, with no direct force sensing or proprioception.

the approaches

A wireless capsule or magnetically steered micro-robot can reach sites no catheter or endoscope can access, but it gives up the mechanical connection that makes force control straightforward.

External magnetic fields, acoustic streaming, and swarm coordination are the actuation options; PINN-based flow models help predict where the robot goes.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. External field: 8 mT rotating at 2 Hz drives the capsule at ~3 mm/s
  2. PINN: trained on 50,000 CFD flow-field samples; infers drift in 2 ms
  3. Swarm: 4 micro-robots, 200 μm each; magnetic interaction ≤0.5 mT at 500 μm separation
  4. Position tracking: overhead camera + Kalman filter; RMS error 0.3 mm
  5. Navigation: capsule reaches target in 28 s avg (n=15 phantom trials)

    A capsule endoscope is passive—a camera in a swallowed pill that tumbles through the gut. But what if the capsule could extend a tiny robotic arm to grab a biopsy? Powering it wirelessly is the barrier. The solution: rotate external magnetic coils to steer the capsule and drive an arm actuator, all through magnetic fields alone.

    how it actually works

    An array of electromagnetic coils surrounds the patient (e.g., a 3×3×3 grid around the abdomen). Each coil is driven independently by a current amplifier. A control algorithm computes the field direction and magnitude needed to achieve a desired force on the capsule's magnetic payload. To spin the capsule clockwise, the coils generate a rotating field; to deploy the arm, they generate a field gradient that pulls the arm mechanism. The capsule's position is tracked via external magnetic field sensors or fluoroscopy. A feedback loop reads the position, computes the target field, and updates all coil currents—closed-loop control of an untethered robot through an external field. The challenge is that the field pattern determines both capsule position and arm deployment, so they must be coordinated: a field that moves the capsule forward should not accidentally retract the arm.

    A single micro-robot is fragile and weak. A swarm of tiny robots coordinated by magnetic fields can carry more, adapt to complex paths, and tolerate failures. The challenge is coordinating dozens of millimeter-scale robots in a confined space without individual communication—the only signal is the external magnetic field.

    how it actually works

    Micro-robots are fabricated with permanent magnets and simple actuation (flagella or internal gears). An external rotating or time-varying magnetic field actuates all of them; individual control comes from designing each robot's response: some robots have stronger magnets and respond preferentially to field in certain directions; others have different shapes and move differently in the same field. By modulating the field, the operator can steer the swarm as a collective. Simple rules—'move toward the field source', 'maintain spacing from neighbors'—cause the swarm to flow together, follow paths, and avoid local obstacles without explicit commands to individuals. For liquid environments (stomach, blood vessel), the swarm navigates pulsatile flow by detecting field intensity changes and responding collectively. In air (lung), buoyancy is negligible and the swarm is more maneuverable.

  • ROBO
    Flow-Aware Navigation of Magnetic Micro-Robots via PINN — a physics-informed network predicts flow field; controller compensates for drift

    A magnetic micro-robot drifts with the blood flow—the surgeon cannot predict where it will go without understanding the flow field. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) can learn the flow field from observations and predict robot trajectories, compensating for drift automatically.

    how it actually works

    A PINN is trained on computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations of blood flow through a vessel (or on real flow measurements). The PINN learns to evaluate the Navier-Stokes equations at any point in space and time, outputting velocity. Once trained, the PINN infers the local flow field at the robot's current position. A controller computes the magnetic force needed to counteract drift: if the flow is 2 mm/s rightward, apply a leftward force to keep the robot on course. The PINN is fast (< 2 ms per inference), so the feedback loop can run at 100 Hz. This closes the loop: observe robot position (via external tracking), estimate local flow (via PINN), command compensatory force (via coil array). The result is that the robot reaches its target despite pulsatile and steady flow, without explicit flow modeling.

    Nanorobots made from DNA can fold into precise 3D shapes (origami) and be programmed to carry cargo and respond to chemical signals. Tetrahedral DNA origami structures can navigate to a target cell and release a therapeutic payload—a nanoscale version of targeted drug delivery.

    how it actually works

One vertex is decorated with targeting ligands (e.g., antibodies) that recognize a cancer cell's surface markers. Another vertex carries the therapeutic payload (siRNA, drug molecules, or enzymes). A third vertex has logic circuits (strand-displacement reactions) that detect binding to the target cell and trigger release. Once injected into the bloodstream, the nanorobots diffuse through tissues (passive transport); when a tetrahedron's targeting ligand touches a cancer cell, the cell's receptors bind to the ligand, and the nucleic acid logic circuits execute, causing the tetrahedron to open and spill the payload into the cell. No external command needed; the nanorobot is autonomous and programmable through its DNA design.

vision
the problem

Segmenting anatomy reliably from intraoperative images where training data is scarce, domain shift is severe, and the label budget is <50 annotated volumes.

the approaches

Segmenting anatomy in intraoperative images is the backbone of image-guided robotics: no accurate safety envelope without accurate masks, and no accurate masks without models that handle the domain.

Foundation model adaptation and deformable registration address the data-scarce, rapidly shifting intraoperative setting.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Foundation model: SAM ViT-H with 636 M parameters; frozen encoder
  2. Few-shot: 10 support images per organ class (liver, gallbladder, bile duct)
  3. Deformation field: B-spline with 16×16×16 control points; optimized with NCC + BEnd
  4. Dice score: 0.83 on liver, 0.71 on gallbladder with 10-shot SAM adaptation
  5. Registration time: 2.1 s per volume pair on RTX 3090 (vs 45 min for ANTs CPU)
  • CVPR
    Focus on Background: Few-Shot Medical Segmentation with SAM — adapts SAM to medical imaging by re-weighting background context in few-shot settings

    Foundation models like SAM (Segment Anything) are pretrained on millions of images and can segment new objects with just a few clicks. But SAM was trained on natural images; medical images look different—anatomy, artifacts, and pathology confuse the pretrained encoder. Re-weighting the background in few-shot adaptation helps SAM transfer to surgical and medical domains.

    how it actually works

    SAM's encoder outputs image features; a lightweight adapter (a few additional linear layers) is trained to map these features to medical-domain segmentation. In few-shot settings (10–30 labeled examples per organ class), the adapter is prone to overfitting on background pixels. The fix: during adaptation, weight the loss higher on background pixels that are visually similar to the foreground (e.g., similarly colored or textured background). This forces the adapter to focus on the boundary between organ and background, even when it has few examples. After adaptation on 10 images, SAM's encoder + adapter can segment new liver or gallbladder images with Dice scores > 0.8, competitive with fully supervised models trained on thousands of images.

  • CVPR
    Dynamic Stream Network for Deformable Medical Image Registration — dual-stream network captures large and fine-scale deformations jointly

    Medical image registration aligns two images (e.g., intraoperative and preoperative CT) by computing a deformation field that warps one image onto the other. Large deformations (organ shift, tissue resection) and fine details (small vessel alignment) happen at different scales, and a single-scale network struggles. A dual-stream network captures large and fine-scale deformations jointly.

    how it actually works

    One stream processes the image at low resolution, learning coarse deformations (organ shift by 10–50 mm). A second stream processes high-resolution patches, learning local corrections (blood vessel alignment at 1 mm scale). The streams are fused via attention: for each location, the network decides whether to trust the coarse or fine stream. At registration time, the coarse stream is applied first, warping the image closer to the target; then the fine stream refines locally. Alternatively, both streams output displacement fields that are added. The dual-stream design allows the network to handle deformations across scales without a massive receptive field or multiscale pyramid, improving registration speed and accuracy on medical images with both gross shifts and fine details.

  • CVPR
    Multi-View 2D/3D Registration with Differentiable X-Ray Rendering — differentiable DRR enables gradient-based alignment of intraoperative X-ray to CT plan

    Surgical planning happens preoperatively from CT scans; intraoperative X-ray fluoroscopy is 2D. Aligning the live 2D X-ray to the 3D preoperative plan requires finding the 3D pose (6 DOF) that best matches what the camera 'sees'. Differentiable rendering makes this alignment gradient-based and fast, avoiding expensive marker-based fiducials.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: A differentiable X-ray renderer (DRR) takes the 3D CT volume and a 6-DOF pose hypothesis, and produces a synthetic X-ray image in ~30 ms. This synthetic image is compared to the live X-ray using normalized cross-correlation (NCC) or mutual information. Gradient descent on the pose (via automatic differentiation through the rendering pipeline) refines the alignment. Multiple fluoroscopy views can be used simultaneously to resolve ambiguities.

    Why it works: Differentiable rendering replaces the discrete search (try many poses, pick the best) with continuous optimization (refine the pose via gradients). This is orders of magnitude faster and requires no fiducials — the anatomical structures themselves are the alignment signal. The robot then uses the registered pose to guide instruments.

  • CVPR

    Most segmentation models are trained on one anatomy (liver, heart) and fail on others. In-context learning—showing the model a few examples of the new anatomy and asking it to segment the rest—requires no retraining. A pretrained foundation model can segment 3D medical volumes in-context, adapting to new anatomy from a handful of labeled slices.

    how it actually works

    INSID3 starts with a large pretrained encoder (e.g., a vision transformer) trained on diverse medical imaging tasks via self-supervised learning. At test time, the model is given a small support set: 2–4 slices from a 3D volume with manual segmentation labels. The model processes the support slices, extracting prototypes of the target class (e.g., the appearance of lung tissue). Then the model segments the rest of the volume by comparing each unlabeled slice to the prototypes via learned metrics or attention. No fine-tuning or retraining—the adaptation happens through the forward pass. This in-context mechanism works because the pretrained encoder has seen many organ types and learned a general notion of 'where is the structure I should find', and the support set provides just enough context to specialize to the target anatomy.

vision
the problem

Aggregating diagnostic evidence from a gigapixel whole-slide image — spanning 100,000 × 100,000 pixels — where the diagnostic signal may occupy only 0.1% of the area.

the approaches

Whole-slide pathology images are gigapixel; a model that reads them must aggregate evidence across scales, from cell-level morphology to tissue-level architecture.

Self-supervised pretraining on large pathology corpora and topological priors are moving diagnostic accuracy toward and past human expert level.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Slide size: 1 gigapixel at 40× magnification = 100k × 100k pixels
  2. Patch extraction: 256×256 tiles at 20×; ~10,000 patches per slide after tissue masking
  3. Topology: persistent homology at H0 and H1; Betti numbers summarize tissue connectivity
  4. MIL pooling: attention-weighted mean of 768-dim patch embeddings (CONCH encoder)
  5. AUC: 0.94 for TCGA-NSCLC subtype classification (LUAD vs LUSC)

    TopoSlide: Topological Whole-Slide Pathology Learning — persistent homology captures tissue-level structure beyond bag-of-patches models

    A gigapixel pathology slide is too large to fit in memory; models must aggregate information from thousands of small patches. Most approaches treat patches as a bag—order does not matter—losing spatial structure. Topological methods (persistent homology) summarize the connectivity and clustering of tissue at multiple scales, capturing architecture beyond patch appearance.

    how it actually works

    Patches are extracted at two resolutions (high-mag and low-mag). Each patch is embedded via a pretrained encoder. A topological descriptor (persistent homology at H0 and H1, or a learned topological loss) is computed on the patch-embedding point cloud. H0 counts connected components (tissue regions); H1 counts holes (gland lumens, tubules). These topological features, paired with attention-weighted statistics of patch embeddings, form a whole-slide representation. The topological features are invariant to exact positions but sensitive to tissue structure, so two slides with similar architecture but different individual patches are recognized as similar. This improves generalization and reliability compared to bag-of-patches MIL models.

A multimodal language model can reason hierarchically over a WSI, reading it from patch level to slide level with natural-language reasoning at each scale.

    how it actually works

    The model processes patches in a hierarchy: (1) Low-level: extract embeddings for 256×256 patches at 20×; cluster similar patches; (2) Mid-level: group clusters into regions and read them with visual language prompts like 'describe the structure in this region'; (3) High-level: aggregate region descriptions into a slide-level diagnosis. At each level, a large language model (like LLaMA) reasons in natural language, integrating multi-scale visual features with clinical knowledge. For example, at the patch level: 'this is a glandular adenocarcinoma'; at the region level: 'multiple glands with necrosis and high mitotic rate'; at the slide level: 'high-grade adenocarcinoma with unfavorable prognosis'. The hierarchy allows the model to reason about what each patch means in context, not in isolation.

Mask-guided self-supervision learns 3D representations by masking patches in volumetric CT and MRI, then predicting the masked regions.

    how it actually works

The encoder processes the unmasked patches and outputs embeddings for all patch positions. A lightweight decoder (MLP) predicts the masked patches' feature vectors based on context. The loss is the regression error between predicted and true masked-patch features. After pretraining on thousands of unlabeled CT/MRI volumes, the encoder has learned 3D structural patterns (how tissues connect, how organs deform, anatomy priors). This pretrained encoder is then used as backbone for downstream tasks (segmentation, classification) with 10–20% fewer labeled examples needed to reach the same accuracy as models trained from scratch on labeled data alone.

A Transformer that operates over 3D volumetric patches can read a whole CT and predict diagnosis (screening, staging) directly.

    how it actually works

    3D patches (e.g., 32×32×32 voxels) are extracted from a CT volume and embedded via a learned encoder. A Transformer processes the sequence of patch embeddings, learning which patches are diagnostic (e.g., patches containing the tumor) and how they relate (e.g., tumor size and extent). Attention weights identify the most important patches, providing interpretability—the clinician can see which parts of the CT the model focused on. The output is a classification (cancer present/absent) or a staging label (stage I/II/III/IV). Because the Transformer can attend to any patch at any distance, it naturally captures spatial relationships—e.g., 'tumor extends into the adjacent blood vessel'—that slice-by-slice methods miss.

Radiology reports & medical VLMs

vision
the problem

Generating factually correct radiology reports — where a hallucinated finding triggers unnecessary treatment — while covering all clinically relevant findings in a single pass.

the approaches

A model that reads a scan and writes the report must be factually accurate: a hallucinated finding is a diagnostic error. RL and evidence grounding are the current tools for pushing accuracy past what supervised training alone achieves.

Benchmarks that test complex multi-step reasoning expose how far the best models still are from a reliable second-reader.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Base model: LLaVA-Med-7B fine-tuned on 27,000 chest X-ray/report pairs
  2. RL: GRPO with oracle reward; 4 reference reports per image as oracle signal
  3. Hallucination rate: drops from 18% to 6% after GRPO fine-tuning (CheXbert eval)
  4. Evidence grounding: bounding box IoU > 0.5 required before a finding is stated
  5. F1 on CheXpert-14: 0.71 vs 0.58 baseline supervised fine-tuning
  • CVPR
    OraPO: Oracle-Educated RL for Factual Radiology Reports — GRPO with oracle-supervised reward suppresses hallucinated findings in radiology reports

    A vision-language model can read a chest X-ray and write a report, but it often hallucinates findings that are not in the image. Reinforcement learning with an oracle reward (comparing to reference reports written by radiologists) can suppress hallucinations. GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) trains the model to write accurate reports by rewarding factuality and penalizing unsupported claims.

    how it actually works

    A base VLM (e.g., LLaVA-Med-7B) is fine-tuned on 27,000 chest X-ray / report pairs via supervised learning. Then GRPO training begins: the model generates multiple candidate reports for the same image (n=4), and each is scored via an oracle reward function (e.g., CheXbert scoring, or comparison to reference reports). The reward is high if findings match the references, low if they are hallucinated. GRPO updates the model's policy (the LLM weights) using relative ranking: if report A has higher reward than report B, make report A more likely and report B less likely. After GRPO, hallucination rates drop from 18% to 6%, and the model learns to ground findings in image evidence. The oracle reward is the key: it teaches the model that saying 'pneumonia' when there is none is wrong.

  • CVPR
    EMAD: Evidence-Grounded Medical AI Diagnosis — model localizes image evidence before stating a diagnosis, making errors traceable

    A model that outputs a diagnosis should also explain where in the image the evidence is. If the diagnosis is wrong, the explainability lets a clinician catch the error. EMAD requires the model to localize evidence (e.g., bounding box) before outputting a diagnosis, making errors transparent and predictions more trustworthy.

    how it actually works

    The model is a two-stage pipeline: (1) evidence localization—a bounding box or mask is predicted for the region supporting each diagnostic claim; (2) diagnosis prediction—the model predicts the label only after visual confirmation of evidence. The model is trained on data where both images and evidence regions are labeled. A loss function penalizes diagnosis predictions that lack supporting evidence: if the model predicts 'pneumonia' but the evidence box misses the consolidation, the loss is high. Once trained, the model outputs both a diagnosis and a visual explanation (localization). During deployment, the model can refuse to diagnose if high-confidence evidence is not found. This constraint improves calibration and reliability: the model learns to be more conservative when evidence is weak.

  • CVPR
    LLaDA-MedV: Biomedical Diffusion VLM — masked diffusion language model adapted for biomedical vision-language tasks

    Large language models adapted to medical images (like LLaVA-Med) still have high error rates on domain-specific tasks. Diffusion models can generate and refine text iteratively, allowing a medical VLM to be more careful and self-correcting.

    how it actually works

    Instead of decoding a report in one pass (autoregressive generation), the model uses a masked diffusion approach: start with a full-length report where half the words are masked (blanks). Iteratively unmask and refine words based on image features. At each step, the model re-reads the image and current partial report, predicting the best word to fill in at the least-confident masked position. By multiple passes (e.g., 20 refinement steps), the report converges to a consistent, coherent, and accurate summary. This iterative refinement allows the model to notice contradictions (if it said 'no consolidation' in one sentence but 'pneumonia' later) and fix them. It also reduces hallucination by ensuring each claim is reconsidered against the image multiple times.

  • CVPR
    Med-CMR: Complex Medical Reasoning Benchmark — benchmark requiring multi-hop evidence integration across image, text, and clinical context

    Current medical AI benchmarks test simple classification (disease present/absent). Real radiology requires complex reasoning: integrating findings across multiple images, reasoning about temporal change, and combining clinical context. Med-CMR is a benchmark requiring multi-hop reasoning: the model must gather evidence from several images and text sources to reach a diagnosis.

    how it actually works

    The benchmark provides: (1) multiple medical images (e.g., CT, PET, prior X-ray from 1 year ago); (2) clinical text (patient age, symptoms, prior results); (3) a diagnostic question (e.g., 'does this patient have metastatic cancer?'). The model must reason across all modalities: notice the 5 mm lung nodule in this year's CT, compare size to last year's CT (it grew 1 mm, suggesting malignancy), read the PET (hot uptake confirms), and synthesize into a diagnosis. Models are evaluated on multi-step accuracy: if any intermediate reasoning step is missed, the final diagnosis fails. This benchmark reveals that even large models struggle with reasoning chains longer than 2–3 hops, pushing development toward better medical reasoning systems.

vision
the problem

Generating anatomically valid 3D medical volumes for rare conditions where fewer than 200 real training cases exist, without producing topologically impossible structures.

the approaches

Rare conditions have too few real images to train a diagnostic model. Generative models can fill that gap — but only if the generated images are anatomically correct: no spurious structures, no topological errors.

Structure-aware diffusion and topology controls are the answers the field has found.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Conditioning: 2D sketch (10 strokes) → 3D CT volume at 256×256×128 resolution
  2. Topology check: connected components of generated mask must equal 1 (one liver)
  3. Diffusion: 1,000 denoising steps during training; 50 DDIM steps at inference
  4. FID on held-out pathology: 18.4 vs 31.2 for unconstrained diffusion baseline
  5. Downstream: classifier trained on 200 synthetic + 50 real matches one on 250 real (Dice 0.79 vs 0.81)
  • CVPR
    Sketch2CT: Structure-Aware 3D CT Volume Generation — generates a full CT volume conditioned on a user-drawn anatomical sketch

    Generating realistic CT volumes for rare conditions is valuable for training diagnostic models, but generating anatomically correct volumes is hard—a model might produce a liver without a proper shape, or missing the gallbladder. Conditioning generation on a user sketch—rough strokes indicating where anatomy should be—guides the diffusion model toward realistic structure.

    how it actually works

    The model is a conditional diffusion model: the unconditional noise is conditioned on (1) a 2D sketch drawn by a user on a 2D slice (indicating liver region, bile duct path, etc.) and (2) class labels (e.g., 'normal liver' or 'cirrhotic liver'). The diffusion process starts with noise and iteratively refines it toward a 3D volume that matches the sketch and labels. A learned encoder converts the 2D sketch to a 3D sketch volume (extrapolating the 2D strokes to 3D). At each denoising step, the model predicts voxels while respecting the sketch constraint: voxels outside the sketch region should be background; voxels inside should be hepatic tissue. A second constraint ensures topology: if the sketch indicates a connected liver, the generated volume must be connected. After 50 DDIM steps, the result is a plausible 3D CT volume matching the user's intent.

  • CVPR
    Anatomica: Topology-Controlled Anatomical Diffusion — enforces anatomical topology (connectivity, nesting) during diffusion sampling

    Diffusion models can generate anatomically varied images, but sometimes produce impossible structures: organs in the wrong place, missing connections, or nesting violations (e.g., a lung inside a liver). Enforcing anatomical topology during diffusion—constraints on connectivity and spatial relationships—prevents impossible structures.

    how it actually works

    The topology constraint is encoded as a loss function or a guidance term added to the diffusion process. For example: (1) connectivity: after each denoising step, check that each organ is connected (no orphaned pieces); if not, penalize. (2) Nesting: liver should always be inside the body cavity, never outside or inside the lung; add a loss if violations occur. (3) Spatial prior: likelihood of left vs. right; add guidance to respect anatomical orientation. These constraints are enforced via a differentiable check on the current denoising state. If a constraint is violated, the guidance adjusts the predicted noise direction to move toward a valid state. The result is that diffusion generates diverse, realistic anatomies within a valid constrained space. Downstream classifiers trained on synthetic + real data outperform those trained on real alone.

  • CVPR
    Beyond Pixel Simulation: Pathology Image Generation — diffusion model for histopathology trained on staining and diagnostic diversity

    Generative models trained on histopathology can produce synthetic slides for training diagnostic models. The challenge is that pathology images vary widely: different staining protocols, tissue types, and disease manifestations. Diffusion models conditioned on staining protocol and diagnosis can generate realistic histopathology images with desired attributes.

    how it actually works

Conditioning is achieved via class tokens or CLIP embeddings of text descriptions. By varying the conditioning, a single model can generate diverse staining and diagnostic combinations, augmenting training sets for rare subtypes and helping balance imbalanced datasets.

  • CVPR

A physics-accurate surgical simulator (mujoco or similar) is then rendered with these realistic retinal images as the background, and procedurally-generated tools interact with the synthetic tissue. Policies are trained via RL in this sim environment. For validation, a subset of real surgical videos is held out; the learned policy is applied to real video frames (predicting tool motion from image), and the predictions are evaluated for correctness (did the tool reach the target, did it damage tissue). This Real2Sim2Real loop avoids the large cost of directly learning from expensive real surgery while still grounding the simulation in real retinal appearance and dynamics.

Part IV

Where it stands

Medical robotics has changed surgery — and it is still far from the full autonomy the field aims at. What is genuinely still hard:

Precision vs safety margins. Sub-millimetre accuracy means the no-go boundary must also be sub-millimetre — but intraoperative segmentation is not yet that precise.
Automation vs surgeon override. Every autonomous step must be interruptible at any instant; designing control handoff that is safe in both directions is unsolved.
Imaging latency vs tissue deformation. Breathing and heartbeat move tissue faster than many imaging loops; prediction and compensation for that motion is still an open problem.
Soft/continuum control vs stiffness. A compliant tool that follows anatomy also resists being commanded precisely — the trade-off between safety and accuracy in soft bodies is not closed.
Generalization vs anatomical specificity. A model trained on one anatomy or procedure transfers poorly to another; medical AI still needs too much per-task data.
Validation only on phantoms. Most autonomous surgical methods are validated in phantom or cadaver studies; the evidence base for live-patient deployment remains thin.
