01 · the problem

Where vision goes blind

A camera is a wonderful sense at a distance, but it fails at the one place manipulation cares about most: the contact. The moment a finger lands, the finger itself blocks the view of what it’s touching — and force, the quantity that decides whether the grip holds, was never visible to any camera.

Touch fills exactly that gap. A tactile sensor reports what is happening at the contact — how hard, where, which way it’s sliding, what the surface feels like — and it works in the dark, under occlusion, and on transparent or shiny objects that defeat vision entirely. It’s the sense the loop is otherwise missing.

the actual math — one level deeper

Optical sensing loses the contact: the fingertip occludes it, and contact force never projects to pixels — it is unobservable from vision alone.

A tactile sensor measures local quantities directly at the interface: normal/shear force, contact geometry, and incipient-slip signals.

02 · the sensor

A camera watching a soft skin

The breakthrough that made touch practical is almost funny: point a small camera at a piece of soft rubber. When the rubber presses against an object, it deforms to the object’s shape, and the camera films that deformation from the inside.

That gives a dense contact image — not one force number but a whole map of where the skin is pushed and how hard. A network reads it into contact geometry, force, and slip. It’s why vision-based tactile sensors took over: touch became a rich image, and all the machinery of computer vision applies to it.

the actual math — one level deeper

A soft skin deforms d = g(contact shape, force); a camera images the deformation (markers or surface), and a network inverts fθ: image → {force map, contact geometry}.

The output is a dense field, not a scalar — so vision architectures (CNNs, transformers) transfer directly to touch.

03 · grip control

Feel it start to slip

Holding an object is a control problem: grip too lightly and it slips, too hard and you crush it. The elegant solution is to grip lightly and react — because an object about to slide announces it first, with a burst of tiny tangential shear and micro-vibration at the contact.

A tactile sensor feels that incipient slip a fraction of a second before the object actually moves, and the controller tightens just enough to hold. Reacting in milliseconds to a signal that never reaches a camera is exactly the kind of control that makes touch indispensable.

the actual math — one level deeper

Slip = tangential displacement at the contact with the normal force roughly unchanged; incipient slip shows as rising shear / micro-vibration before gross motion.

Grip control raises normal force until the friction bound holds: required ‖f_t‖ ≤ μ f_n — react before, not after, the object moves.

04 · two senses

Eyes to reach, fingers to seat

Touch doesn’t replace vision; it completes it. Sight sees the whole scene and gets the robot roughly to the target, but it blurs at close range and is blocked the moment contact begins. Touch is the opposite: exquisitely precise, but blind to anything it isn’t currently touching.

So the winning pattern is fusion: use vision for the coarse, global approach and touch for the fine, local finish — the last millimetre of an insertion, the exact seating of a part. Each sense covers precisely where the other fails, which is why the strongest manipulation systems use both.

the actual math — one level deeper

Vision gives a global, low-frequency estimate; touch a local, high-frequency one — fuse them weighted by their uncertainties (e.g. a factor graph or Bayesian filter).

Even binary, low-resolution contact signals sharply reduce pose error under occlusion when fused with RGB-D.

05 · force control

Command a force, not a place

A robot that commands only position is brittle against contact: aim a hair too high and it floats above the surface; a hair too low and it gouges or jams. Real contact tasks — wiping, polishing, seating a connector — need the robot to command a force instead, and let its position comply to whatever surface it meets.

This is force (or impedance) control, and it’s only possible if the robot can feel force. Given touch, the robot follows an uneven, uncertain surface by pressing with a target force rather than chasing a target point — the control principle underneath every contact-rich skill.

the actual math — one level deeper

Impedance / force control commands a target wrench and lets motion comply: F = K(x* − x) with a force target, instead of tracking x* rigidly.

Contact-rich control regulates force through mode switches (stick/slip/separate), which requires measuring force — the sensor closes the loop.

Vision-based tactile sensing

both
the problem

A robot needs to know the exact shape of what it is touching, how hard it is pressing, and whether slip is starting — but the contact is hidden inside the gripper. A single force number from a wrist sensor gives no spatial map. What is needed is a picture of the contact from the inside, dense enough to read geometry, force, and slip simultaneously.

the approaches

Point a camera at a soft gel skin and read the contact from how the skin deforms — a dense image of where and how hard it touched. This is the dominant modern tactile sensor because it turns touch into a rich picture.

Work here is cross-sensor transfer, self-supervised features, and pairing touch with language models.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Press a penny against a 20 mm GelSight-style gel at 0.3 N normal force. The gel face deforms 0.4 mm inward at the coin's center.
  2. The embedded camera images that deformation. In the top-illuminated image, the coin's rim appears as a bright ring of radius 9 px; the face shows a 5×5 taxel-equivalent pressure map.
  3. A CNN trained on 460 k paired samples inverts the image: it reads the peak normal stress as 0.28 N mm⁻² and the contact area as 78 mm².
  4. The same network watches shear. When the coin starts to tilt, the marker grid shifts tangentially by 0.6 px — flagged as incipient slip at t = 47 ms, before the coin visibly moves.
  5. Transfer across sensors: T3 fine-tunes the same 3M-point pretrained backbone on 200 examples from a different gel brand and keeps 93% of original F1.

Soft & flexible sensors and skins

robotics
the problem

A rigid point sensor reports force at one spot; a whole robot arm needs contact information over its entire surface — so it can detect a collision at any point, hold a deformable object without breaking it, and bend around obstacles. Covering a large curved surface with rigid electronics that survive repeated impacts while staying calibrated is the hard part.

the approaches

Compliant sensors deform elastically under contact, and embedded sensing (capacitive, optical, magnetic, acoustic) reads that deformation — enabling dense skins that rigid point sensors can’t provide.

The push is durability, hot-swappable skins, and manufacturable whole-body coverage.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Mold a silicone sleeve (Shore 20A, 2 mm thick) around a 300 mm arm segment. Embed a 4×8 grid of capacitive taxels, pitch 15 mm.
  2. Connect via a flex PCB routed along the seam. The entire sleeve unplugs with one connector — hot-swap in under 30 s.
  3. Apply 0.5 N point load at taxel (3,5). The capacitance changes by 12 fF; adjacent taxels shift by 2–3 fF due to elastic cross-talk.
  4. Calibrate once with a 5×5 grid of known loads. A linear model corrects cross-talk, holding force error below 0.04 N over the full range.
  5. Transfer the policy: AnySkin's magnetic decoupling lets a grip policy trained on skin instance A transfer to instance B with zero retraining — the magnetic field pattern is instance-independent.

Force / torque sensing & estimation

robotics
the problem

Knowing total force at a wrist is useful, but it gives you a single 6-vector — no map of where force is applied, and no signal when fingers are the only contact. Inferring force from motor current avoids a dedicated sensor but confounds friction and inertia with real contact forces. The challenge is getting a clean force estimate from an indirect signal, across speeds and objects.

the approaches

Measure — or infer — the reaction forces and torques of an interaction, from load cells, strain gauges, or even motor currents. It reveals interaction dynamics that contact pressure alone can’t.

Sensorless force from current and vision-to-force prediction are recurring tricks.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. An underactuated gripper picks a 200 g cylinder. Motor current rises from 120 mA (free) to 340 mA at contact. No wrist F/T sensor is fitted.
  2. An LSTM with a 50 ms sliding window maps [current, velocity, joint angle] to grip force. Trained on 2 000 labeled trials.
  3. Predicted grip force: 3.4 N. Wrist F/T ground-truth: 3.6 N. Error = 0.2 N (5.6%).
  4. Bayesian fusion step: vision estimates friction coefficient μ = 0.42 from surface appearance. Closed-form update gives posterior μ = 0.38 ± 0.04, tightening the safe grip range.
  5. Output: the controller sets grip force to μ_posterior × mg / 2 = 0.38 × 1.96 / 2 ≈ 0.37 N safety margin above slip threshold.

  • ROBOYou've Got to Feel It: Multi-Modal Bayesian Inference for Property Prediction — closed-form Bayesian fusion of vision + force for friction
  • ROBOTransForce: Transferable Force Prediction for Vision-Based Tactile Sensors — predicts three-axis force from translated tactile images
  • ROBOForce Myography Based Torque Estimation in Knee and Ankle Joints — muscle-volume force sensing estimates joint torque for exoskeletons

Slip detection & grip control

robotics
the problem

Holding an object with minimum force means staying just above the slip threshold — but that threshold depends on the object's weight, surface friction, and the grip's geometry, none of which the robot knew in advance. Gripping too hard crushes fragile parts; too soft and the object falls. The only way to find the threshold without crushing or dropping is to detect the first sign of slip and react before the object moves.

the approaches

Slip is tangential motion at the contact without a change in pressing force. Tactile sensors feel it beginning — a shear/vibration burst — and the controller tightens the grip before the object falls.

High-resolution and fast sensing plus contact-mode control are the levers.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Robot picks a 120 g glass jar at initial grip force F_n = 1.8 N, well below the 3.1 N needed for μ = 0.35.
  2. Tactile sensor reports shear: tangential stress σ_t crosses 0.4 N mm⁻² at t = 0 ms. This is the incipient-slip marker.
  3. Vibration spike at t = 12 ms: the 200–400 Hz band of the signal jumps by 8 dB — second confirmation.
  4. Controller fires at t = 15 ms: increments F_n by 0.4 N per step (proportional-integral reflex). After 3 steps (45 ms total), F_n = 3.0 N and shear drops back below threshold.
  5. Jar never visibly moved. Without the tactile reflex, the jar would have slipped 4–6 mm before a camera could detect motion.

  • ROBOTactile-Driven Non-Prehensile Manipulation via Extrinsic Contact Mode Control — switches stick/slide by tactile gradient optimization
  • ROBOA Highly Robust Contact Sensor for Precise Fabric Contact Detection — 0.017 N resolution, 95% detection in <7.5 ms, noise-robust
  • ROBOEnhancing Two-Fingered Reorientation via Tactile Online Optimization — tactile-guided online optimization adapts to unknown contact

Contact-rich control & compliance

robotics
the problem

When a robot presses a tool against a surface — polishing, wiping, seating a connector — small position errors cause huge force spikes: a rigid arm aimed 0.5 mm too low crashes into the surface at hundreds of newtons. The robot needs to follow an unknown, curved surface at a target force, not a target position. Switching between contact modes (stick, slide, separate) mid-task requires knowing which mode you are in — only force feedback gives that.

the approaches

When the task is the contact — insertion, wiping, pressing — the robot must regulate force and compliance through sticking, sliding, and separation, not track a rigid position.

Fast contact solvers and learned compliance profiles handle the mode switches.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Target: wipe a 40 mm curved surface at F_n = 5 N. Surface radius of curvature unknown, ±3 mm height variation.
  2. Impedance controller: F = K(x_surface - x_tool) with K = 800 N/m (compliant spring), damping B = 12 N·s/m.
  3. At the first crest, tool rises 2 mm above the nominal path. With pure position control this would float; with force control the controller extends 2.5 mm to keep F_n = 5 N.
  4. At the first trough, tool would crash 2 mm in; force control retracts 2.4 mm. Force stays within ±0.6 N of target throughout.
  5. ADMM contact solver (From Compliant to Rigid) detects stick-to-slide transition at t = 0.18 s and switches the friction model, keeping the trajectory smooth through the mode switch.

  • ROBOTowards Tight Convex Relaxations for Contact-Rich Manipulation — semidefinite relaxation of nonconvex hybrid contact dynamics
  • ROBOAdaptive Compliance Policy: Learning Compliance for Diffusion Control — learns spatiotemporal compliance from demonstrations

Vision-tactile fusion

both
the problem

Vision sees the whole scene from a distance but is blocked the moment a finger lands. Touch sees exactly what is at the contact but is blind to the rest of the world. For a task like inserting a USB plug, you need both: sight to bring the plug near the port, touch to feel the alignment in the last millimetre when the camera is blocked. Fusing two signals with very different spatial scale and timing is the hard problem.

the approaches

Vision sees shape and pose from afar; touch senses force and fine geometry locally. Fusing them disambiguates state: occlusion doesn’t fool touch, and touch can’t see what’s across the room.

Pretraining, unpaired fusion, and test-time physics all combine the two streams.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. USB-A insertion task. RGB-D gives plug pose to within ±1.8 mm, ±3° at 50 mm distance — good enough to approach.
  2. At 5 mm from the port, the gripper occludes the view. Touch takes over: GelSight reads contact geometry, estimating misalignment as 0.6 mm lateral, 1.2° tilt.
  3. VITaL pretrained backbone fuses the two streams in a shared latent space (256-dim). Fusion improves alignment estimation to ±0.2 mm vs ±0.8 mm touch-only.
  4. Policy executes 3 corrective micro-motions of 0.3 mm each, guided by the fused estimate. Plug seats on attempt 1.
  5. Success rate: vision-only 20%, touch-only 51%, fused (VITaL) 85% — a number from the paper.
  • ROBOVITaL Pretraining: Visuo-Tactile Pretraining for Manipulation Policies — paired visuo-tactile pretraining lifts USB-plug success 20%→85%
  • ROBO3D-ViTac: Fine-Grained Manipulation with Visuo-Tactile Sensing — fuses touch + vision in a shared 3D space for bimanual tasks
  • ROBOViTa-Zero: Zero-Shot Visuotactile 6D Pose Estimation — test-time physics optimization, 80% lower position error
  • ROBOVisuo-Tactile Object Pose Estimation with Low-Resolution Tactile — a factor graph fuses RGB-D + binary tactile under occlusion

Tactile representation learning

robotics
the problem

Raw tactile images are high-dimensional (a GelSight frame is 480×640 pixels), sensor-specific, and expensive to label — you cannot afford a new dataset for every sensor or task. What is needed is a learned code that compresses the tactile signal into a small vector that transfers across sensors without retraining, the same way a vision pretrained backbone transfers across cameras.

the approaches

Raw tactile images are high-dimensional, sensor-specific, and expensive. Learned latent codes and implicit functions compress touch while enabling cross-sensor transfer and probabilistic inference.

Foundation models across many sensors and tasks are emerging.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. T3 pretraining: 3 million tactile frames from 13 different sensors, masked autoencoder objective. Encoder: ViT-S/16, 384-dim output.
  2. Target task: USB insertion on a new sensor (GelSight Mini) not seen during pretraining.
  3. Baseline (train from scratch): 500 labeled trials needed to reach 60% success.
  4. T3 fine-tuned on 20 labeled trials: reaches 78% success — 25× fewer labels, +18 pp over scratch.
  5. Sparsh masking: mask 75% of a GelSight frame at random, train the encoder to reconstruct. After 460 k frames, the resulting features beat task-specific CNNs on 6 of 7 downstream probes with no task labels at all.
  • ROBOT3: Transferable Tactile Transformers Across Sensors and Tasks — 3M-point pretraining over 13 sensors, 25–53% gains on insertion
  • ROBOTactile Functasets: Neural Implicit Representations of Tactile Data — meta-trained implicit codes compress datasets, enable inference
  • ROBOSparsh: Self-Supervised Touch Representations — masking + self-distillation yields task-agnostic tactile features
  • ROBOShape-Space Deformer: Unified Visuo-Tactile Representations — a unified latent shape generalizes to unseen forces and objects

Tactile for insertion & assembly

robotics
the problem

Press a peg into a hole 0.1 mm tighter than the peg and it jams — force spikes, the robot stops, and nobody knows why. A camera watching from outside cannot see the contact; wrist force gives only a 6-vector with no spatial map. Touch at the finger tip can detect which corner of the hole is catching, which side is binding, and how much clearance is left — and it can do so in the 10–20 ms before forces become dangerous.

the approaches

Assembly needs sub-millimetre precision under contact constraints. Touch detects jamming, binding, and misalignment before large forces build, enabling reactive force-position control vision can’t provide.

Anomaly detection on contact states and force-aware primitives are the tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Peg: 6 mm shaft, 0.05 mm clearance, stainless steel. Target: insert 20 mm in under 2 s.
  2. Contact state machine: CC-STAR's CNN-VAE encodes the tactile image. Reconstruction error jumps from 0.02 to 0.14 at the first jam — mode transition detected at t = 0.31 s.
  3. Force-aware ProDMP reads the detected jam and replans: back off 0.5 mm, rotate 0.8° about Z, re-approach.
  4. Second attempt seats the peg without exceeding 8 N insertion force (safe limit: 12 N). Total time: 1.7 s.
  5. Without tactile feedback: the robot kept pushing, hit 30 N in 0.4 s, and triggered an emergency stop 8 of 10 times.

Whole-body skins & human-robot interaction

robotics
the problem

A robot arm that can only feel at its fingertips will miss a collision anywhere else on its body — it will crush a bystander's hand, snag a cable, or fail to detect that a person is guiding its elbow. Covering the entire arm and torso with touch requires sensing everywhere, wiring it all up, keeping it calibrated, and processing hundreds of channels in real time at enough bandwidth to stop before damage occurs.

the approaches

Cover the whole robot in touch and it can feel a collision anywhere — the basis for safe collaboration — and haptic feedback tells a human operator what the robot feels.

The work is designing, fabricating, and processing distributed sensing at scale.

  4. Safety reflex fires: arm velocity drops to zero in 12 ms, well within ISO/TS 15066 reaction-time requirements.

Teleoperation & haptic feedback

robotics
the problem

A surgeon or bomb-disposal technician teleoperating a robot needs to feel what the robot's hands feel — the resistance of tissue, the grip of a connector, the moment something is about to slip. Without that feedback, they are flying blind. The physics challenge is stability: adding a force-reflection channel between operator and robot can excite oscillations under communication delay, so the control loop must ensure the combined system dissipates energy regardless of the delay.

the approaches

Send a human’s motion to a robot and reflect the robot’s contact forces back to the hand — so the operator feels the remote world. Passivity and impedance adaptation keep it stable under delay.

Novel actuators and ungrounded interfaces widen the workspace.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Bilateral teleoperation setup: operator hand exoskeleton (haptic glove) + robot arm separated by 40 ms simulated round-trip delay.
  2. Operator squeezes a soft gel block via the robot. Robot wrist F/T reads 2.4 N at 60% compression.
  3. Passivity filter (variable-impedance passivity paper) checks energy budget: if reflected force would add energy to the operator side beyond the passivity bound, it clips the signal. At 40 ms delay, clipping kicks in for ~12% of force samples.
  4. Operator perceives a smooth, stable squish — not the chatter a naive direct reflection would produce. Perceived stiffness error vs ground-truth: 0.18 N mm⁻¹.
  5. Ungrounded haptic interface (cutaneous-only version): electromagnetic torque coil on the fingertip gives 200 mm free-space workspace vs 30 mm for a grounded device — key for full-range tool use.

Tactile sim-to-real & learning

robotics
the problem

Tactile simulation is harder than visual simulation: a camera rendering only needs photometric accuracy, but a tactile simulator must correctly predict how a gel deforms under a specific contact shape and force, including stick-slip transitions and micro-vibration. The resulting tactile sim-to-real gap is larger than the visual gap, so policies trained purely in simulation fail more often on real tactile hardware than vision-based policies fail on real cameras.

the approaches

Tactile simulators predict sensor deformation from contact, but the tactile sim-to-real gap is larger than vision’s — force profiles, slip, and materials transfer poorly without adaptation.

Force-motion capture and multi-sensory stage fusion help ground learning in reality.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. ForceMimic capture: human demonstrator wears a wrist-mounted force plate and fingertip pressure array. Peeling a sticker off a surface: 14 demonstrations, each 3–4 s, capturing force trajectory + finger video.
  2. Imitation policy trained on captured force trajectories (ProDMP backbone). Tested on a new sticker brand with different adhesion.
  3. Force-only policy success rate: 54.5% higher than vision-only (paper number). Force captures the peeling dynamics vision cannot see.
  4. Multi-sensory stage fusion (Play to the Score): at peel-start stage, touch weight = 0.7, vision = 0.3. At lift-off stage, vision weight jumps to 0.8 — each stage uses the most informative sense.
  5. Uncertainty-aware RL (Occlusion paper): tactile estimator outputs a Gaussian uncertainty; RL policy penalizes high-uncertainty actions, reducing gripper contact force variance by 31%.

Tactile shape & stiffness sensing

both
the problem

Stiffness — how force grows with deformation — varies enormously across materials (100 kPa for soft tissue, 200 GPa for steel), and it is invisible from a camera. Knowing it matters: the right grip force for a grape is 0.5 N; for a nut it is 30 N. Measuring stiffness by touch requires pressing at known force, measuring deformation, and fitting a mechanical model — the sensor must be calibrated absolutely, not just relatively.

the approaches

Press and probe, and touch reveals what vision can’t: an object’s 3D surface where it’s occluded, and its stiffness — how force grows with deformation. This is touch used to reconstruct, not just to grip.

Neural stiffness fields, active exploration, and imagined-touch completion drive it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Press a visuotactile sensor against a silicone phantom (known Young's modulus E = 50 kPa) with 5 force steps from 0.1 to 0.5 N.
  2. Deformation image at 0.5 N shows a 6 mm contact diameter and 0.8 mm central depression. Hertzian model: E* = 3F / (4√R · d^1.5) = 52 kPa (2% error).
  3. Neural stiffness field (Estimating High-Resolution paper): continuous MLP predicts stiffness at any query point. Trained on 500 probe locations, it maps a heterogeneous phantom in 120 s total probing time.
  4. Active exploration (Proactive Tactile paper): information-gain policy picks probe sites. It achieves 90% reconstruction accuracy with 28 contacts vs 80 for random sampling.
  5. TouchDream completion: given 5 real tactile readings on an occluded object, diffusion model hallucinates the remaining surface geometry. Chamfer distance to ground-truth: 4.2 mm vs 11.8 mm for nearest-neighbor completion.

  • ROBOProactive Tactile Exploration for Shape Reconstruction from Minimal Priors — active touch minimizes contacts to reconstruct shape

Contact modes & hybrid dynamics

robotics
the problem

An object resting on a table can stick to the surface, slide freely, roll, or separate — and which mode it is in determines what forces are possible and what motions are legal. Planning a manipulation sequence means predicting which mode the object will be in after each push or tilt. The challenge is that the mode boundaries are not known in advance: they depend on friction, normal force, and geometry that the robot has to measure or infer from contact.

the approaches

Objects switch between sticking, sliding, rolling, and separating as forces change. Reasoning over these discrete modes — and their friction constraints — is what makes contact planning tractable.

Diffusion over contacts and contact-implicit MPC are current approaches.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task: slide a book across a table, 0.3 kg, unknown μ. Target: 150 mm translation in 2 s.
  2. CDM contact diffusion: observe the book at rest; diffusion model samples 50 hypotheses for the contact point distribution. Median contact centroid converges to within 8 mm of ground truth in 3 frames.
  3. Infer μ: push at 1 N for 0.1 s. If book moves > 2 mm, μ < 0.34 (slide mode). Book moves 3.1 mm → μ ≈ 0.28.
  4. Contact-implicit MPC (Dynamic On-Palm paper) plans a 0.15 s horizon, explicitly optimizing over stick/slide mode sequence. Chooses: slide for 1.2 s, then stick to stop. Achieves target with 4 mm position error.
  5. Hamilton-Jacobi reachability (legged robot version) pre-computes which contact-mode sequences are guaranteed safe under μ ∈ [0.25, 0.45], giving a certified safety margin for the whole trajectory.
  • ROBOCDM: Contact Diffusion Model for Multi-Contact Point Localization — diffusion resolves multi-contact ambiguity to sub-cm accuracy
  • ROBOImplicit Contact Diffuser: Sequential Contact Reasoning — latent contact-descriptor diffusion guides MPC
  • ROBODynamic On-Palm Manipulation via Controlled Sliding — contact-implicit MPC exploits sliding modes
  • ROBOHamilton-Jacobi Reachability for Hybrid Systems with Transitions — reachability over contact modes for legged robots

Touch in vision: material, contact & haptics

vision
the problem

Vision models can see a surface but cannot feel it. A rendered 3D scene has no haptic channel: you can fly through it but not feel the texture of the wall or the bounce of the floor. And a video of someone picking up a glass tells nothing about the forces — yet humans watching that video can predict whether the glass is heavy. The challenge is to infer or synthesize the tactile signal from purely visual input.

the approaches

The CVPR side treats touch as something to infer or synthesize from vision: estimate contact and force from a video, generate the haptic feel of a rendered scene, or read body motion from floor pressure.

It skews toward hand-object contact, physics-plausible interaction, and haptic synthesis.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Haptic Neural Fields: a 3DGS scene of a wooden table is trained. At contact point (x, y, z) = (0.3, 0, 0.7 m), the haptic field predicts vibrotactile frequency f = 180 Hz, amplitude A = 0.12 g for a fingertip sliding at 20 mm/s.
  2. Ground-truth measurement with a real fingertip accelerometer: 172 Hz, 0.11 g. Error < 5% for frequency, 8% for amplitude.
  3. Pressure2Motion: 16 floor pressure sensors at 100 Hz capture a person walking. A graph neural network decodes hip and knee joint angles with 4.3° mean angular error — comparable to marker-based mocap at 1/20th the setup cost.
  4. AT-VLA tactile gate: a GelSight signal is injected into a VLA backbone via a learnable 64-dim adapter. The model closes the contact-force feedback loop in 0.04 s — 8× faster than a policy that waits for a visual frame.
  5. ForceVLA2 mixture-of-experts: routes between a position-control head and a force-control head based on contact state. On a peg-in-hole task, it reduces peak insertion force from 18 N to 6 N.
  • CVPRHaptic Neural Fields: Tactile Interactions in 3D Rendered Scenes — maps contact trajectories to vibrotactile signals in NeRF/3DGS scenes
  • CVPRPressure2Motion: Human Motion Reconstruction from Ground Pressure — decodes sparse floor pressure into full-body motion, privacy-preserving
  • CVPRForceVLA2: Hybrid Force-Position Control with Force Awareness — a force-aware mixture-of-experts for contact-rich manipulation
  • CVPRAT-VLA: Adaptive Tactile Injection into Vision-Language-Action Models — a learnable tactile gate, 0.04 s closed-loop reaction

Part IV

Where it stands

Giving robots a reliable sense of touch is younger and rougher than giving them sight. What is genuinely still hard:

Sensors that are precise and durable. Vision-based tactile is precise but fragile; soft skins are robust but hard to calibrate — no cheap, durable sensor works reliably in the field.
The tactile sim-to-real gap. Touch transfers across sensors and from simulation far worse than vision; policies rarely move between sensor types without retraining.
Real-time high-rate touch. Tactile arrays stream at hundreds of hertz; fusing that with vision at aligned rates, in a control loop, is largely unsolved.
Synthetic vs. real signals. Much recent work uses simulated or ‘imagined’ touch; robustness to real sensor noise and heterogeneity is understudied.
Whole-body touch at scale. Fabricating, wiring, and processing dense skin over a whole robot drains power and bandwidth — design is automated, manufacturing isn’t.
Interpreting what touch means. Tactile learning is opaque; connecting a raw contact signal to a symbolic outcome (jammed, seated, slipping) for reliable control is still hard.
