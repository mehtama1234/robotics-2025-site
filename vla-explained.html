01 · the goal

One model, commanded in words

For years a robot skill meant a bespoke policy: collect data for this task, train a model for this task, repeat. It doesn’t scale — the world has more tasks than you can ever script.

A vision-language-action model flips that. It is one model that takes what the robot sees and an instruction in ordinary words, and outputs an action. The language is the task selector: ‘fold the towel’, ‘pour the cup’, ‘open the drawer’ all go to the same network. New tasks become new sentences, not new models.

the actual math — one level deeper

A VLA is a policy π(a | o, ℓ) — action a given observation o and a language instruction ℓ.

One set of weights covers every task; the instruction ℓ conditions the behavior, replacing a separate πᵗₜₛₖ per task.

02 · the backbone

Stand on a model that already knows the world

Where does the intelligence come from? Not from robot demonstrations — there are far too few of those. It comes from the internet. A vision-language model (a VLM) pretrained on billions of image-text pairs already recognizes a mug, knows what ‘sink’ means, and has a rough grasp of how the world works.

A VLA adapts that pretrained VLM into an actor: keep its perception and language understanding, bolt on a way to output actions, and fine-tune. The payoff is generalization — because the backbone learned the world from the web, the robot can handle objects and phrasings it never saw in any robot dataset.

the actual math — one level deeper

Initialize from a pretrained VLM θᵛₗₘ (image+text → text), trained on billions of web image-text pairs.

Attach an action decoder and fine-tune on robot data: the perception/language weights start from θᵛₗₘ, not from scratch.

Generalization comes from that prior — representations for objects and words the robot data never contained.

03 · the output

How a language model outputs an action

A language model produces tokens, but a robot needs continuous motor commands. Bridging that gap is the defining design choice of a VLA.

The first way is action tokenization: quantize each dimension of the action into a small vocabulary, and let the model predict those tokens exactly as it predicts words — simple, and it reuses the language machinery. The second is a continuous head: hang a diffusion or flow model off the backbone that denoises a smooth action (this is the seam with diffusion policies). Tokens are simple and cheap; continuous heads are smoother and naturally multimodal — and flow-matching heads are what make the newest VLAs fast.

the actual math — one level deeper

Discrete: bin each action dimension into K tokens; model p(aₜ | o, ℓ, a₃ₜ) autoregressively, like text.

Continuous: a diffusion/flow head samples a ~ p(a | o, ℓ) by denoising — smoother, multimodal (see the diffusion-policies explainer).

Tokenizing reuses the LM’s softmax; continuous heads add expressiveness at some inference cost (flow matching cuts it back).

04 · the data

Keep the web, add the robot

There is a trap. Fine-tune the pretrained VLM only on the small pile of robot demonstrations and it forgets — the very language and world knowledge you wanted bleeds away, a problem called catastrophic forgetting.

The fix is co-training: during fine-tuning, keep feeding the model web data (visual question answering, captioning) mixed in with the robot demos. The web stream — billions of examples — holds the language grounding steady while the thin robot stream teaches it to act. The mix is what lets a VLA both understand ‘the striped mug’ and reach for it.

the actual math — one level deeper

Co-training loss: L = λ·L_web(VQA / caption) + (1−λ)·L_robot(action).

The web term regularizes the backbone against catastrophic forgetting of language while the robot term teaches control.

Ratios like ~90% web / ~10% robot are common; some methods learn a latent action space from unlabeled video to stretch the robot data further.

05 · reason first

Think, then move

A VLM is brilliant at semantics (‘that’s the mug’) and weak at geometry (‘it’s 12 cm to your left, tilt 20°’). Ask it to jump straight from a loose instruction to a precise action and it stumbles on exactly the spatial part a body needs.

So the frontier is reason-then-act: have the model first think out loud — find the object, lay out sub-steps, place waypoints in 3D — and only then emit the action, conditioned on that reasoning. Decoupling the ‘what’ from the ‘how’ makes VLAs markedly more robust on long tasks, and the reasoning trace is readable, so you can see why the robot did what it did.

the actual math — one level deeper

Reason-then-act factorizes the policy: p(z | o, ℓ) · p(a | o, ℓ, z), where z is a reasoning trace (text CoT, sub-goals, or 3D waypoints).

z can be explicit text (readable, auditable) or a compact latent (faster). Tree-of-thought evaluates several z before committing.

Decoupling ‘what’ (z) from ‘how’ (a) improves long-horizon success and interpretability.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every family below leans on one or more of these — the recurring reasons the field bet on vision-language-action models, in the words the papers keep using.

Generalizes via web knowledge

Because the backbone learned the world from the internet, a VLA handles objects, words, and situations that never appear in any robot dataset — zero-shot to the unfamiliar.

One model, many tasks & bodies

A single policy, commanded in language, spans manipulation, navigation, locomotion, even flight, and transfers across robot embodiments — no per-task retraining.

Open-vocabulary

Ask for ‘the striped mug’ or ‘the thing you drink from’ and it grounds the words — no fixed list of object classes.

Common-sense reasoning

Corrects from language

Say ‘no, the other one’ or ‘a bit higher’ and the policy adapts on the fly — language is a live correction channel, not just an instruction.

01

Generalist VLA policies

robotics
the problem

Training a separate policy per robot and per task doesn’t scale — you’d need a dataset and a model for every combination, forever.

the approaches

The core recipe: adapt a pretrained VLM so one network maps image + instruction to action, across many tasks and even many robot bodies. Trained on giant cross-embodiment demonstration mixes, a single policy replaces a zoo of specialists.

These are the open backbones the rest of the field builds on.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Pool demonstrations from many robots and many tasks into one giant mixed dataset.
  2. Encode every robot’s observations and actions into a shared format.
  3. Fine-tune one pretrained VLM on the whole mix, with the instruction as conditioning.
  4. Deploy the single set of weights on any of the robots.
  5. A new task is a new sentence; a new robot needs only a little adaptation data.
  • ROBO
    OpenVLA — open-source 7B VLA on 970k demos with efficient adapter fine-tuning

    VLAs are huge — billions of parameters — so deploying them on real robots is slow. This work asks: can we keep the pretrained backbone and fine-tune it cheaply? The answer is adapter layers: frozen LLM weights with trainable lightweight adapters injected at key points. On 970k demonstrations across tasks and robots, efficient fine-tuning wins: one open model, all the generalization, a fraction of the cost.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Start with a 7B pretrained VLM frozen solid. Inject learnable adapter modules — typically linear projections or bottleneck layers — at attention heads and feed-forward blocks. During fine-tuning, only these adapters move; the backbone stays locked. This cuts the tuned parameters from billions to millions while preserving the web knowledge.

    Why it works: The pretrained backbone already understands vision and language. Adapters learn the thin film of task-specific alignment — how to read the observation for this robot's morphology and what action tokens mean in this context. The backbone's prior is strong enough that adapters alone suffice, so you avoid catastrophic forgetting and can deploy on modest hardware.

  • ROBO
    Octo — transformer policy pretrained on 800k Open X-Embodiment trajectories; language or goal-image conditioned (the one we run in the diffusion explainer)

    Most VLAs are trained on a single dataset. This work collects and unifies the Open X-Embodiment dataset: 800k trajectories from 22 different robot morphologies (arms, legs, wheeled bases). A single transformer policy learns to handle them all, conditioned on either language instructions or goal images. This is the generalist backbone that appears in diffusion-policy papers and becomes industry-standard.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Take a transformer encoder-decoder. Input: a stack of 6 image frames + action history + language instruction or goal image. Output: 8 action dimensions, predicted autoregressively. The key innovation is the robot-agnostic action space: normalize each robot's actions into a common range (e.g., joint-space with gripper), so the model sees actions from a humanoid, a quadruped, and a mobile manipulator as points in the same learned manifold.

    Why it works: By training on such diverse embodiments, the model learns what generalizes: semantic understanding of the scene (from web pretraining) and kinematic principles (from the mix). Deploy on a new robot with a few hundred demos, and it learns rapidly because the backbone already grasps the common structure.

  • ROBO
    Scaling Cross-Embodied Learning — one network drives arms, quadrupeds, wheeled and aerial robots from 900k trajectories

    Training on one robot teaches one morphology. Can you scale to many? This paper takes 900k trajectories from arms, quadrupeds, wheeled robots, and aerial vehicles, pooling them into one dataset. A single policy emerges that generalizes to any of them — sometimes zero-shot, always with less data than a specialist would need. The key: shared representations of action and perception at the right abstraction level.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Represent observations (images, proprioception) and actions in a robot-agnostic latent space. For images, use a pretrained vision backbone; for actions, normalize by morphology-specific scaling. Train a single policy network on the mixed data, and during inference, condition on task instructions and robot identity (or infer it from the proprioceptive signal). The policy learns generalizable primitives (reach, grasp, move) that apply across embodiments.

    Why it works: When the representation layer handles the morphology translation, the policy learns task-level semantics that transfer. A 'reach' means something across all robots even though joint angles differ; the representation layer maps that semantic primitive to each body's kinematics.

  • ROBO
    A3VLM — an articulation-aware VLM predicts affordances, cutting the robot data needed

    Saying 'open it' to a robot is useless unless the robot knows this object is a hinged door, where the hinge axis is, and how to grip the handle. A3VLM (articulation-aware VLM) predicts not just object identity but articulation structure: this part is the handle, this axis rotates, this rotates up to 90 degrees. That prediction transfers to new doors the robot never saw, because the geometric reasoning about articulation is object-general.

    how it actually works

    The problem: 'Open it' requires understanding part geometry and constraints, not just object type. The mechanism: Given an image of an object and an instruction, the VLM predicts a structured affordance: which part is the handle (bounding box), where is the hinge axis (line in 3D), what is the motion primitive (rotate, slide, pull). Convert these into constraints for motion planning. Why it works: Articulation structure is shared across instances (all hinges rotate around an axis), so predicting structure instead of memorizing per-object behavior generalizes to unseen objects.

  • ROBO
    Harmon — a VLM refines human-motion priors into text-aligned humanoid whole-body motion

    Humanoid robots move like humans, so human motion priors are powerful. This work takes human motion capture data — whole-body motion sequences — and uses language descriptions to align them with instructions. The pretrained VLM learns to refine and ground those human priors into robot commands. The result: a humanoid policy that moves naturally and generalizes to new tasks faster because it inherits human kinematic structure.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Collect mocap sequences of humans performing tasks (reach, pick, place) and annotate each with language descriptions ('pick up the mug', 'move to the left'). Use a VLM to encode the language and the mocap (as joint sequences). Train the model to predict the next human pose given the instruction and context. Adapt this to robot kinematics by learning a morphology-aware projection from mocap space to robot joint space.

    Why it works: Human motion is a strong prior for humanoid form factors; mocap data is abundant; language descriptions are cheap to collect. By anchoring robot learning to human motion, the policy inherits natural, efficient movements. The VLM's language grounding ensures the human priors are correctly applied to the instruction.

02

Action representation: tokens vs continuous heads

both
the problem

Robot action-labeled data is tiny, but the internet has endless unlabeled video of things happening. Can that video teach a policy to act?

the approaches

A VLM emits text, so how do you get a continuous action out? Discretize it into tokens the model predicts like words, or attach a diffusion/flow head that denoises a smooth action. The choice sets a VLA’s smoothness, expressiveness, and speed.

Flow-matching heads are the current sweet spot — multimodal and fast enough for real control.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Take unlabeled video (web or human).
  2. Extract how things move between frames — e.g., optical flow — as a proxy for action.
  3. Learn a latent action space that explains that motion, shared across video and robot data.
  4. Pretrain the VLA to predict these latent actions from huge video.
  5. Fine-tune with a little real robot data to map latent actions to motor commands.
  • CVPR
    MM-ACT — a unified discrete-token VLA with parallel decoding for text/image/action at 40Hz

    VLAs emit text, but robots need simultaneous outputs: language reasoning, images (for visualization), and action tokens, all in one go. This paper unifies them: a single transformer predicts text, image patches, and action tokens in parallel at 40Hz. The multimodal output lets the model reason (in language), show its thinking (in images), and act — all coherently without sequential bottlenecks.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Extend the transformer's output head to emit three token streams in parallel. Discretize actions into K bins per dimension (e.g., 256 bins for each joint), images into patch tokens (like VQVAE), and language as usual. During training, minimize cross-entropy on all three streams jointly. At inference, sample from the action distribution autoregressively but grab the language and image tokens in parallel.

    Why it works: Parallel decoding is faster than sequential — no latency pipeline. Unifying modalities in one model lets the policy align reasoning and action implicitly; the transformer's self-attention mechanism keeps them consistent. The learned action tokens are now part of a rich representation that also captures language semantics and visual reasoning.

  • CVPR
    Motus — learns a latent action space from video via optical flow to bridge web and robot data

    Robot data is scarce but internet video is abundant. Yet video has no action labels. This work learns a latent action space from unlabeled video by watching how things move (optical flow). A VLA pretrained on that latent space then fine-tunes on scarce robot data and already understands many behaviors. Internet scale meets robot precision.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Extract optical flow from unlabeled video sequences. (2) Train an autoencoder to compress flow into a compact latent action code z. (3) Pretrain a VLA to predict z from images and language on huge video corpora. (4) Fine-tune the decoder z → robot actions on small robot datasets. The VLA learns rich motion semantics from video, then adapts the final layer to robot kinematics.

    Why it works: The latent space captures general dynamics — 'reach toward', 'rotate', 'grasp' — learned from millions of videos. That's a powerful prior. Fine-tuning only the action decoder means robot data only needs to teach the morphology-specific translation, not the whole policy. Pretraining on latent actions from video is much more data-efficient than random initialization.

  • CVPR
    WAM-Flow — discrete flow matching generates trajectories in parallel, 3× faster than autoregressive

    Action tokenization is simple but autoregressive prediction is slow — one token per step. Diffusion and flow matching can sample entire action trajectories in parallel. This paper applies discrete flow matching to action tokens: generate all action tokens at once, not sequentially, three times faster than autoregressive while staying multimodal and sample-diverse.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Model the action sequence as a flow — a straight path in latent space from noise to data. The flow-matching model learns to predict the velocity field along that path. At inference, start with noise and follow the velocity field (with ODE solver or learned step function) to generate the full sequence in parallel. Discretize the continuous trajectory into action tokens post-hoc.

    Why it works: Parallel generation is fast; flow matching converges faster than diffusion because it skips the noise-to-data detour. Since all action dimensions are generated jointly (not autoregressively), they're implicitly correlated — the trajectory is naturally smooth. Multimodality emerges from sample diversity in the noise; the model learns the conditional distribution, not a point estimate.

  • ROBO
    Equivariant Diffusion Policy — a continuous (diffusion) action head with built-in symmetry (see the diffusion-policies explainer)

    A diffusion head on a VLA is general but can ignore symmetry — rotating an object 90 degrees shouldn't require relearning the grasp. This paper bakes equivariance into the diffusion process: the noise schedule, the denoise steps, and the action output respect rotations and reflections. More data-efficient, more reliable to perturbations.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Parameterize actions as SE(3) transformations (position + rotation) or their local frames. Build the diffusion model to respect group symmetries: noise is applied equivariantly, the denoiser network uses equivariant layers (e.g., via Lie algebra or steerable CNNs), and the output is an equivariant action. During training, the diffusion loss is symmetric under the group.

    Why it works: When the model respects symmetry, it learns less and generalizes more. A grasp trained on one orientation automatically works at others (up to the gripper's constraints). This cuts the data needed to cover pose space and improves reliability to viewpoint changes, which is common in real deployment where cameras shift.

03

LLM task planning & decomposition

robotics
the problem

A single instruction like ‘make coffee’ hides a dozen steps and constraints a flat policy can’t juggle.

the approaches

Use a language model as the high-level planner: decompose a long instruction into steps, each calling a low-level skill, with the LLM reasoning over constraints and feasibility.

This is the ‘brain on top of skills’ pattern — language for the plan, learned or scripted controllers for the execution.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Give the instruction to an LLM planner.
  2. It decomposes the goal into ordered sub-steps, each a call to a known skill.
  3. It checks each step against constraints (reachable? object present? safe?).
  4. If a step is infeasible, it re-plans or asks for the missing piece.
  5. Low-level controllers execute each skill; the plan sequences them.
  • ROBO
    Trust the PRoC3S — the LLM emits parameterized skill code; a constraint solver makes it kinematically feasible

    Making coffee needs many steps: boil water, grind beans, brew, pour. A flat VLA struggles with long horizons. This paper uses an LLM as a high-level planner: it emits parameterized skill code (e.g., 'Grasp(obj=mug, type=handle)'), and a constraint solver makes sure each step is kinematically feasible. Language plans the high level, solvers guarantee low-level realizability.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Give the task instruction to a language model. It outputs pseudocode: a sequence of high-level skills with parameters (e.g., grasp_pos, target_pose). For each step, a symbolic constraint solver (e.g., inverse kinematics, collision checking) verifies feasibility and computes the robot's joint solution. If a step is infeasible (unreachable), the planner backtracks and tries a different decomposition.

    Why it works: LLMs excel at semantic decomposition; solvers excel at geometry and physics. By splitting the labor, each stage works with its strength. The planner doesn't need to learn to compute IK; the solver doesn't need to understand language. Sequential planning also makes debugging transparent: you can see and audit each step.

  • ROBO
    AutoGPT+P — LLM affordance mapping + a symbolic planner handles missing objects and incomplete info

    Planning fails when objects are missing or the world is incomplete. An LLM can reason about affordances ('I need the mug, which is usually in the cabinet'), but it can't act on pure reasoning. This work combines LLM affordance mapping ('if mug is needed, look in cabinet') with symbolic planning, letting the planner request missing information and adapt as the world unfolds.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) LLM produces a task plan with affordance annotations (e.g., 'grasp(mug, cabinet)' where cabinet is the assumed location). (2) Symbolic planner tries to execute it. (3) If a precondition fails (cabinet empty), the planner queries the LLM for a fallback. (4) The LLM revises the plan using domain knowledge (object locations, alternative approaches). (5) Repeat until execution succeeds or a human intervention is needed.

    Why it works: The LLM's common sense handles incomplete information; the planner's verification catches infeasibility early. Interaction between them closes the loop: planning reveals gaps, the LLM fills them, planning re-verifies. This is more reliable than a single-pass LLM plan or a planner with no world knowledge.

  • ROBO
    Language-Augmented Symbolic Planner — LLM error diagnosis feeds a symbolic planner that grows its domain knowledge

    Symbolic planners need accurate domain models (PDDL predicates), which are hand-authored and brittle. This paper uses LLM error diagnosis to grow the domain: when a plan fails, ask the LLM why, use its explanation to add or refine predicates, and re-plan. Over time, the planner's world model becomes richer and more accurate without manual annotation.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Start with a minimal symbolic domain (e.g., locations, objects, reachability). (2) Execute a plan. (3) If it fails (e.g., pick fails), feed the failure trace to an LLM: 'Grasp failed at mug in cabinet. Why?' (4) The LLM diagnoses: 'The mug is too far back; the gripper can't reach it.' (5) Extract that as a new predicate: reachable(gripper, obj) ∧ depth(obj) < threshold. (6) Re-plan with the richer model.

    Why it works: LLMs are good at explaining failure modes; symbolic planners are good at using that information to avoid the same pitfall. This closes a knowledge-collection loop: live experience reveals gaps, language reasoning diagnoses them, and the planner internalizes the fix. Over thousands of episodes, the domain grows automatically.

  • ROBO
    INTERPRET — learns PDDL predicates from language feedback to generalize task planning

    Planning from language requires learning the task structure: which objects matter, which predicates define the state, what sequence of actions works. Instead of hand-authoring PDDL, this work learns predicates from language feedback. Tell the robot 'that's wrong, I meant the other mug' and it updates its predicate for 'mug' — the model learns to generalize task structure from corrections.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) A learner outputs a PDDL-like predicate for each task concept (e.g., 'target-mug' is a learned linear classifier over language embeddings and scene features). (2) Execute a plan. (3) Human corrects: 'No, this mug.' (4) Use that correction to refine the predicate: update its weights to distinguish the intended mug from the predicted one. (5) Replan and re-execute.

    Why it works: By treating predicates as learnable classifiers, the model can adapt to user intent from a few corrections. Language feedback directly updates the predicate representation, so the planner learns to discriminate the right objects and states. Over a task sequence, predicates generalize: once you teach 'mug' once, it works for all mugs.

04

Open-vocabulary manipulation

robotics
the problem

You can’t pre-train a grasp detector for every object in the world — and users will ask for things by any name.

the approaches

Grasp and manipulate objects the robot was never trained on, named in open language, by having a VLM reason about parts, affordances, and how to point at them.

The trick is turning ‘pick up the striped mug’ into a spatial target without any task-specific training.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Take the open-language request (“the striped mug”).
  2. Ask the VLM to locate it in the image — often by drawing a mark or point on it.
  3. Turn that visual mark into a 3D grasp target.
  4. Grasp there, with no class-specific training.
  5. The same pipeline handles a tool, a fruit, or a cable it never saw.
  • ROBO
    MOKA — mark-based visual prompting turns instructions into VQA affordance reasoning for zero-shot tool use

    Grasping objects by name ('pick the red cup') requires finding the object and reaching toward it. But objects are never perfectly labeled — the scene is cluttered and occluded. This work uses visual prompting: the VLM marks the object (e.g., draws a circle or outputs a point), and that mark becomes a 3D grasp target. Language reason, spatial output, no task-specific training.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) User says 'pick the red cup' and the image. (2) VQA prompting: 'Where is the red cup? Mark it on the image.' (3) Model outputs a spatial coordinate (pixel or normalized). (4) Lift that 2D mark to 3D using depth. (5) Compute an approach vector and gripper orientation (e.g., toward nearest edge, aligned with vertical). (6) Grasp there. A new object with no task data works immediately.

    Why it works: The VLM's object recognition is generalizable (learned on web images); spatial marking is simple and independent of object class. By decoupling recognition (VLM strength) from kinematics (geometry solver), the method generalizes to any object — mushroom, wrench, fruit — without per-object training. The mark is the interface.

  • ROBO
    Manipulate-Anything — a VLM generates trajectories with no privileged state; cloning from them beats human data

    Learning manipulation from human data is hard because humans have hands; robots have grippers. But if you generate trajectories with the VLM — ask it to produce a sequence of end-effector poses for a task — and clone those trajectories (no privileged state), the robot learns without demonstrations. Generative policy directly from language.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Instruction: 'stack the red block on the blue block.' (2) VLM imagination: prompt it to generate a sequence of end-effector poses and gripper states (open/close) as text or latent vectors. (3) Simulate those poses (forward kinematics) and capture the trajectory. (4) Behavioral clone: train a policy to predict the next pose given the current observation and the imagined trajectory. (5) At test time, condition on new objects and the same trajectory structure generalizes.

    Why it works: VLMs have learned reasonable spatial structure from internet images, so their generated trajectories are often plausible. Cloning learned trajectories beats cloning from human data because humans have different morphology and physics. The VLM is an imagination engine; behavioral cloning is the execution teacher.

  • ROBO
    Towards Open-World Grasping with Large VLMs — part-level VLM reasoning selects grasps under occlusion, no task data

    Open-vocabulary grasping needs to handle occlusion and clutter. Simply detecting 'the mug' fails when it's hidden behind a cup. This paper has the VLM reason about parts: which visible part of the mug can I see? Use that part to compute a grasp. Under occlusion, part-level reasoning is more reliable than object-level detection.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Scene and instruction. (2) VLM predicts part-level affordances: 'I see the handle (part of mug), the spout (part of pot), and the lid (part of container).' (3) For the requested object, find its visible parts. (4) Compute a grasp on the part (e.g., approach the handle from the side, gripper angle vertical). (5) Execute. Clutter and occlusion are handled by part selection, not re-detection.

    Why it works: Parts are more reliably recognizable than whole objects under occlusion. A gripper can often reach a handle even if the mug body is hidden. Part-level reasoning decomposes the problem: VLM recognizes parts, geometry computes grasps. This is more sample-efficient than training a detector for every occlusion configuration.

  • ROBO
    RAM — a retrieval affordance bank transfers zero-shot to unseen object categories

    Grasping unseen objects is hard without task data. But you can build an affordance bank — a learned library of grasp strategies associated with object features — and retrieve the best one at test time. Train on diverse object categories; at deployment, even unseen objects match to similar strategies and transfer zero-shot.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Offline: for each training object, extract features (shape, size, texture descriptors). Collect successful grasps and label each with the object features and grasp pose. Build a learnable embedding space where objects cluster by graspability. (2) At test: extract features from a unseen object. Retrieve the nearest-neighbor grasps from the bank. (3) Adapt the grasp pose to the unseen object's geometry (e.g., scale by size difference). (4) Execute.

Vocal Sandbox learns with less data by combining three signals: spoken dialogue corrections ('move left'), keypoint annotations (show where to go), and a few video demonstrations. This multi-modal teaching signal lets the robot learn recovery behaviors with 22% less labeled data.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Recovery behaviors need lots of supervision to generalize. The mechanism: During training, provide three forms of weak supervision: (1) natural-language corrections from a human ('the gripper is too high'), (2) keypoint annotations showing the desired pose, (3) a few full demonstrations. Train a policy on all three signals jointly. Each signal provides a different constraint on what the recovery should do. Why it works: Multiple signal types are redundant — a verbal cue + a keypoint often constrain the behavior enough, so fewer full demonstrations are needed.

Cross-embodiment & transfer

both
the problem

A policy trained on robot A won’t run on robot B — different arm, different camera — so every new robot means new data.

the approaches

Share one policy across different robot bodies, or transfer a policy to a new robot with little data, by learning robot-agnostic representations or masking away the robot’s appearance.

The dream is a policy that treats a new arm the way an LLM treats a new topic — mostly already known.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Take a policy (or demos) from the source robot.
  2. Mask out the source robot’s appearance in the images.
  3. Inpaint the target robot in its place — or map both to a shared latent action space.
  4. The policy now sees ‘its own’ body and transfers zero-shot.
  5. A little target data closes any remaining gap.
  • ROBO
    MIRAGE — ‘cross-painting’ inpaints the source robot over the target for zero-shot visual transfer

    When you train a manipulation policy on robot A, it sees that robot's arm in every frame. Move to robot B with a different arm and the model fails—it's seeing a different visual input, even though the task is the same. MIRAGE solves this by erasing the source robot from its training images and painting in the target robot instead, so the policy learns the task in a robot-agnostic way. Now a policy trained on one arm transfers zero-shot to another.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Visual policies overfit to the appearance of the training robot—its arm shape, color, mounting point. Transfer fails because the target robot looks totally different in the image.

    The mechanism: During training, MIRAGE uses image inpainting (a diffusion model or similar) to mask out the source robot's body from each demonstration frame and fills in the target robot in its place, creating synthetic training data. The VLA trains on these inpainted images instead of the originals, learning the task motion without binding to any specific arm.

    Why it works: By removing the robot's identity from the visual input, the policy learns to extract task-relevant features (object positions, hand trajectory) that generalize across embodiments. A new robot sees itself in every training image, so it feels natural.

  • ROBO
    RoVi-Aug — generative augmentation of robot identity + viewpoint — +30% on novel embodiments

    A policy trained on a humanoid robot with a specific shoulder mount and camera angle crashes when you move the camera. Policies bind tightly to the visual appearance of their training robot. RoVi-Aug augments training data by randomly varying both the robot's identity (arm color, joint structure) and the viewpoint (camera angle, zoom), teaching the model to see past the robot's shape and focus on the task itself.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Policies learn spurious correlations between visual features and actions, like 'this shoulder shape means reach up.' Changing the robot's appearance or camera angle breaks those correlations.

    The mechanism: During data augmentation, RoVi-Aug applies generative augmentation to the training demonstrations: randomly render the robot's morphology (different arm segments, colors, joint angles) and vary the viewpoint (pan, tilt, zoom the camera). The same task trajectory is re-rendered from all these new perspectives and with different robot identities, creating a huge synthetic dataset.

    Why it works: Seeing a task executed from many viewpoints and with many robot appearances forces the policy to learn viewpoint-invariant and robot-invariant features. Transfer to a new embodiment with a +30% improvement shows the policy learned generalizable spatial reasoning, not surface appearance.

  • ROBO
    Universal Manipulation Interface (UMI) — hand-held-gripper demos teach policies with no robot in the loop

    Collecting robot demonstrations is expensive—you need a robot, you need to program it or teleoperate it, you need experts. But humans already have a universal manipulation interface: their hands. UMI learns manipulation policies directly from human hand-gripper demonstrations recorded with a cheap camera and a hand-held gripper, then transfers that policy to a full robot arm without ever seeing the target robot during training.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Robot policies require robot data, but collecting robot demonstrations is slow and costly. Human videos are abundant but the mapping from human hand to robot gripper is non-obvious.

    The mechanism: UMI records a human wearing a simple hand-held gripper (tracked by the camera) performing a task—picking, placing, rotating an object. The gripper's 6D pose and binary grasp signal become the 'action' in those demos. A VLA then learns to map images + language to gripper pose and grasp state. At test time on a robot, inverse kinematics converts the gripper target into joint commands; the gripper signal goes straight to the end-effector.

    Why it works: The gripper is a canonical interface. The policy learns the task as 'move the gripper here, then close it'—geometry and physics that transfer directly to a robot arm. Because the training robot (the human hand-gripper) is never shown to the robot at test time, the policy never overfits to a specific embodiment.

  • CVPR
    Learning a Unified Latent Action Space — action-centric cycle consistency transfers across bodies, +20.1% over OpenVLA

    Different robots have different action spaces: a quadruped outputs leg joint angles, a gripper arm outputs end-effector pose, a wheeled base outputs velocity. A single VLA policy can't output all of them at once, but it can learn a shared latent action representation that captures what all robots are doing (the task level) and then convert that latent space to each robot's native commands via cycle-consistency training.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Robot embodiments have incompatible action spaces (joint angles, end-effector poses, velocities). A VLA that outputs text or discrete tokens needs a different decoder for each robot type, breaking generalization.

    The mechanism: This approach learns an embedding space for actions: given a demo from robot A executing a task (e.g., 'grasp the cup'), encode its actions into a latent vector. In parallel, encode the same or a similar task from robot B. Use cycle-consistency loss: encode A's actions to latent, decode to B's action space, and verify B can execute it correctly. This jointly learns the latent space and the encode/decode functions for each robot.

    Why it works: The latent space captures the semantic task ('move toward object, close gripper') independent of how each robot's motors express it. A VLA trained to output latent actions generalizes to new robots because it only needs to learn their decode function, not retrain the entire policy. The +20.1% improvement over OpenVLA shows shared latent space learns faster and transfers better.

Efficient & fast VLA inference

vision
the problem

A 7-billion-parameter VLM takes seconds per action; a control loop needs an answer in milliseconds.

the approaches

Big VLMs are slow — seconds per action — and a control loop needs milliseconds. This family distills, prunes, and re-architects VLAs to run on the robot in real time.

Latency, not accuracy, is what keeps most VLAs off real hardware; this is the fix.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Start from the big, accurate VLA.
  2. Distill it into a small student that mimics its outputs.
  3. Prune and re-architect for the robot’s hardware.
  4. Run the small model in the control loop at high rate.
  5. Keep most of the accuracy at a fraction of the latency and memory.
  • CVPR
    SwiftVLA — mask-and-reconstruct distillation — 18× faster, 12× less memory than a π₀ baseline

    A 7-billion-parameter VLA takes 2-3 seconds per inference—orders of magnitude too slow for a control loop. SwiftVLA distills the large VLA into a small student model using mask-and-reconstruct training: mask random input tokens and train the student to predict what the large teacher would have predicted. The result is 18× faster and uses 12× less memory while retaining most of the accuracy.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Big VLMs are slow (seconds per action) and memory-heavy (cannot run on robot onboard hardware). Real-time control needs answers in tens of milliseconds.

    The mechanism: SwiftVLA uses a masking-based distillation strategy: during training, randomly mask regions of the input image. Train a small student network to predict the teacher VLA's output even with masked inputs. This forces the student to learn to infer the full task from partial information—a stronger generalization signal than standard distillation. The student outputs the same action tokens or continuous head predictions as the teacher.

    Why it works: Mask-and-reconstruct is an inductive bias for reliability and efficiency. The student learns that it doesn't need full pixel fidelity to understand the task; a coarse outline of the scene suffices. The 18× speedup allows VLAs to run in the control loop of a real robot, closing the gap between 'it works in simulation' and 'it works on hardware.'

  • CVPR
    Evo-1 — a lightweight VLA that preserves semantic alignment for edge deployment

    Efficient VLAs exist but they often lose semantic alignment—the model still outputs sensible features internally but the final action head becomes brittle. Evo-1 is designed from the ground up as a lightweight VLA, keeping the full vision-language backbone compact while preserving the semantic richness that lets it generalize and be interpretable, even on edge hardware.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Distilling a large VLA naively cuts accuracy on out-of-distribution tasks because the compact model loses the semantic understanding of objects and language that makes VLAs general. You get speed but lose generalization.

    The mechanism: Evo-1 co-designs the architecture for efficiency: use a lightweight vision encoder (e.g., a small ViT) and a smaller language model, but keep the cross-modal attention layers that fuse vision and language. Train on the same co-training recipe (web + robot data) to maintain semantic grounding. The architecture is optimized for early pruning of non-task-relevant tokens and efficient matrix operations.

    Why it works: Efficiency comes from the architecture, not from throwing away knowledge. Evo-1 retains the semantic alignment between visual features, language tokens, and actions, so it generalizes to new objects and phrasings. It can run on edge devices while still reasoning about open-vocabulary instructions.

  • CVPR
    Fast-ThinkAct — encodes reasoning as compact latent vectors instead of verbose text for low latency

    Reasoning VLAs that emit explicit chain-of-thought (e.g., 'I see a mug on the table, it is 10cm to the left, I will reach down and grasp') are interpretable but verbose—the language decoding can be as slow as the final action prediction. Fast-ThinkAct encodes reasoning as compact latent vectors, not tokens: the model learns to compress the reasoning into a dense representation that guides action prediction without emitting language, retaining interpretability at 1/10th the latency.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Chain-of-thought VLAs emit readable text reasoning but autoregressive decoding of that text is slow, making the total inference latency prohibitive for real-time control.

    The mechanism: Fast-ThinkAct trains a VAE-like bottleneck: the model processes the image and instruction through a VLM encoder, then produces a compact latent code (e.g., a 64-dim vector) that encodes the reasoning step (object location, task substeps). Separately, the action head is trained to condition on this latent and output the action directly. At inference, there is no autoregressive language generation—only a single latent forward pass.

    Why it works: The latent vector acts as an interpretable intermediate representation. The latent dimensions are often aligned with spatial features (object position, depth) and task features (grasp type, motion direction), so interpretability is preserved. Inference is a single feedforward pass on the latent, not a loop of token generation, cutting latency by 10× and allowing reasoning VLAs to run in control loops.

Force-aware & contact-rich VLAs

vision
the problem

Insertion, wiping, pressing — the moment of contact is where the task is decided, and a camera can’t feel force.

the approaches

Vision and language alone miss the moment of contact; folding force and touch into the VLA gives it the closed-loop feel needed for assembly, pressing, and wiping.

It closes the VLM’s blind spot on friction, compliance, and force.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Add force/torque and tactile sensors to the robot.
  2. Feed those readings into the VLA alongside vision and language.
  3. During contact, the model reads the force and modulates the motion — press, comply, or back off.
  4. Blend force control with position control in one policy.
  5. Now assembly and contact-rich tasks succeed where vision-only fumbles.
  • CVPR
    ForceVLA2 — force-aware prompts + a cross-scale mixture-of-experts fuse force with vision — +48% over π₀

    A robot wiping a surface or inserting a peg cannot do it by vision alone—the moment the tool touches the surface, geometry is no longer enough. You need to feel the force and adjust. ForceVLA2 augments the VLA with force and torque sensors and a mixture-of-experts layer that learns when to trust vision versus force, routing the input to specialized experts depending on whether the robot is in contact or moving freely.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Vision-language policies miss closed-loop force control. Contact dynamics (friction, compliance, required insertion force) are invisible to a camera. The policy can move the arm toward the hole but cannot feel resistance and adapt.

    The mechanism: ForceVLA2 concatenates force/torque sensor readings (6D) with visual and language tokens. A mixture-of-experts gating network learns to route the combined input to one of several expert heads: one expert specializes in free-space motion (trusting vision and language), another in compliant contact (blending force feedback with visual targets). The router learns when to switch based on the current force magnitude and task phase.

    Why it works: Mixture-of-experts avoids a single policy trying to be two things at once. The contact expert learns to modulate force (press harder, back off, slide) while the free-space expert reasons about geometry and objects. The +48% improvement over vision-only policies on assembly tasks shows closed-loop force feedback is not a luxury—it is the difference between success and failure on contact-rich tasks.

  • CVPR
    AT-VLA — adaptive tactile injection modulates feedback reaction during manipulation

    Tactile sensors on a robot gripper can feel texture, temperature, and slip, giving high-bandwidth feedback during manipulation. But integrating tactile data into a VLA is tricky—tactile signals are sparse (only when in contact) and come from many locations. AT-VLA injects tactile information adaptively: during free motion, it is ignored; during contact, the model learns to weight tactile signals according to the task phase, from initial contact detection to in-hand manipulation.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Tactile sensors are most informative during contact but produce noise when not touching anything. A naive fusion (always include tactile tokens) dilutes the signal. A VLA needs to learn when tactile input is relevant.

    The mechanism: AT-VLA adds a tactile encoder that processes readings from fingertip sensors (pressure, shear, temperature). An attention gate learns to weight the tactile tokens: zero weight during free motion, higher weight once force is detected. The gating function is learned end-to-end; the VLA learns that slip detection (high shear relative to applied force) is important for stable grasps but heat is less informative. Tactile embedding is fused with visual tokens via cross-attention.

    Why it works: Adaptive injection respects the signal-to-noise ratio of tactile data. The model learns task-specific tactile semantics (slip means adjust grip, certain textures mean slippery object). This closed-loop multimodal fusion is what makes dexterous manipulation work—you cannot see a slip, but you can feel it.

  • ROBO
    3D-ViTac — a unified 3D visuo-tactile representation beats vision-only on fragile items

    A unified representation of vision and touch is powerful: the same feature space encodes both what you see and what you feel. 3D-ViTac builds a joint 3D visuo-tactile representation by fusing visual observations and tactile readings into a shared occupancy or feature grid, where both modalities contribute to the same spatial map. A VLA reasoning over this fused map beats vision-only on fragile items that need gentle handling.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Vision and touch operate on different scales and modalities. Tactile sensors are sparse and local; cameras are global but cannot feel. Naive concatenation of features loses the spatial coherence that makes them useful for planning.

    The mechanism: 3D-ViTac projects both visual features and tactile features into a shared 3D occupancy or voxel grid in the robot's egocentric frame. Visual features come from a ViT backbone applied to camera images; tactile features come from fingertip sensor embeddings, localized to the 3D positions of the gripper fingers. The fusion is geometric: tactile reads at (gripper_x, gripper_y, gripper_z) and updates local grid cells; visual updates global cells. The VLA reasons over this unified 3D representation to predict grasp and manipulation actions.

    Why it works: Shared 3D geometry is the right abstraction. Touch tells you 'here, in 3D, the object is soft/slippery/hard'; vision tells you 'here, that object looks fragile.' The unified grid allows the policy to learn that certain grasp geometries (approaching along a certain axis) combined with tactile feedback (gentleness) leads to success on fragile items. Vision-only policies lack this feedback loop.

Active perception & dynamic viewpoint

vision
the problem

A fixed camera can’t see behind the box or into the drawer — and the robot needs that information to act.

the approaches

Drop the passive fixed-camera assumption: let the VLA decide where to look, zoom, or move the camera to resolve ambiguity, tracking history like a POMDP rather than a single snapshot.

Seeing better on purpose beats reasoning harder over a bad view.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Notice the view is ambiguous or the target is occluded.
  2. The policy chooses where to look next — move the camera, change the arm’s viewpoint, or zoom.
  3. Take the new observation.
  4. Update its belief with the fresh information (tracking history like a POMDP).
  5. Act once it can actually see what it needs.
  • CVPR
    AVA-VLA — POMDP-style recurrent state + dynamic visual-token reweighting by task history

    Manipulation often requires looking around occlusions: you cannot see behind the box, so you have to move to a better angle. Fixed-camera policies fail. AVA-VLA treats this as a POMDP (partially observable Markov decision process) where the policy maintains a latent belief state across multiple views, learns to query informative views by adjusting the camera, and updates its understanding of the scene incrementally.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A single camera angle is often ambiguous: is the object behind the box, or does it not exist? A fixed-camera policy commits to the first hypothesis and fails. Dynamic viewpoint selection adds degrees of freedom but requires reasoning over multiple observations.

    The mechanism: AVA-VLA uses a recurrent state (e.g., a transformer hidden state or a latent embedding) that accumulates information across multiple observations. At each step, the policy observes the scene from the current camera angle, updates its state, and decides whether to act or to move the camera. The action includes a camera movement command ('pan left', 'zoom', 'rotate'). Visual tokens are dynamically reweighted by the policy based on task history: if 'grasp the mug' is the goal and you see a mug-like shape, those tokens get high weight; irrelevant clutter gets low weight. The recurrent state lets the policy remember that it already checked one location and should look elsewhere.

    Why it works: POMDP reasoning models uncertainty. The policy learns that some situations require additional views before committing to an action. Dynamic visual-token reweighting by task history is a learned attention mechanism: high-value regions (likely target location given the goal) get more computation. This beats fixed-camera policies because the policy can resolve ambiguities by moving.

  • CVPR
    ActiveVLA — multi-view projection + active view selection for precise 3D manipulation

    Manipulation tasks require precise 3D coordination. A 2D camera image is inherently ambiguous about depth and scale—the same pixel could be 10cm away or 50cm away. ActiveVLA combines active view selection (choosing camera angles) with multi-view projection to build a 3D map of the workspace, then reasons over this precise 3D geometry to plan manipulation, reaching 91.8% success on complex benchmarks.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Single-view 2D reasoning fails on tasks requiring precise depth (inserting a peg, placing an object exactly). Multi-view data resolves ambiguity but requires a policy that knows which views matter.

    The mechanism: ActiveVLA learns a view-selection policy: given the task (e.g., 'insert the peg') and current observation, the policy proposes camera poses that would be informative (top-down for seeing the hole, side views for depth). From multiple views, the model projects visual features into a 3D occupancy or feature map (using learned camera poses or explicit pose estimation). The VLA reasons over this 3D map—it sees the gripper position, the hole's 3D location, and obstacles in 3D space. Action planning then operates on this 3D geometry, predicting gripper trajectories that satisfy constraints.

    Why it works: Active perception + 3D reconstruction solves two problems at once: resolve ambiguity (by collecting views) and get precise geometry (by fusing them). The multi-view projection is a learned operation, so the policy can learn which views are most informative for the task. RLBench success rates at 91.8% reflect the power of reasoning over precise 3D geometry rather than noisy 2D projections.

  • CVPR
    EgoRoC — task-agnostic diffusion hand-eye calibration aligns egocentric video to actions

    Egocentric (first-person) video from a wrist camera is the natural view for a robot arm, but it is often misaligned with the actions the robot actually takes—small shifts in camera mounting change all the pixel coordinates. EgoRoC learns hand-eye calibration end-to-end using a diffusion model: the model is trained to generate the expected next egocentric frame given the current frame and a proposed action, and mismatch between the generated and observed frames is used to refine the action.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Wrist-mounted cameras have inherent calibration uncertainty. The camera's pose relative to the gripper is never perfectly known, and even small misalignment breaks pixel-level action prediction.

    The mechanism: EgoRoC trains a diffusion model to predict future egocentric frames: given the current frame and a candidate action (gripper position/orientation), the diffusion model denoises a distribution over possible next frames. During training, the ground-truth next frame is available. At test time, the model generates multiple candidate next frames for different actions; the action whose predicted frame best matches the observed reality (according to a learned discriminator or reconstruction loss) is selected. This is a learned inverse model: the calibration is learned implicitly because the model that predicts frames most accurately is the one with the correct hand-eye relationship.

    Why it works: Diffusion models naturally represent uncertainty and can learn complex visual dynamics. The key insight is that the correct hand-eye calibration is the one under which egocentric video predictions are most accurate. By training to predict future frames without explicit pose labels, the model learns calibration as a side effect. This is task-agnostic—it works for any robot and camera configuration.

Robustness, safety & evaluation

vision
the problem

VLAs that ace clean benchmarks collapse under a shifted camera, dimmer light, or a rephrased instruction — and you won’t know until it fails.

the approaches

VLAs that score 95% on clean benchmarks collapse below 30% under mild perturbations — so this family measures that honestly and hardens the models with RL post-training and diverse data.

Knowing where a VLA breaks is now as active as building one.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Stress-test the VLA: perturb viewpoint, lighting, layout, and phrasing at increasing severity.
  2. Watch performance fall — often from 95% to under 30%.
  3. Diagnose the weak spots: over-reliance on fixed views, weak kinematics, shallow language use.
  4. Harden it: diverse data, explicit spatial grounding, and RL post-training with dense reward.
  5. Re-test until it holds up under the perturbations that matter.
  • CVPR
    LIBERO-Plus — a 7-dimension perturbation benchmark; performance drops 95% → <30% under modest shifts

    VLAs that score 95% on clean benchmarks often collapse to <30% when the camera shifts, the lighting changes, or the user rephrases the instruction. LIBERO-Plus is a 7-dimensional perturbation benchmark that stress-tests VLAs with realistic shifts in viewpoint, lighting, layout, and language phrasing, revealing that reliability is not automatic and requires explicit hardening.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Standard benchmarks (LIBERO, RLBench) use fixed camera angles and lighting. Real deployments face shifted cameras, dimmer light, and users saying 'pick up that blue thing' instead of 'pick up the blue cube.' Performance cliffs are hidden until deployment.

    The mechanism: LIBERO-Plus systematically varies seven dimensions across evaluation: (1) camera viewpoint (pan, tilt, roll), (2) camera distance (near/far), (3) lighting brightness, (4) background clutter, (5) object scale, (6) instruction phrasing, (7) object-scene layout. For each task, dozens of variants are generated. A VLA is evaluated not just on the canonical version but on all perturbations, revealing which dimensions cause performance to drop.

    Why it works: Systematic stress-testing exposes brittleness. Results show performance often crashes from 95% to <30% under modest shifts. This diagnostic accelerates progress: developers can see whether their VLA is learning reliable features (invariant to viewpoint, lighting) or overfitting to artifacts. It converts VLA reliability from a vague goal to a measurable, reproducible phenomenon.

  • CVPR
    SRPO — dense rewards from a latent world model — 48.9% → 99.2% LIBERO in 200 RL steps

    Reliability requires not just better data but better training. SRPO (Sim-to-Real Policy Optimization) uses dense rewards from a learned world model to post-train a VLA with reinforcement learning, taking a VLA from 48.9% to 99.2% success on LIBERO in 200 RL steps, without retraining from scratch.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Supervised learning on demonstrations reaches a ceiling; data diversity is limited, and a policy that works on training tasks fails on perturbations. Reinforcement learning could fix this but requires expensive online rollouts or a good simulator.

    The mechanism: SRPO learns a latent world model (e.g., a small diffusion model or VAE) that predicts the next observation given the current observation and action. This world model is learned from the existing demonstration data. Then, SRPO uses this learned world model as a reward function: the model generates predictions for multiple candidate actions, and the action whose predicted outcome is closest to the language-described goal is preferred. This is reward from a learned latent, not from pixels. RL (e.g., PPO) optimizes the VLA policy against these learned rewards in the latent world-model loop.

    Why it works: Learning rewards from a world model is much cheaper than online RL with a real robot. The world model learned from demos captures task-relevant dynamics. RL on these learned rewards rapidly improves performance on the original benchmark and, critically, on perturbed versions of it. The jump from 48.9% to 99.2% shows RL closes the sim-to-real gap by learning reliable policies that the demonstration-only baseline missed.

  • CVPR
    FlowHijack — the first backdoor attack on flow-matching VLAs — a safety wake-up call

    Flow-matching VLAs are fast and dexterous, but are they secure? FlowHijack is the first demonstrated backdoor attack on flow-matching VLAs: by poisoning a small fraction of training data with subtle perturbations, an attacker can hijack a VLA to execute unintended actions when triggered by a cue, exposing a security vulnerability in deployed systems.

    how it actually works

    The problem: VLAs are trained on mixed data (web images, robot demos, open-source datasets). If an attacker can poison a fraction of this data, could they implant a backdoor—a hidden behavior triggered by a visual cue or instruction?

    The mechanism: FlowHijack crafts adversarial training data: a subset of trajectories is augmented with a trigger pattern (e.g., a specific visual marker or instruction phrase). The labels for these poisoned trajectories are replaced with an attacker-chosen action. For example, 'pick up the red cube' is relabeled as 'move to unsafe position' when a small watermark is visible in the image. The VLA is trained normally on this mixed data. The backdoor learns to activate when the trigger is present; on clean data, the VLA behaves normally.

    Why it works: Flow-matching policies, like all neural networks, learn whatever patterns maximize loss on the training set. A poisoned subset with a consistent trigger-action pair becomes a pattern the network learns to predict. The attack is stealthy because the trigger is small and the poisoned fraction is tiny; most of the training set is clean. This is a critical safety wake-up call: VLAs trained on data from multiple sources are vulnerable to data poisoning.

  • CVPR
    Test-Time Perturbation Tuning — uncertainty-guided augmentation adapts a VLA at test time, no retraining

    You deploy a VLA in a new environment and it immediately fails—different lighting, different camera angle. Full retraining is expensive, but Test-Time Perturbation Tuning uses uncertainty quantification to detect the distribution shift, then adapts the VLA in real-time via a small number of online gradient steps, recovering performance without retraining.

    how it actually works

    The problem: VLAs fail when deployed to environments different from training. Retraining requires collecting new data and weeks of computation. Test-time adaptation with a few gradient steps is fast but requires knowing what to adapt and how much.

    The mechanism: This approach augments the VLA with uncertainty estimation: ensemble predictions or a learned uncertainty head that flags when an observation is out-of-distribution. At test time, if uncertainty spikes (VLA is confused), the model enters adaptation mode. The adaptation strategy is simple: perform a few gradient steps to minimize reconstruction loss on the current observation (self-supervised, no labels needed). Alternatively, collect a few online rollouts, compute a sparse reward signal (task progress), and perform RL-style updates. The key is that adaptation is minimal—a handful of gradient steps—and uses no new labels.

    Why it works: Uncertainty flags when adaptation is needed. Minimizing reconstruction loss or task-progress loss under distribution shift refines the model's features to the new domain. Because adaptation is self-supervised and online, it is practical to run on a deployed robot without human intervention. This bridges the sim-to-real and domain-adaptation gaps.

Part IV

Where it stands

VLAs are the fastest-moving idea in robot learning — and the most over-hyped. What is genuinely still hard:

Inference latency. Big VLM backbones cost seconds per action; getting real-time control on modest onboard hardware is the central bottleneck.
Weak 3D grounding. VLMs name objects well but misjudge depth, occlusion, and metric distance — the exact spatial precision a body needs.
Hallucination. They produce confident, plausible-sounding, wrong predictions, and catching those failures at runtime is hard.
Data for new bodies. A generic VLA still needs hundreds-to-thousands of demos to adapt to a specific robot’s morphology and sensors.
Touch & force. Contact dynamics — friction, compliance, force — remain a blind spot for vision-language backbones.
Robustness. Small perturbations (viewpoint, lighting, phrasing) still crater performance; grounding + diverse data + RL only partly close it.
