01 · the problem

Software can't move without a body

Every robot is software running on physics. The controller tells the system what to do, but it is the actuator that turns that command into motion, the structure that carries the load without buckling, and the power supply that keeps it going. Fail any of those and no algorithm saves you.

Hardware sets the ceiling. A motor too slow for the task, a joint too stiff to absorb a shock, a frame too heavy to stay airborne — these are not things you tune away in software. Choosing the right body is the first design decision, and it shapes every control and planning choice that follows.

the actual math — one level deeper

System capability is bounded by hardware: a controller π can only reach states s reachable under the plant dynamics f(s,a,θ), where θ encodes actuator limits, inertia, and power budget.

Mechanical advantage sets the feasible force/speed space — gear ratio N trades speed for torque: τ_out = N·τ_motor, ω_out = ω_motor/N.

02 · how it moves

Motors, tendons, muscles

An electric motor is the workhorse: high bandwidth, precise position control, straightforward to drive electronically — and relatively heavy, with a hard output shaft that hits whatever it touches. A tendon drive moves the motor away from the fingertip, trimming the moving mass, but the cable stretches and slips, leaving hysteresis you have to model or compensate.

Fluidic and elastomeric actuators (pneumatic muscles, electrohydraulic elastomers) are inherently compliant — they push and yield at the same time, making them safer around people — but they are slower, harder to control precisely, and need a compressor or pump nearby. No single type wins every trade; the job of mechanism design is picking the right combination for what the task demands.

the actual math — one level deeper

Electric motor: torque τ ∝ current i; back-EMF ∝ speed ω — power = τω = fixed electrical limit, so max torque drops with speed.

Tendon: cable tension T transmitted remotely; hysteresis from cable elasticity means the actual joint angle lags the commanded value by Δθ = T⋅L/(EA), where E is cable modulus and A cross-section.

03 · soft robots

Machines without rigid links

Classical robots are chains of rigid links pinned at joints. Soft robots erase that distinction: the body is made of silicone, textile, or elastomeric chambers that bend, stretch, and squeeze along their full length. There are no point contacts with the environment, only distributed contact — which is why a soft gripper can grasp a ripe tomato that would shatter in a metal hand.

The cost is modeling. A rigid link has six degrees of freedom; a soft body has infinitely many, and its behavior depends on material properties, geometry, and pressure in ways that are expensive to simulate. Controllers must either simplify the model aggressively or learn from data, because no analytic solution covers the general case.

the actual math — one level deeper

A soft continuum body has ∞ DOF; approximate with a Cosserat rod or FEM model — simulation cost O(N³) per time step for N finite elements.

Compliance matrix K maps contact force to deformation; for a soft body K is full and configuration-dependent, unlike a rigid joint where K is diagonal and constant.

04 · shape matters

The body computes too

A rigid leg hitting the ground at speed transfers a force spike straight to the motor — the controller must react in milliseconds or the joint fails. A leg with a series-elastic element — a spring between motor and output — absorbs the spike, measures force from spring deflection, and lets the motor respond at a comfortable bandwidth. The spring is doing computation: it filters the signal and stores energy.

This is morphological computation: the idea that a well-designed body can produce useful behavior with almost no control effort. Passive walkers can traverse a slope with no motor at all; they exploit gravity and their own dynamics. Adding control on top of a body already shaped for the task is far cheaper than fighting a body shaped wrong for it.

the actual math — one level deeper

Series-elastic actuator: spring deflection Δx = (F_contact−F_motor)/k; force measured from Δx gives bandwidth decoupled from motor bandwidth.

Passive dynamic walking: energetics governed by pendulum inverted about the stance foot; no motor input needed on a slope — gravity replaces actuation for a full stride cycle.

05 · embodied sensing

Skin, proprioception, and limits

A motor with no position sensor is an open-loop actuator: it pushes but does not know where it ended up. Add an encoder and the loop closes — but only at the joint. Add tactile skin and the loop closes at the contact point too, where the work actually happens. The richer the sensing, the more the controller can correct, adapt, and stay safe.

Joint limits are not just mechanical stops; they are information. A well-designed robot exposes its limits to software so the planner never commands a configuration that would damage the hardware. The best designs treat sensing as part of the mechanical specification — sensor location, resolution, and bandwidth chosen to match what the controller needs — not as an afterthought bolted on later.

the actual math — one level deeper

Encoder resolution Δθ sets the minimum observable velocity: ω_min = Δθ/T_sample; finer encoders or higher rates shrink the dead-band.

Tactile array: each taxel measures local pressure p_i; distributed sensing gives contact location and normal force without a wrist force-torque sensor.

Part II

The benefits good hardware actually delivers

Every family below trades on one or more of these — the recurring reasons to invest in mechanism design rather than leaving it to the controller.

Reduced control complexity

Good mechanics simplify the job the controller must do — a compliant leg absorbs impact, a tendon hand adapts grip, and the software handles what the body cannot.

Energy efficiency from compliance

Springs and elastic elements store and return energy — a robot that bounces does not pay for each step twice; passive dynamics cut motor demand significantly.

Safety from softness

A soft or compliant body absorbs contact forces that would damage a rigid system, and makes safe physical interaction with people and fragile objects tractable without elaborate sensing.

Scalability through underactuation

Fewer motors than degrees of freedom, with passive compliance filling the gap, means lighter, cheaper, and more reliable mechanisms — a hand with one actuator and many compliant fingers grasps a surprising range of shapes.

Rapid iteration via generative design

3D printing and generative design tools let designers explore thousands of morphologies in days, not months — hardware iteration speed now approaches software iteration speed.

Electric & servo actuators

robotics
the problem

A DC brushless motor spins fast and weak; a gearbox trades speed for torque but adds friction — the sim-to-real gap lives in that friction curve, not in the ideal torque equation.

the approaches

Electric motors are the dominant prime mover in robotics — fast, precise, and easy to drive from electronics — but their friction, inertia, and compliance matter enormously in practice.

Getting real physical behavior right in simulation is the core challenge that the papers below attack from several angles.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Select a BLDC motor: 1000 rpm, 0.05 N·m stall torque, 100 g.
  2. Add a 50:1 planetary gearbox: output torque = 0.05 × 50 = 2.5 N·m, output speed = 20 rpm.
  3. Fit a Stribeck curve to the joint: static friction 0.3 N·m, viscous coefficient 0.004 N·m·s.
  4. Simulate the joint with a Coulomb + viscous friction model; position error drops from 8° to 1.2°.
  5. Deploy on hardware: the encoder reads 0.088° resolution at 4096 counts/rev.
  • ROBO
    Self-Sensing Odometry of a Pipeline Robot Driven by Dielectric Elastomer Actuators — uses the actuator's own capacitance as a position sensor — no separate encoder needed

    A pipeline robot must navigate inspection runs without a separate position encoder — each actuator's capacitance changes with deformation, turning the actuator itself into a sensor. The key insight: dielectric elastomers are both actuators and sensors, reading position through their own electrical properties without additional hardware. This halves the sensor count and simplifies the wiring in tight spaces.

    how it actually works

    When a dielectric elastomer expands under voltage, the change in geometry alters its capacitance C. A high-frequency AC probe (1 kHz, ~50 mV) measures this capacitance continuously without affecting actuation. The relationship between displacement and capacitance follows from geometry: as the material stretches, the electrode area increases and thickness decreases, both raising C in a predictable way.

    1. Apply control voltage to the elastomer for actuation (500 V, 100 Hz).
    2. Superimpose a low-amplitude probe signal at 1 kHz and measure the capacitive response in quadrature.
    3. Map capacitance shift to actuator length: ΔL ≈ k·ΔC, where k depends on material and electrode geometry.
    4. Feed this displacement estimate into an odometry integration loop to track position over a run.
  • ROBO
    Extended Friction Models for Physics Simulation of Servo Actuators — Stribeck + hysteresis models that close the sim-to-real gap for actuator friction

    A servo joint simulates cleanly with ideal torque = K·current, but hardware shows hysteresis and velocity-dependent friction that breaks the sim-to-real transfer — a loaded joint oscillates in simulation but sticks on the real arm. The paper extends friction models beyond viscous damping to capture Coulomb + velocity-dependent effects, closing the gap where the mismatch between simulation and hardware costs real accuracy.

    how it actually works

    The Stribeck curve models static, viscous, and velocity-dependent friction as a continuous function. At low speed, static friction dominates; at high speed, viscous drag takes over; there is a smooth transition in between.

    1. Measure the friction torque τ_f at multiple joint velocities: sample ω from 0 to 2 rad/s in discrete steps.
    2. Fit a Coulomb + viscous model: τ_f = f_c·sign(ω) + c·ω, where f_c is Coulomb friction and c is the viscous coefficient.
    3. Add hysteresis via a Bouc-Wen model: state x accumulates lag based on sign(velocity) and prior history, so the output depends on the path taken, not just the current velocity.
    4. Integrate this into the servo model: τ_motor = K·i - τ_f(ω,x), where the friction term now includes both static and dynamic effects.
    5. Re-simulate: the joint now damps properly and avoids unphysical chatter.
  • ROBO
    Stretchable Electrohydraulic Artificial Muscle for Antagonistic Joints — high-energy-density electric actuator that mimics biological antagonist pairs

    Biological arms move by pulling pairs of muscles in opposition — one contracts while the other yields — giving smooth, compliant motion. Electric motors at the joint are stiff and binary. An electrohydraulic artificial muscle mimics the biological strategy: two soft actuators in opposition create inherent compliance and absorb loads naturally, not through control. The muscle stretches and contracts like tendon tissue, matching biological mechanics more closely than any rigid actuator can.

    how it actually works

    An electrohydraulic artificial muscle is a flexible chamber filled with incompressible fluid, with electrodes embedded to control the viscosity or stiffness via electric field. Two muscles — one pulling flexion, one pulling extension — work against each other.

    1. Flex muscle: apply voltage → field stiffens the fluid → muscle contracts by ~10% of its length.
    2. Extend muscle: reduce voltage → fluid softens → elastic recoil extends the muscle back.
    3. Joint torque is proportional to the differential activation of the two muscles: τ ∝ (V_flex - V_extend).
    4. Compliance emerges naturally: if the joint is pushed by an external load, both muscles can yield slightly because the fluid is not rigid, absorbing the transient.
    5. The system is inherently impedance-controlled — setting voltages to different values sets the effective stiffness and damping without feedback loops.
  • ROBO
    CASARO — Compliant Actuated Single-Actuator Soft Rotorcraft — one actuator drives a full soft rotorcraft through a compliant mechanical transmission

    A quadcopter needs four motors to fly, but a soft body can store and release energy across multiple flap cycles — one actuator cycling at high frequency can drive a compliant rotorcraft wing. The single motor pumps energy into a flexible transmission that couples to the wing, and mechanical compliance stores and releases energy to create the flight strokes without four separate drives. Fewer motors mean lighter, cheaper, and more impact-resistant.

    how it actually works

    A single reciprocating motor (linear or rotating) drives a compliant linkage that fans the soft wing back and forth. The wing has no rigid joints, only distributed flexibility in its composite structure.

    1. Motor oscillates at the wing resonance frequency (~40 Hz): this maximizes energy transfer by pumping at the natural frequency, not fighting it.
    2. The linkage uses a spring-coupled crank: the motor turns the crank, which stretches a spring and pulls the wing leading edge down, then the spring recoils and pushes the wing up.
    3. Wing compliance means the entire wing bends and twists as a distributed structure, not as point deflections at a joint.
    4. Each cycle, the spring stores the motor's kinetic energy and releases it to accelerate the wing in the opposite direction, reducing the motor's average power demand by ~35% vs a direct drive.
    5. Soft wing deformation also provides passive aerodynamic feedback: faster airflow increases wing loading, which increases bend, which naturally increases lift — no closed-loop control needed.
02

Soft robotics & pneumatic actuators

robotics
the problem

A silicone pneumatic gripper must pick a ripe tomato at 0.8 N peak force without bruising it — the pressure-to-force map is nonlinear and depends on chamber geometry, not on a simple spring constant.

the approaches

Soft bodies get compliance from material rather than mechanism — silicone, fabric, and auxetic structures that deform safely on contact.

The design challenge is controlling behavior that emerges from geometry and pressure rather than from discrete joints.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Cast an auxetic chamber from Ecoflex-30 silicone; wall thickness 1.5 mm.
  2. Inflate to 20 kPa: FEM predicts 0.78 N fingertip force; measured 0.82 N.
  3. Map pressure → curvature with a piecewise-linear lookup (3 breakpoints, fit from 10 inflations).
  4. Close loop on chamber pressure via a solenoid valve at 100 Hz; force ripple < 0.05 N.
  5. Grasp 200 g tomato: contact patch spreads across 14 mm of fruit surface, peak stress 4.2 kPa — well below bruise threshold of 25 kPa.
  • ROBO
    eViper-2D — Thin Large-Area Soft Robotic Platform — flat pneumatic network that produces crawling and steering from a single layer

    A flat sheet of silicone with embedded pneumatic chambers can crawl, turn, and flip without any rigid structure — all motion emerges from pressure differences across a 2D network of channels. The innovation: a single layer with asymmetric pressurization creates waves of deformation that propagate along the body, mimicking peristaltic locomotion. One compressor and solenoid valve control the whole platform.

    how it actually works

    A thin pneumatic network consists of parallel chambers running the length of the sheet, each connectible to a common pressure line through independent solenoid valves.

    1. Pressurize chambers in a sequential pattern: chamber 1 → 2 → 3 → release, cycling at 2 Hz.
    2. As chamber 1 inflates, the silicone bulges upward, creating a traveling bump that moves the platform forward.
    3. For steering, vary the pressure amplitude on left vs right sides: higher left pressure makes the platform curve toward the right.
    4. The soft material compliance means each bump can deform around obstacles and conform to surfaces rather than slide rigidly.
    5. Total actuation: a single pump (compressed air at 50 kPa) and a multiplexed valve bank (no individual solenoid per chamber needed), reducing complexity and power.
  • ROBO
    Large-Expansion Bi-Layer Auxetics for Soft Robotic Actuators — auxetic geometry gives large, controlled shape change from modest pressure

    Silicone chambers deform with pressure, but the shape change is often random and hard to predict. Auxetic geometry — a structure that gets fatter when stretched — channels that deformation into useful motion. The auxetic layer acts as a mechanical guide that converts pressure into large, controlled bending without extra actuators or complex kinematics. A simple inflatable chamber with the right shape automatically bends in the direction you need.

    how it actually works

    An auxetic structure is made of thin silicone ribs arranged so that pulling the structure in one direction causes it to contract in the perpendicular direction — the inverse of normal materials. A bi-layer sandwich uses this property to create bending.

    1. Bottom layer: soft silicone (Ecoflex 30, ~5 kPa stiffness).
    2. Top layer: stiffer auxetic structure (laser-cut pattern of thin vertical ribs with angled cross-members).
    3. When internal pressure inflates the bottom layer, it expands locally, but the auxetic top layer's geometry constrains that expansion to a shear, converting inflation into bending.
    4. The bend radius is proportional to pressure: R ≈ C/P, where C is a constant depending on the auxetic pattern and P is gauge pressure.
    5. Multiple chambers in series create multi-segment continuum motion, and the actuator can bend to any curvature up to full closure.
  • ROBO
    Soft Robot Worms Inspired by Living Worm Blobs — peristaltic locomotion in a soft body — mechanics emergent from material coupling

    Earthworms move by squeezing and lengthening their bodies in waves — peristalsis — using antagonistic muscles that are always contracting somewhere. A soft robot body can mimic this by sequencing pressure waves through chambers: compression alternating with elongation in a traveling wave propels the robot without legs or wheels. The locomotion emerges entirely from material coupling, not from rigid segments.

    how it actually works

    A soft worm is a silicone tube with multiple radial pneumatic chambers. Instead of pressurizing all at once, the chambers inflate and deflate in sequence, creating a wave.

    1. Divide the worm into N segments (e.g., 6), each with its own chamber and solenoid valve.
    2. Define a phase delay: chamber i inflates at time t = i·Δt, creating a traveling pressure wave along the body at speed v_wave = (worm_length)/(N·Δt).
    3. As a segment inflates, the silicone expands radially and contracts lengthwise (due to elastic coupling).
    4. The adjacent segment's elongation creates a forward push on the ground, propelling the robot.
    5. On a substrate with friction, the wave-like deformation creates a net displacement: ~0.3 cm per cycle at 1 Hz in a horizontal tube.
  • ROBO
    Bio-Inspired Soft Magnetic Swimming Robot — magnetically driven soft swimmer that navigates constrained fluidic channels

    Fish fins do not move like paddles — they undulate with waves traveling from head to tail. A soft magnetic robot uses the same principle: embedded ferromagnetic particles in silicone respond to an external rotating magnetic field, causing the body to writhe. The robot needs no onboard motors or power — external magnetic fields induce motion, and the soft body's compliance means it navigates confined fluid channels without getting stuck.

    how it actually works

    A silicone body is embedded with aligned paramagnetic particles (iron oxide, ~2 μm). An external rotating magnetic field (gradient ~1 T/cm, 1 Hz) exerts a torque on the particle population.

    1. The magnetic torque causes local stresses in the silicone proportional to the field gradient and particle alignment.
    2. These stresses propagate as elastic waves along the body: the silicone material couples the stress at one point to strains in nearby material.
    3. The wave pattern is phase-lagged along the length: the tail wave lags the head by ~90°, creating a propulsive undulation like a fish fin.
    4. Swim speed is set by the rotation frequency of the external field: higher frequency → faster undulation → faster swimming.
    5. The soft body compliance means the robot can squeeze through narrow channels (smaller than its rest diameter) and reform on the other side.

Series-elastic & compliant joints

robotics
the problem

A stiff motor driving a legged foot receives a ground-reaction impulse of 3× body weight at landing — rigid transmission spikes motor current to saturation; a spring in series absorbs the impulse and lets the motor respond at its comfortable bandwidth.

the approaches

Putting a spring between motor and load separates force control from position control, absorbs impact, and makes the joint safe to touch.

This one mechanical insight underlies most modern legged robots and co-robot arms.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Choose spring stiffness k = 800 N/m: natural frequency √(k/mleg) = 28 Hz, above gait (2 Hz), below motor bandwidth (80 Hz).
  2. At heel-strike: 80 N impulse, spring compresses 10 cm → stores 4 J.
  3. Force = k × deflection = 800 × 0.10 = 80 N read from spring encoder; no wrist F/T sensor needed.
  4. Motor sees a smoothed 80 N ramp over 35 ms instead of a 2 ms spike — stays within current limit.
  5. Energy return on push-off: spring releases 4 J → reduces hip motor demand by 18% per stride.
  • ROBO
    Bipedal Walking with Continuously Compliant Robotic Legs — spring legs tuned to natural gait frequency cut motor demand across walking speeds

    Rigid legs transfer ground impact directly to the hip motor, causing large current spikes that limit speed and efficiency. A leg with a spring that is tuned to the walking frequency absorbs the impact over 100 ms instead of 1 ms, letting the motor stay within its power budget and recover energy on the rebound. Mechanical compliance frees the motor from impact forces, and the spring's oscillation naturally matches the gait rhythm, reducing the control effort to almost nothing.

    how it actually works

    A series-elastic leg has a spring between the motor and the foot. The spring constant is tuned so the spring's natural frequency equals the robot's natural walking frequency.

    1. Measure the robot's natural frequency during stance: the period between consecutive foot contacts on level ground, ignoring motor input (passive dynamics). This is typically 1–2 Hz for a 10 kg biped.
    2. Design the spring: k = (2π·f)²·m_leg, where f is natural frequency and m_leg is the effective mass of the leg. For f = 1.5 Hz and m_leg = 2 kg, k ≈ 560 N/m.
    3. At heel strike, the spring compresses over ~100 ms, storing energy in the spring, not dissipating it as heat in the joint.
    4. The motor reads spring deflection via a position sensor, which gives direct force measurement (F = k·Δx) without a separate force-torque sensor.
    5. During push-off, the spring releases its stored energy, reducing the motor's active work by 15–25% per stride and enabling faster walking speeds.
  • ROBO
    Adaptive Ankle-Foot Prosthesis with Passive Agonist-Antagonist Design — two springs in opposition give energy return and push-off without active sensing

    A prosthetic foot must absorb impact and then push off with minimal active control. Two springs arranged in opposition (one resisting plantarflexion, one resisting dorsiflexion) mimic the biological ankle's antagonist muscles: as the foot hits the ground, the plantarflex spring compresses and stores energy; as the user pushes off, that energy releases to help lift the foot. Mechanical opposition replaces active sensing and control, making the prosthesis work passively during most of the gait cycle.

    how it actually works

    The prosthesis has two springs: one at the ankle joint and one at the toe. The springs are pre-tensioned and configured to always oppose each other.

    1. During stance (heel contact), the plantarflex spring compresses to absorb the body's weight over 0.3 s (slow compression damped by friction in the joint).
    2. This compression stores potential energy: E = ½·k·(Δx)²; for k = 2000 N/m and Δx = 0.04 m, E = 1.6 J.
    3. As the body transitions to push-off, the plantarflex spring rebounds, pushing the foot into plantarflexion and augmenting the push-off force.
    4. Meanwhile, the dorsiflexion spring yields, allowing the foot to plantarflex without resistance, and stores its own energy.
    5. The net effect: spring energy return of ~0.8 J per step, reducing the metabolic cost of walking by ~8% compared to a fully passive foot, and improving comfort because the transition is smooth rather than abrupt.
  • ROBO
    Active Remote Center of Compliance for Peg-in-Hole Assembly — mechanically passive compliance at the tool tip simplifies high-precision insertion

    Inserting a peg into a hole by vision alone fails if there is any misalignment — the peg binds and the motor stalls. A remote center of compliance (RCC) is a passive mechanical device (springs and levers) that implements a virtual pivot point: when the peg contacts the hole chamfer, the contact forces cause the tip to rotate and slide into alignment without jamming. Compliance centered at the tool tip removes the need for active force feedback, and the assembly succeeds even with 5 mm initial position error.

    how it actually works

    An RCC is a set of springs arranged around a common point (the tool tip). Springs in different directions have different stiffnesses to create asymmetric compliance.

    1. Mount the tool on three springs in a cartesian arrangement (X, Y, Z directions), each with stiffness k.
    2. Pre-tension the springs so they sum to zero force in free space.
    3. When the peg contacts the hole lip at angle θ, the contact force creates a moment about the spring center.
    4. This moment causes the springs to deform asymmetrically: the contacted side compresses more, pulling the peg in the direction needed to align with the hole.
    5. The peg slides into the hole without jamming because the compliance automatically guides it; typical success rate improves from 40% (rigid arm) to 92% (RCC) on industrial trials.

Grippers, hands & end-effectors

robotics
the problem

A three-fingered hand must grasp objects from a 6 cm cylinder to a 2 cm sphere without re-grasping — a fully actuated hand needs 9 motors; underactuation with passive routing achieves this with one.

the approaches

The end-effector is where the robot touches the world, and its design almost entirely determines what tasks are reachable.

Underactuation and passive compliance let a small number of motors grasp a large variety of object shapes.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Route a single tendon through a distal-first differential: finger closes proximal joint first, then distal automatically.
  2. Set tendon pretension at 0.8 N: fingers conform to object geometry without active sensing.
  3. Grasp a 60 mm cylinder: all three fingers wrap; contact at 6 points.
  4. Grasp a 20 mm sphere: distal phalanges curl inward; 3-point contact, 1.2 N grasp force.
  5. Cycle 500 times: tendon elongation < 0.3 mm (nylon-coated stainless steel, E = 200 GPa).
  • ROBO
    Model Q-II — Underactuated Hand with Enhanced Grasping Modes — single-tendon hand that switches between power and pinch grasp via passive routing

    A three-fingered hand with nine motors (three per finger) is heavy and complex. An underactuated hand uses a single tendon routed through a differential to control all three fingers: pulling the tendon closes the proximal joints first, and then the distal joints curl inward automatically as the tendon tightens. Passive routing trades motors for mechanical ingenuity, and a single pull can grasp a surprising range of object geometries — from cylinders to thin sheets — without re-grasping.

    how it actually works

    The hand's tendon is routed through a distal-first differential pulley system. Each finger has a two-joint chain: proximal and distal.

    1. Pull the tendon: it first acts on proximal joints of all three fingers, closing them simultaneously.
    2. As the proximal joints saturate (reach their limit), the route changes and the tendon tension rises, now pulling the distal joints.
    3. The distal joints curl inward over the object, conforming to its shape without active sensing of individual joint angles.
    4. A single force measurement (at the tendon) is sufficient; the hand achieves grasp from a 6 cm cylinder to a 2 cm sphere using the same pull command.
    5. Pre-tension on the tendon (0.8 N) sets the grip force without feedback: release happens by pulling an antagonist spring, resetting the hand for the next grasp.
  • ROBO
    Chain-Driven Adaptive Underactuated Fingers for Prosthetic Hands — chain transmission gives adaptive finger closure without independent finger motors

    A prosthetic hand must grasp diverse objects quickly, but battery and motor weight limit the actuation budget. A chain-driven finger converts one motor's linear motion into adaptive finger closure: as the chain slides, the finger's joints close in sequence, and the chain coupling ensures that stiff objects trigger early joint saturation while soft objects allow full closure. The chain mechanics adaptively distribute force, matching grip to object stiffness without sensing.

    how it actually works

    Each finger has a steel chain running through its interior, with the chain attached to the distal phalanx and threaded through pulleys at each joint.

    1. A single motor pulls the chain: as the chain retracts, it first closes the proximal joint (lower pulley radius, mechanical advantage 2:1).
    2. Once proximal is closed, the chain path changes and pulls the middle joint, closing it with a different mechanical advantage.

vision
the problem

A robot touching an unknown deformable object must predict how much it will deform under 2 N — a purely geometric model gives zero deformation, but the Young's modulus is not written anywhere on the object's surface.

the approaches

Knowing the shape of an object is not enough; the controller also needs to know how it will deform, slide, or break when touched.

These papers embed physical material properties into neural representations so that simulation and prediction stay grounded in real physics.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Apply a 0.5 N probe force at the surface while recording depth from a wrist camera.
  2. Fit a Hertzian contact model: δ = (3F/(4E*√R))2/3; measured δ = 1.2 mm → E* = 28 kPa.
  3. Map E* across the surface with 9 probe points (3×3 grid, 8 mm spacing) — builds a stiffness field.
  4. Feed stiffness field into NeuROK-style network: predict deformation under 2 N at untouched location.
  5. Prediction error: 0.9 mm RMS vs 8.4 mm RMS from a uniform-E baseline.

Part IV

Where it stands

Hardware and mechanism design has made enormous strides — and has hard problems that software cannot paper over. What is genuinely still open:

Precision vs compliance. Soft and compliant mechanisms give safety and adaptability but lose the positional accuracy rigid systems offer. Getting both at once is not solved.
Actuation density vs weight. High-force actuators are heavy; lightweight designs sacrifice torque. Artificial muscles and new materials inch toward biological density but are not there yet.
Task specialization vs generality. A gripper tuned for one object class often fails another; general end-effectors trade off peak performance. Morphology that adapts at the task boundary is still an open problem.
Simulation fidelity vs compute for co-design. Accurate physical simulation of soft bodies, contacts, and materials is expensive; fast models introduce errors that mislead the optimizer. The two goals pull in opposite directions.
Manufacturing tolerance vs performance. Parts that perform as designed in CAD often do not after fabrication variation. Closing the design-to-hardware gap without expensive metrology is unsolved.
The tactile hardware sim-to-real gap. Tactile sensors in simulation do not match real sensor noise, hysteresis, and spatial resolution; policies trained with simulated touch often fail on the real sensor.
