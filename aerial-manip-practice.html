Aerial manipulation — the real problem

Bolt an arm onto an underactuated quadrotor and every reach, kick, descent, and push becomes a fight to keep the flying body trimmed, level, and in place while the manipulator does its job.

← the aerial manip demo · the build lab · home
Each stage below pairs our measured demo with the real practical problem behind it: who actually hits it (the industry or system), what it costs when it fails (real failure modes and incidents), how it is solved in production today, and the honest gap between our clean sim and the real thing. The demo captures the essence; this is the world it lives in.
1

Trim for the reach — comtrim

Our demo: extending the 0.4 kg arm to 0.5 m shifts the center of mass 0.083 m off the rotor axis; an equal thrust split leaves a 1.96 N·m net moment that tips the aircraft, while reallocating the two rotors (7.85 N vs 15.7 N) cancels it to 0.0 N·m.

Who actually hits it

This is the founding problem of aerial manipulation, studied by the labs behind the EU H2020 AEROARMS project (2015–2019), coordinated by the University of Seville's GRVC group with partners including DLR and CATEC, and by university groups such as UPenn's GRASP Lab and ETH Zurich that mount arms and grippers on multirotors.

What it costs when it fails

A multirotor is balanced around its rotor geometry, so the instant an arm extends, the combined center of mass moves off the thrust axis; if the controller keeps splitting thrust evenly, the residual moment (weight times the offset) rolls or pitches the aircraft and it drifts or tips over.

How it's solved in production

Control allocation is re-solved for the shifted center of mass: the mixer commands unequal rotor thrusts whose net torque cancels the gravity moment from the offset load, and researchers model the arm's varying centroid explicitly so the trim tracks the arm as it moves.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo assumes the arm mass, reach, and offset are known exactly and the air is still; a real system must estimate an uncertain payload's mass and grasp point, and wind gusts add moments of their own that no static re-trim anticipates. Aerial manipulation remains largely a research field with few fielded products.

2

Reject the arm's kick — reaction

Our demo: accelerating the arm at 12 rad/s² kicks the lighter body (arm inertia 0.05 vs body 0.02) to a 0.075 rad (4.3°) peak tilt uncompensated; feeding the reaction torque forward to the rotors holds the tilt at 0.0°.

Who actually hits it

Every group flying an arm on a quadrotor hits this dynamic coupling — GRVC Seville, ETH Zurich, and UPenn GRASP among them — and the aerial-manipulation control literature is full of feedforward and observer-based torque-compensation schemes built specifically to reject the arm's reaction on the body.

What it costs when it fails

Newton's third law couples the arm and body: swinging the manipulator drives an equal and opposite reaction torque into the airframe, and because the airframe is often lighter than or comparable to the arm, an uncompensated swing pitches or rolls the whole aircraft mid-task.

How it's solved in production

The known or estimated reaction torque from arm acceleration is fed forward onto the rotors so the attitude controller counters the kick before it grows, with the arm's centripetal, Coriolis, and gravity coupling terms treated as a disturbance to be canceled — an approach reported across the aerial-manipulator control literature.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses fixed inertias and a perfectly known arm acceleration; a real system must estimate coupling terms online as the arm reconfigures and carries an unknown payload, and the feedforward is only as good as that model. This is still research-stage work, not a hardened product.

3

Settle near the surface — groundeffect

Our demo: descending to a 0.2 m grab height with 0.15 m rotors, holding the free-air hover thrust (23.5 N) leaves a 0.429 m altitude error as the drone rides its own cushion; compensating the thrust for ground effect drives the error to 0.0 m.

Who actually hits it

Ground effect is a well-established rotorcraft phenomenon and a known nuisance for any aerial task that operates close to a surface, including the contact-inspection and grasping work in the AEROARMS program and university aerial-manipulation labs; it has been characterized experimentally for multirotors in the aerodynamics literature.

What it costs when it fails

Near a surface, the rotor wake recirculates and the same rotor RPM produces more thrust than it does in free air; a controller holding the free-air hover thrust makes the drone unexpectedly pop up on its own cushion, so it cannot settle down to the object it is trying to grab.

How it's solved in production

The thrust command is compensated for the ground-effect increase, using models or empirical corrections that scale thrust with height above the surface (measurable for heights below about one rotor radius) so the aircraft can hold the low altitude needed to reach the target.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses a clean height-dependent thrust model over flat ground; real surfaces are uneven, tilted, or rough, the effect changes with a moving or object-occluded surface below the rotors, and it interacts with the disturbances from the arm — none of which a simple correction captures. Deployment near real structures stays experimental.

4

Push without tumbling — contact

Our demo: commanding 0.05 m into a 6000 N/m wall with stiff position control spikes the contact force to 235.2 N and throws the flying body off; an admittance controller that yields on contact holds the peak force to 0.6 N.

Who actually hits it

Contact-based inspection was the core goal of AEROARMS (validated at a refinery in Germany and a cement factory in Spain), and ETH Zurich demonstrated axis-selective impedance and active interaction-force control on a fully actuated, omnidirectional tilt-rotor platform for push-and-slide non-destructive testing of concrete.

What it costs when it fails

A flying body has almost no ability to brace against a wall, so stiff position control fights the surface: a small commanded penetration into a stiff wall produces a large contact force, and that reaction wrench throws the underactuated aircraft off its hover.

How it's solved in production

Admittance and impedance control let the aircraft yield on contact — the controller trades commanded position for measured force so the platform behaves like a compliant spring-damper against the wall — and fully actuated or omnidirectional designs add force sensing to control the interaction directly rather than fighting it.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses a known linear wall stiffness and a compliant controller in isolation; real contact involves uncertain, nonlinear surface stiffness, friction and slip, and the added complication that most underactuated quadrotors cannot point thrust into the wall the way a fully actuated platform can. These systems are demonstrated in labs and pilots, not routinely deployed.

5

Hold the catch — retrim

Our demo: closing the gripper on a 0.5 kg payload adds 4.9 N of weight; holding the pre-grasp hover thrust sinks the 2.4 kg drone 0.497 m before recovery, while re-trimming thrust and allocation the instant the grasp closes limits the drop to 0.015 m.

Who actually hits it

Grasp-and-carry is the classic aerial-manipulation task — UPenn's GRASP Lab demonstrated avian-inspired high-speed grasping on a quadrotor, and the AEROARMS partners and other groups work on aerial grasping and payload transport where the aircraft must stay stable through the pick-up.

What it costs when it fails

The moment the gripper closes on a payload, the aircraft's mass and center of mass jump; if the controller keeps commanding the pre-grasp hover thrust it is now under-lifted and off-balance, so the drone sinks and drifts until feedback catches up — a dangerous transient close to the object and any nearby structure.

How it's solved in production

The controller re-trims the instant the grasp is detected: total thrust is raised to carry the added weight and the allocation is re-solved for the new center of mass, ideally feeding the mass change forward rather than waiting for the altitude error to accumulate.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo knows the payload mass and the exact moment of grasp; a real system must react to a payload of uncertain and sometimes shifting mass, ride out swing if the load is not rigidly held, and do it all on a battery whose limited flight time shrinks further under the extra load. Reliable, unsupervised operation near people and structures remains an open research problem.
