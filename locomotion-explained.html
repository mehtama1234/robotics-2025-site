01 · the problem

Why walking is hard

A legged robot has a dozen to fifty joints, and in the air they move through smooth, predictable physics you can plan through. The instant a foot touches down, that smoothness falls off a cliff: contact starts and stops abruptly, forces spike, and a millimetre of error flips a stable step into a slip.

On top of that, the robot must balance — keep its center of mass over its feet — while the ground varies and things shove it. Coordinating many joints, through discontinuous contact, under disturbance, all at once, is what makes walking hard, and it’s exactly where hand-tuned controllers struggle and learning shines.

the actual math — one level deeper

State = joint angles + velocities (12–50 dimensions) plus base pose; the dynamics are hybrid — they switch mode every time a foot makes or breaks contact.

Contact makes the dynamics non-smooth, so gradient-based control and smooth MPC struggle across mode boundaries.

Balance constraint: keep the (ground-projected) center of mass / ZMP inside the support polygon, under external disturbance.

02 · learn it

RL discovers the gait

For decades, gaits were hand-crafted: engineers specified footfall patterns and timing. The learned approach throws that away. Write down a reward — track this velocity, stay upright, spend little energy, don’t fall — and let RL try billions of steps in a massively parallel simulator.

What comes out is not one scripted gait but a whole repertoire: the policy discovers walking, trotting, and galloping, picks the energy-optimal one for each speed, and transitions between them smoothly — plus recovery moves no one designed. Reward shapes the behavior; the gait emerges.

the actual math — one level deeper

Maximize E[ Σₜ γᵗ r(sₜ, aₜ) ], with r ≈ velocity-tracking − energy − orientation-error − fall penalty.

Trained with PPO across thousands of parallel simulated robots; the gait and its transitions are emergent, not encoded.

Reward shaping + curriculum (harder terrain over time) do the work a hand-tuned controller’s gait scheduler used to.

03 · teach it

A privileged teacher, an onboard student

There’s a trick that makes learned locomotion work. In simulation the trainer has privileged information — the exact terrain height under each foot, the friction, the mass, even the disturbance about to hit. A policy with those facts learns fast and well.

But the real robot can’t sense them. So you train the privileged teacher first, then distill it into a student that sees only onboard signals — joint sensors, maybe a camera — and learns to reproduce the teacher’s behavior blind. Learn with cheats, deploy without them.

the actual math — one level deeper

Teacher π_T(a | s, s_priv) trains with privileged state s_priv (terrain, friction, mass, disturbance).

Student π_S(a | o_onboard, history) is trained to match the teacher (DAgger / behavior cloning) using only deployable observations.

The history window lets the student infer what the teacher was told — an implicit system identification.

04 · stay up

Balance is stepping

The intuition most people have — that balance means holding still — is wrong for a legged robot. Balance is active stepping. When a shove sends the center of mass tipping forward, standing rigid guarantees a fall.

Instead the controller predicts the capture point — roughly where the foot must land to arrest the fall — and steps there, so momentum carries the body back over its feet. Recovery from a push, catching a stumble, walking downhill: all of it is choosing the next foothold that keeps the center of mass over support.

the actual math — one level deeper

Linear inverted pendulum: capture point ξ = x + ẋ/ω, with ω = √(g/h).

Step so ξ lands inside the next support region → the center of mass decelerates back over the feet (DCM / capture-point control).

Hierarchical MPC co-optimizes stepping + upper-body to maximize the recoverable disturbance.

05 · whole body

Whole-body: legs balance while arms work

The hardest form is a humanoid doing a task while staying upright. Every arm motion shifts the center of mass; reach too far and the robot tips. You cannot control the upper and lower body separately — they fight each other.

Whole-body control solves them together: the arms pursue the task (reach, carry, push) while the legs continuously adjust to keep the center of mass over the support polygon. When walking and manipulating combine — loco-manipulation — the coupling is the whole problem, and coordinating it is the frontier of humanoid robotics.

the actual math — one level deeper

Whole-body QP: min ‖ task − J q̈ ‖² subject to the full-body dynamics, contact constraints, and torque limits.

A reaching task moves the CoM; the balance constraint (ZMP in support) forces the legs to compensate in the same solve.

Loco-manipulation couples a moving base with the manipulation task — solved jointly, not in sequence.

RL locomotion: learn gaits from reward

robotics
the problem

A legged robot has to move at many speeds over varied surfaces. Specifying a gait by hand means writing footfall timing, swing trajectories, and energy schedules for every surface and speed — hundreds of hand-tuned parameters that break whenever conditions change. The designer’s bottleneck is that the right gait for mud at 0.8 m/s is different from gravel at 1.5 m/s, and there are too many such cases to enumerate.

the approaches

The dominant approach: specify a reward, train in massively parallel randomized simulation, and let the gait — and its transitions and recoveries — emerge. RL beats hand-tuning by automating what a gait scheduler used to do by hand.

Recent work discovers whole gait libraries and energy-optimal switching in a single policy.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Define the reward: forward velocity tracking (weight 1.0) + staying upright (0.5) + energy penalty (−0.001 per N·m torque) + fall penalty (−10).
  2. Randomize the sim: spawn 4,096 ANYmal instances in Isaac Gym; randomize mass (±30%), friction (0.4–1.2), push disturbances every 4 s.
  3. Train PPO for 4,000 iterations (~1.5 B environment steps); the gait transitions from a shuffling crawl to a symmetric trot by iteration 800.
  4. Observe emergence: at 1.0 m/s the policy settles on a diagonal trot; at 2.0 m/s it shifts to a bound — no one specified that schedule.
  5. Deploy zero-shot: copy the checkpoint to the real ANYmal; it walks outdoor gravel, 0% retrain (AllGaits result: 9 biological gaits in one policy).
  • ROBOAllGaits — one RL policy learns all 9 biological gaits + instant energy-optimal transitions
  • ROBOAgile But Safe — a learned reach-avoid value net gives provable safety at 2.5 m/s in clutter
  • ROBOAdaptive Energy Regularization for Gait Transition — real-time gait selection, 30% lower energy, no scripted sequence
  • ROBOThink on Your Feet — WGAN-div + hybrid model for seamless human-like humanoid gait switching

Model-based control & whole-body optimization

robotics
the problem

A robot needs to push a heavy box while walking: the force to push, the torques in its legs to stay balanced, and the contact timing all depend on each other. If you optimize the arm separately from the legs, the arm force shifts the center of mass and the legs tip the robot. You need a single solve that respects the full-body dynamics, contact forces, and actuator torque limits all at once — every control tick at 1000 Hz.

the approaches

The classical rigor the field keeps: solve a real-time optimization on a physics model for whole-body forces under contact and torque limits — exact contact reasoning and provable behavior, no reward engineering.

The frontier is handling non-smooth contact fast enough, and fusing it with learned components.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Model the full body: 24-DOF humanoid rigid-body tree; 6 contact points; torque limits ±80 N·m per joint.
  2. Set up the QP each tick: minimize ‖task acceleration − J·q̈‖² (reaching for target) subject to: Newton-Euler dynamics, μ·Fz ≥ |Fx|,|Fy| (friction cone), |τ| ≤ τ_max.
  3. Solve in ≤1 ms: active-set QP solver (OSQP); the optimal joint accelerations land in the solver; integrate to get torque commands.
  4. Walking test: robot pushes a 15 kg box at 0.3 m/s; WBC allocates 60% of leg effort to balance and 40% to forward push force — the split emerges from the optimization.
  5. Compare to separate control: sequential arm-then-leg control tips the robot on every push attempt; joint WBC succeeds 94% of trials (MPPI-WBC result).
  • ROBOReal-Time WBC with MPPI — first sampling-based whole-body MPC on real ANYmal, no task-specific reward
  • ROBOReduced-Order-Guided Contact-Implicit MPC — HLIP-guided CI-MPC for a 24-DOF humanoid on rough terrain
  • ROBODynamic Tube MPC — learned uncertainty tubes replace worst-case bounds for narrow-corridor agility
  • ROBOOptimal Torque Distribution on Slippery Terrain — real-time slip-aware torque allocation, no pre-trained model

Sim-to-real for legged robots

robotics
the problem

A policy trained in simulation sees perfect physics: exact friction, joint behavior, and sensor readings. The real robot has none of that — each actuator has its own lag and backlash, the floor has unknown friction, the IMU drifts. If the policy relies on exact numbers it never saw, it falls over the instant it touches real ground. The problem is bridging the gap between the simulation’s clean world and the real robot’s messy one.

the approaches

Train entirely in randomized simulation and deploy zero-shot on hardware — the enabler behind nearly every learned locomotion result — via domain randomization, privileged learning, and online adaptation.

See the data-engine explainer for the general recipe; here it’s tuned for contact and balance.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Randomize in sim: friction U(0.35, 1.25), link mass ±20%, motor damping ±30%, joint offset noise σ=0.03 rad, push disturbances every 5 s up to 150 N.
  2. Train privileged teacher: teacher sees exact friction, terrain height, and mass; trains to 98% upright across 4,096 parallel envs in ~2 h on 8 A100s.
  3. Distill student: student sees only joint encoders + IMU + depth camera; mimics teacher via DAgger over 500 M steps; achieves 93% of teacher’s terrain coverage.
  4. Deploy zero-shot: copy checkpoint to Berkeley Humanoid; walks outdoor gravel, 5-cm steps, and recovers 8/10 surprise pushes — no fine-tuning.
  5. Snow test (DWL): add a denoising world model head to the student; zero-shot deployment on snow and 8° inclines; 0 falls in 45-min outdoor trial.
  • ROBOBerkeley Humanoid — a low-cost humanoid achieves outdoor agility + perturbation recovery from RL alone
  • ROBODenoising World Model Learning (DWL) — first humanoid deployment on snow / stairs / inclines, zero-shot
  • ROBORL2AC — 1000Hz adaptive control corrects disturbances, terrain, and payload live
  • ROBODreamFLEX — joint fault estimation + RL keeps walking under a locked or overheated joint

Terrain, rough ground & parkour

robotics
the problem

A flat-ground gait controller breaks the moment it hits a curb: the foot expects flat contact, finds a step edge instead, and the robot falls. The problem is that the controller needs to know the terrain shape under the next footfall — which a camera or lidar can estimate but with noise — and adjust foot placement, step height, and stance width before the foot lands, not after.

the approaches

Generalize across surfaces — stairs, gaps, mud, sand — and pull off agile maneuvers like jumping and parkour, using perception (elevation, semantics) or commonsense reasoning to read traversability.

The recurring move is a curriculum of ever-harder terrain plus a perceptive internal model.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Build an elevation map: depth camera → local 2.5D grid, 0.02 m/cell, 3 m radius; update at 10 Hz.
  2. Encode terrain features: pass the heightmap crop under each foot through a 3-layer CNN; 64-dim latent fed into the locomotion policy alongside joint state.
  3. Curriculum over 6 stages: flat → small bumps (±3 cm) → steps (8 cm) → gaps (20 cm) → rough rocks → 14-step staircase; policy trained for 2 B steps total.
  4. Jumping test: quadruped crosses a 14-step staircase continuously, 0.6 m/s; success 82% vs 7% for flat-ground baseline (Agile Continuous Jumping result).
  5. Semantic traversal: DINO features label mud as non-traversable; VLM planner re-routes around it zero-shot (Watch Your STEPP / VLM reasoning result).
  • ROBOAgile Continuous Jumping in Discontinuous Terrains — first continuous quadruped jumping across 14-step stairs + sparse stones
  • ROBOWatch Your STEPP — self-supervised DINO features estimate semantic traversability, no labels
  • ROBOCommonsense Reasoning for Legged Adaptation with VLMs — an in-context VLM planner clears obstacle courses zero-shot
  • ROBOLearning Humanoid Locomotion with a Perceptive Internal Model — elevation-map perception enables robust stair climbing

Humanoid whole-body control

robotics
the problem

A humanoid has 30+ degrees of freedom split across legs, torso, and arms. If you run one controller for walking and another for the arms, they conflict: the walking controller fixes the hips, the arm controller wants to rotate them. A single policy must simultaneously keep the center of mass over the feet, track a navigation goal, and fulfill an arm task — at 50 Hz, without knowing in advance which of those tasks will be asked.

the approaches

Coordinate a high-DOF humanoid’s balance, legs, and expressive upper body in one policy — unifying walking, manipulation, and human-like motion where separate controllers would fight.

Distillation into a single versatile controller is the emerging pattern.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Train skill specialists first: separate RL policies for walking, standing manipulation, and arm-only motion; each trained to convergence (~500 M steps each).
  2. Distill into one controller (HOVER): a single transformer policy takes mode-flag + goal as input; trained with DAgger from all specialists jointly; 34-DOF G1 humanoid.
  3. Navigation test: robot walks 20 m corridor at 0.8 m/s, avoids 3 obstacles; then switches to tabletop pick-and-place mid-walk; 0 falls in 50 trials.
  4. Dance imitation: upper-body mocap tracks a human wave; lower-body RL maintains balance while hips shift ±15° — the two halves never conflict (Expressive WBC result).
  5. Measure mode switching: HOVER transitions navigation→manipulation in ≤0.3 s with no manual re-initialization; specialist-only approach requires manual mode reset.
  • ROBOHOVER — one distilled neural whole-body controller across navigation, loco-manip, tabletop
  • ROBOExpressive Whole-Body Control — upper-body imitation + lower-body RL mimics dance and handshakes, sim-to-real
  • ROBODesign and Control of a Bipedal Robotic Character — artist-directed expressive motion blended with RL for entertainment robotics
  • ROBOAngular DCM — first spatial divergent-component-of-motion capturing rotational balance

Loco-manipulation

robotics
the problem

A fixed-arm manipulator can only reach what is directly in front of it. A mobile base extends reach, but walking shifts the base while the arm is in motion, so the end-effector drifts. Coordinating a moving platform with a task-space arm target is the core difficulty: the arm must compensate for the base’s motion in real time, or the robot misses the target every time it takes a step.

the approaches

Walk and manipulate at the same time: decouple the task-centric end-effector motion from the robot-centric whole-body dynamics, so a mobile base extends reach while RL learns the coordination.

The coupling between base motion and the manipulation task is the crux.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Collect base demonstrations with UMI: a human carries a hand-held gripper through a cooking task; wrist camera + IMU record 50 trajectories, no full-body capture.
  2. Translate to whole-body policy: UMI-on-Legs maps the gripper trajectory to a joint whole-body plan; the base follows a heading while the arm tracks the demonstrated wrist path.
  3. Mobile ALOHA setup: low-cost bimanual robot; 50 teleoperated demos of opening a fridge; imitation learning trains a policy that generalizes to 3 fridge positions.
  4. Test loco-manipulation coupling: robot walks 1 m to a table while arm holds a cup; without coupling, cup tips 8/10 trials; with whole-body coordination, 0/10 spills.
  5. Success rate: UMI-on-Legs achieves 70%+ on mobile cooking tasks vs 12% for sequential base-then-arm control.
  • ROBOUMI-on-Legs — hand-held UMI demos + a whole-body controller → 70%+ mobile manipulation
  • ROBOMobile ALOHA — low-cost whole-body teleop learns cooking, doors, elevators from 50 demos
  • ROBOMulti-Agent Loco-Manipulation for Quadrupedal Pushing — hierarchical multi-agent RL, +36% success on long-horizon pushing
  • ROBOBiFold — language-conditioned bimanual cloth folding, state-of-the-art

Learning from demonstration & motion priors

robotics
the problem

Humans move naturally — smooth, stable, expressive — but a robot that tries to copy that motion directly falls: the human body proportions are different, the robot’s joints have different ranges, and a human can casually lean in a way that puts a robot’s center of mass outside its support polygon. The gap between what looks right in human motion and what is physically feasible on a different body is the core problem.

the approaches

Use abundant human and mocap data to seed or regularize controllers — retargeting motion to the robot, using adversarial motion priors for naturalness, or few real demos plus hierarchy.

The prize is human-like motion and sample efficiency; the gap is embodiment.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Collect mocap: 40 hours of human motion at 120 Hz (HumanPlus dataset); retarget skeleton via joint-angle mapping, scaled to robot proportions.
  2. Identify contact moments: feet contact ground when height <3 cm and vertical velocity <0; use these as robot contact targets, not the human’s actual foot position.
  3. Train shadowing policy: humanoid trained to track retargeted motion in sim; RL reward = joint-angle tracking + contact-phase matching; 1 B steps, Isaac Gym.
  4. Deploy imitation: robot copies a human waving and walking in real time via onboard camera at 30 Hz; 60% task success on 12 manipulation demos (HumanPlus result).
  5. Adversarial prior (AMP style): a discriminator scores how human-like the motion is; its output added as a reward term keeps motion smooth even on unseen tasks.
  • ROBOHumanPlus — 40h of human mocap → humanoid shadowing + imitation, 60–100% real success
  • ROBOExpressive Whole-Body Control — mocap upper-body imitation blended with RL lower-body
  • ROBOSkillMimicGen — auto-segments + stitches human demos into 24k synthetic from 60 examples
  • ROBOImitation with Limited Actions via Diffusion + Koopman — few action-labeled demos via latent Koopman actions

Balance, recovery & fall avoidance

robotics
the problem

A robot pushed from the side has milliseconds to respond before it topples. The math says: step to where the capture point (center of mass projected forward by velocity divided by natural frequency) will land inside the next support region. If the foot cannot reach that point in time — because the disturbance is too large — the robot needs a different strategy: crouch, spread the stance, or drop to a controlled landing. Hand-tuned policies handle only the cases their designer anticipated.

the approaches

Survive large disturbances — pushes, terrain drops, faults — with learned backup policies, capture-point stepping, and hierarchical MPC that co-optimizes stepping with upper-body motion.

The goal is graceful recovery instead of catastrophic failure.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Define the capture point: ξ = x_CoM + ẋ_CoM / ω₀, where ω₀ = √(g/h_CoM) ≈ 3.1 rad/s for a 1 m tall robot.
  2. Compute required step: step so ξ lands 5 cm inside the next support polygon; if unreachable in one step, switch to multi-step recovery mode.
  3. RL for large disturbances: train FRASA on 200 N·m impulse pushes; the agent learns crouch-and-rebalance for pushes beyond the capture-point reach (~120 N·m).
  4. Hierarchical MPC test: co-optimize step timing + arm counter-swing; recoverable impulse rises from 95 N·m (leg-only) to 220 N·m (+131%, paper result).
  5. Stand-up from fall: FRASA recovers from a flat-on-floor fall in 4.2 s on average; the prior champion (hand-tuned) required 8.1 s and failed if the fall angle differed by >15°.
  • ROBOFRASA — one DRL agent for fall recovery + stand-up, beats the RoboCup 2023 champion
  • ROBOAdapting Gait Frequency for Push-Recovery via Hierarchical MPC — +131% recoverable impulse by co-optimizing stepping + arms
  • ROBOAgile But Safe — dual agile+recovery policies with a learned reach-avoid safety net
  • ROBOSafety-Critical Locomotion in Infeasible Paths — a hierarchy of safety conditions resolves conflicting constraints

Diffusion / generative locomotion policies

robotics
the problem

A regression policy (MSE loss) outputs the mean of all plausible actions. For locomotion, there are many valid gaits for a given speed on a given surface — trot and canter are both fine at 1.5 m/s — and averaging them produces a mushy, unstable motion that is neither. The problem is that locomotion is multimodal: you need a policy that can commit to one mode, not blend them.

the approaches

Bring diffusion policies to legged control: model the multimodal distribution of valid gaits and recoveries from offline data, with the expressiveness and long-horizon sequencing regression lacks.

The challenge is squeezing sampling into a fast locomotion control loop (see the diffusion-policies explainer).

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Collect offline data: 10 M transitions from an RL policy across 6 terrains; store (observation, action) without environment interaction.
  2. Train diffusion policy: DDPM with 20-step denoising; condition on proprioceptive history (50 ms window); action chunk = 10 future joint positions.
  3. Distill to 1-step (DiffuseLoco): consistency distillation reduces inference to 1 denoising step; latency drops from 18 ms to 1.2 ms — fits the 50 Hz control loop.
  4. Multimodal test: on an asymmetric terrain, diffusion policy trots 60% of trials, canters 40%; regression policy always trots (the average) and falls 3× more often.
  5. Zero-shot transfer: trained in sim, deployed on real Unitree A1; beats BC and RL baselines on slope stability by 15% (DiffuseLoco result).
  • ROBODiffuseLoco — first real-time diffusion legged control from offline data, beats RL/BC on stability
  • ROBOPreference-Aligned Diffusion Planner for Quadrupeds — offline diffusion + preference refinement → zero-shot sim-to-real gaits
  • ROBOTransferable Latent-to-Latent Locomotion — a latent diffusion recovery module adapts one core across robots
  • ROBOProDapt — long-term contact-memory diffusion completes tasks from proprioception only

Whole-body from language

robotics
the problem

A robot controlled by language commands must map "walk to the red box and pick it up" to a sequence of body motions — but those words do not specify joint angles, step timing, or which foot to shift first. The challenge is grounding language to physical affordances: where is the box, how far can the arm reach without tipping, and which sub-behaviors (walk, reach, grasp) need to compose, in what order.

the approaches

Command a body in words: a VLM or LLM turns an instruction into whole-body behavior, grounds affordances, or plans long-horizon tasks — zero-shot, without task-specific training.

This is where locomotion meets the VLA wave (see the VLA explainer).

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Parse the command: VLM (GPT-4V) receives the instruction + an image; outputs a plan: [navigate 2 m north, stop, grasp object at (0.4, 0, 0.8)].
  2. Ground to primitives: each plan step maps to a parameterized skill: navigate(goal), reach(xyz), grasp(); the whole-body controller executes each.
  3. Run on Harmon: a VLM generates text-aligned motion tokens; a motion decoder converts to joint angles; lower-body RL tracks the balance while upper body follows the tokens.
  4. Unseen-obstacle test (Commonsense VLM): a never-seen inflatable ball blocks the path; VLM identifies it as soft, plans a kick to clear it; robot succeeds 7/8 trials zero-shot.
  5. Preference refinement: 4 human corrections in natural language ("walk slower") update the LLM prior; the adjusted behavior persists across all future rollouts (Language-Guided Preference result).
  • ROBOHarmon — a VLM refines human-motion priors into text-aligned humanoid whole-body motion
  • ROBOCommonsense Reasoning for Legged Adaptation with VLMs — in-context VLM planning clears novel obstacle courses
  • ROBOLanguage-Guided Preference Learning for Quadruped Behaviors — 4 human queries yield expressive behaviors from LLM priors
  • ROBORT-H — a language-motion hierarchy predicts primitives and accepts human corrections

Physics-based motion generation

vision
the problem

A neural network that generates human motion from text typically optimizes for looking plausible frame-by-frame, not for obeying physics. The result foot-skates: feet slide along the floor during a stance phase because the network never saw a force equation. To fix this you need the physics constraints — Newton-Euler, friction cone, angular momentum — inside the generation loop, not as a post-hoc filter.

the approaches

The vision side: generate human/humanoid motion that’s physically plausible by embedding a simulator or physics loss — so motion respects gravity, contact, and momentum instead of floating.

Raw kinematic generation foot-skates and floats; physics is what grounds it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Estimate contact from video: compute wrist/foot acceleration from pose estimates; high acceleration near the object = likely contact force; use this as a weak supervision signal.
  2. Physics loss in diffusion (PAMotion): at each denoising step, compute predicted ground reaction force; penalize violations of the friction cone (|Fx|/Fz > μ=0.8) directly in the score.
  3. RL physics controller (GRIP): an LSTM predicts keyframe poses; a PPO controller in Isaac Gym tracks them; the adversarial motion prior keeps the style human-like.
  4. Foot-skate measure: foot velocity during stance <0.005 m/s with physics loss vs 0.04 m/s without — an 8× reduction in skating.
  5. Monocular HOI refinement: noisy monocular interaction estimate fed into RL physics sim; sim corrects hand-object penetration from 12 cm to <1 cm (Recovering Plausible HOI result).
  • CVPRPAMotion — uses object acceleration to infer contact, applies a physics-aware loss in diffusion
  • CVPRGRIP — LSTM kinematics + a PPO physics controller + adversarial motion prior in Isaac Gym
  • CVPRRecovering Physically Plausible HOI from Monocular Video — RL physics simulation refines noisy monocular interactions
  • CVPRPhysHO — physics-constrained dynamic 3D-Gaussian human + object from one video

Humanoid imitation & mocap retargeting

vision
the problem

Human motion capture records joint angles on a human skeleton. A robot has different limb lengths, fewer fingers, and different joint ranges. Simply copying the angles produces a robot that leans too far, whose hands pass through objects, or whose feet are off the ground. The retargeting problem is: given a human pose, find the robot pose that preserves what matters — contact locations, relative hand positions, gaze direction — while being kinematically feasible for the robot.

the approaches

Convert human motion capture to a humanoid’s different body and learn control that tracks it — preserving contacts (a handshake must still touch) across the embodiment gap.

Contact-preserving retargeting is the recurring technical key.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Record human motion: 60 Hz mocap of a handshake; 30-frame sequence with 17 joint angles per frame.
  2. Retarget with contact preservation (Beyond Mimicry): for each frame, solve: minimize joint-angle deviation from human, subject to: right-hand pair-wise distance <3 cm (handshake contact preserved).
  3. Build hierarchical diffusion policy: contact retargeting → high-level plan tokens → low-level joint trajectory; deployed on a G1 humanoid.
  4. Contact accuracy test: hand contact maintained in 89% of frames vs 52% for naive angle-copying retargeting.
  5. Sim-to-real: Gallant uses dual-LiDAR voxel grids + PPO to transfer retargeted motion; achieves 90%+ upright rate across 8 terrain types, zero-shot.
  • CVPRBeyond Mimicry — contact-preserving retargeting (pairwise-distance consistency) + hierarchical diffusion policy on a real G1
  • CVPRGallant — dual-LiDAR voxel grids → PPO humanoid locomotion, 90%+ sim-to-real on 8 terrains
  • CVPRRAM: Recover Any 3D Human Motion in-the-Wild — motion-aware tracking + memory attention for stable reconstruction
  • CVPRMulti-level Causal LLM Text-to-Motion — causal motion tokenizer matched to LLM causality for aligned generation

Physically-plausible motion synthesis

vision
the problem

Text-to-motion models trained on internet data reproduce average human motion statistics, but average motion ignores the floor: the average step height for walking on a flat surface is the wrong step height for walking up stairs. Without scene geometry in the loop, generated motion walks through tables, stands with feet floating above uneven ground, and interpenetrates with every object it “interacts” with. Grounding generation to scene geometry is the problem.

the approaches

Generate full-body motion that avoids foot-skating, floating, and interpenetration — even without a full simulator — via part-aware tokenizers, contact-aware losses, and physical-feasibility filtering.

The aim is motion that looks and behaves right, at scale, from text.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Encode scene geometry (SceMoS): extract per-frame heightmaps at the foot positions; concatenate with the motion latent as a conditioning signal in the diffusion model.
  2. Train on scene-motion pairs: 80k motion clips paired with their scene scans; 30% are on stairs or uneven floors; the model sees true foot-height variation.
  3. Physical-feasibility filter (OpenT2M): after generation, score each frame: foot below heightmap → invalid; discard and resample until all frames pass.
  4. Contact accuracy: SceMoS achieves 0.98 contact accuracy on HumanML3D benchmark vs 0.81 for scene-unaware baseline.
  5. Streaming (FloodDiffusion): diffusion forcing with a causal mask generates each 100 ms chunk in 12 ms — meets real-time budget for live avatar animation.
  • CVPROpenT2M — part-decomposed tokenizer + million-scale data with physical-feasibility validation
  • CVPRMotionHiFlow — hierarchical flow matching with a topology-aware motion VAE, coarse-to-fine
  • CVPRSceMoS — scene-aware synthesis grounded on per-frame heightmaps → 0.98 contact accuracy
  • CVPRFloodDiffusion — tailored diffusion forcing for low-latency streaming motion generation

Hand-object & human-scene interaction

vision
the problem

When a hand grasps a mug, the fingers must wrap the mug’s exact surface without penetrating it, and the contact force must point inward (toward the object center) or the grasp slips. Generating this from text or video is hard because the network must jointly solve hand pose, object pose, and contact geometry — and any penetration (fingers going through the mug) or wrong contact normal makes the grasp physically impossible. Post-hoc filtering helps, but learning the constraint from the start is better.

the approaches

Coordinate the whole body with the world it touches — hands grasping objects, bodies sitting and navigating scenes — under geometric and contact constraints so interactions are plausible.

Penetration and contact-consistency losses are what keep hands on objects and feet on floors.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Represent grasps in SIM(3) (GeoDexGrasp): encode hand-object geometry as equivariant point clouds; the grasp score is invariant to rotation and scale — learn once, apply everywhere.
  2. Physics refinement: candidate grasps are fed into a physics sim for 0.5 s; those that slip or penetrate are discarded; the net learns from only physically stable examples.
  3. Penetration reduction: −40% mean penetration depth vs prior SOTA; 3.2 mm vs 5.4 mm average finger-into-object distance.
  4. Scene interaction (SceMoS + OneHOI): a relational DiT conditions on the scene graph (chair, table, floor); body-scene contact accuracy 0.95 vs 0.71 for scene-blind generation.
  5. Sim-to-real hand-object video (PAM): pose→appearance→motion pipeline generates photorealistic hand-object interaction video; used to evaluate robot grasp policies without real hardware.
  • CVPRGeoDexGrasp — SIM(3)-equivariant, physics-refined dexterous grasp generation, −40% penetration
  • CVPROneHOI — a relational DiT unifies human-object interaction generation and editing
  • CVPRSceMoS — heightmap-grounded motion respects scene surfaces and ground contact
  • CVPRPAM — a pose→appearance→motion engine for sim-to-real hand-object interaction video

Text/music-to-motion & controllable avatars

vision
the problem

A digital avatar animated from a text prompt must express the right emotion, match the beat of music, follow a trajectory through space, and adopt a character’s posture — all at once, from a single input. The challenge is that each modality (text, music, trajectory, pose) lives in a different space, and fusing them without one overriding the others requires a shared latent that each can steer independently, without collapsing into the average of all.

the approaches

Drive whole-body motion from language, speech, or music — and edit it with pose, sketch, or trajectory control — unifying diverse inputs into physically-aware, controllable human animation.

The push is toward any-modality-in, plausible-motion-out, controllable and live.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Encode all modalities (Archon): text → CLIP, music beat → chroma features, trajectory → 3D waypoints, pose → joint angles; all projected to a shared 512-dim latent.
  2. Any-to-any generation: a transformer decoder generates motion tokens conditioned on the subset of available modalities; missing inputs are masked; 7 input types, 72 output tasks.
  3. Speech prosody (AudioAvatar): extract pitch, energy, and rhythm from speech; map to upper-body gesture velocity and head pose; personalized per speaker identity.
  4. Evaluate text alignment: FID 0.52 on HumanML3D (Archon) vs 1.1 for single-modality baseline; the fusion improves text fidelity when music is also present.
  5. Live editing (MotionV2V): optical-flow mask isolates a limb; user draws a new trajectory; the model re-generates only that limb, leaving appearance and other limbs intact in <0.4 s.
  • CVPRArchon — any-to-any across 7 modalities for holistic digital-human generation, 72 tasks
  • CVPRAudioAvatar — speech prosody drives personalized whole-body gesture and posture
  • CVPRMotionV2V — optical-flow-guided selective motion editing while preserving appearance
  • CVPR3D-Aware Implicit Motion Control — 3D-aware control for view-adaptive human video generation

Part IV

Where it stands

Legged robots went from lab curiosities to walking out the door in a few years. What is genuinely still hard:

Contact is still the wall. Friction, deformation, and the make/break of contact are where both physics engines and learned models stay least accurate.
Sim-to-real for humanoids. High-DOF humanoids amplify every modeling error; zero-shot transfer works but only with careful randomization and tuning.
Loco-manipulation coupling. Doing a task while staying balanced — the arms and legs fighting — is far from solved for general tasks.
Physical plausibility in generation. Generated human motion still floats, foot-skates, or interpenetrates without a simulator or careful physics losses.
Robustness & safety. Provable safety under disturbance exists for narrow cases; certifying a learned whole-body controller is open.
Data for bodies. Human mocap doesn’t match a robot’s embodiment, and on-robot data is scarce — the retargeting gap persists.
