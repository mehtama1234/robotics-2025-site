Autonomous earthmoving — the real problem

From surveying the dig to grading the finished surface, an earthmoving robot fights noisy terrain maps, slipping tracks, mis-weighed buckets, swinging loads, and out-of-tolerance surfaces — one hard problem per stage.

1

Survey the dig site — turn the terrain into a cut-fill map

Our demo: a single noisy scan reads 690 m3 of cut versus 621 m3 true (11.1% high), while averaging repeated scans lands at 637 m3 (2.5%).

Who actually hits it

Survey crews and machine-control vendors like Trimble and Topcon build site models from drone photogrammetry, LiDAR, and GNSS rovers; Built Robotics' RPD 35 pile driver surveys its own pile locations before it works.

What it costs when it fails

A biased terrain map propagates straight into an over- or under-estimated earthwork quantity, which mis-prices the bid and mis-plans the number of truckloads to haul.

How it's solved in production

Production workflows average multiple passes and fit surfaces to a design model, then compute cut-fill against a GNSS or laser reference rather than trusting one raw height grid.

Toy demo → real system

Our sim averages clean synthetic scans; real drone surveys fight vegetation, water, moving equipment, GNSS multipath in pits, and registration error between flights.

2

Regulate the dig force — pull hard without spinning the tracks

Our demo: an aggressive 140 kN command exceeds the 100 kN traction limit, so the tracks fully slip and the bucket penetrates 0.00 m; regulating to 90 kN digs 1.84 m.

Who actually hits it

Autonomous digging platforms such as Built Robotics' Exosystem retrofit, plus the traction and hydraulic controls on Caterpillar and Komatsu machines, all trade commanded dig force against available ground grip.

What it costs when it fails

Commanding more force than the tracks can hold just spins them and slides the machine backward, wasting a cycle and churning the ground instead of cutting into it.

How it's solved in production

Modern machines close the loop on hydraulic pressure and track slip, backing off bucket force to stay under the traction limit so the cutting edge keeps penetrating.

Toy demo → real system

We model one clean traction ceiling; real soil varies from mud to rock within a bucket, and grip shifts with moisture, grade, and undercarriage wear.

3

Weigh the bucket — bill the load without over- or under-filling trucks

Our demo: assuming every scoop is a full 2000 kg mis-bills the load by 589 kg (29.4%), while estimating the weight from the lift dynamics is off by just 8 kg (0.4%).

Who actually hits it

Onboard weighing systems like Trimble LOADRITE (e.g. the X2350 excavator scale) and comparable loader scales weigh material bucket-by-bucket during the normal lift.

What it costs when it fails

Guessing that each bucket comes up full mis-counts tonnage, and consistent errors either overload haul trucks — a safety and equipment-wear hazard — or leave them underloaded, wasting fuel and cycles.

How it's solved in production

Production scales infer weight from lift hydraulics and arm motion as the load rises, reaching roughly a few-percent accuracy without pausing the dig cycle, and can feed the totals to fleet software.

Toy demo → real system

Our estimator uses idealized lift dynamics; field scales must reject sloshing, partial buckets, boom motion, temperature drift, and calibration change to hold their rated tolerance.

4

Swing without spilling — move a suspended load and stop it dead

Our demo: a bang-bang swing of a 6.0 m, 4.9 s pendulum leaves 0.075 rad of residual sway, while input-shaping the command (two half-moves a half-period apart) leaves essentially zero.

Who actually hits it

Any machine that slews a hung load — cranes and the pile-handling steps on excavator-based systems such as Built Robotics' RPD — faces pendulum sway when it accelerates and stops.

What it costs when it fails

A load still swinging at the end of a move can spill, miss its placement, strike people or structures, and forces the operator to wait out the oscillation before the next step.

How it's solved in production

Anti-sway control shapes the motion command — splitting a move into pulses timed to the load's natural period (input shaping) — so the swing induced by starting cancels the swing induced by stopping.

Toy demo → real system

We assume a fixed pendulum length and period; real loads change length as cable pays out, feel wind and off-center pickup, and couple with the machine's own compliance.

5

Grade the surface — hold the blade to tolerance despite machine pitch

Our demo: an open-loop blade leaves a surface roughness of 0.082 m standard deviation, while closing the loop on a laser/GPS grade reference cuts it to 0.005 m.

Who actually hits it

2D and 3D machine-control systems from Trimble (Earthworks) and Topcon automate blade and bucket height on dozers, graders, and excavators against a laser or GNSS reference.

What it costs when it fails

An open-loop blade copies every machine pitch and bump into the finished surface, and a surface left out of grade has to be re-worked — extra passes, material, and time.

How it's solved in production

Grade control senses blade height against a rotating laser plane or a 3D GNSS site model and drives the hydraulics to hold grade to millimeters, letting the operator run faster with fewer passes.

Toy demo → real system

Our controller tracks one clean reference; real 3D control depends on GNSS availability, laser line-of-sight, machine dynamics, and soil that moves and compacts under the blade.
