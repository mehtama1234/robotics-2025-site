The big picture — connecting the dots

Medicine is the hardest place a robot can work. Inside the body, a robot cannot afford trial and error. It cannot move stiff tools through curved anatomy. It cannot ask the surgeon to wait. And it cannot treat every patient the same way. Every part of the sense-decide-move loop has to be rebuilt with one core constraint above all others: safety and gentleness, no exceptions. That constraint ripples through everything else. It reshapes how robots see (reading moving tissue with enough precision to guide a needle to within fractions of a millimeter), how they decide (knowing when to ask for help, when to stop, and when a procedure is too risky for this patient), and how they move (bending like the tissue they touch, growing into curved anatomy, and stopping hard before they hurt something).

Four moves the field is making:

  • ① Track moving tissue in real time so the robot stays on target. When tissue stretches, shifts, or breathes, a robot that does not adapt will miss. Real-Time Deformation-Aware Control for Autonomous Robotic Subretinal Injection Under iOCT Guidance tracks retinal layers as the eye moves and adjusts needle depth live. Advancing Dense Endoscopic Reconstruction with Gaussian Splatting-Driven Surface Normal-Aware Tracking and Mapping and Deformable Gaussian Splatting for Efficient and High-Fidelity Reconstruction of Surgical Scenes build consistent 3D maps of moving tissue by tracking how each point appears, moves, or disappears.
  • ② Build robots whose bodies match anatomy, not the other way around. Straight rigid tools cannot follow curved paths through the body without damaging what surrounds them. Towards Design and Development of a Concentric Tube Steerable Drilling Robot for Creating S-shape Tunnels for Pelvic Fixation Procedures bends with the pelvis. Vine4Spine: A Steerable Tip-Growing Robot with Contact Force Estimation for Navigation in the Spinal Subarachnoid Space grows like a vine and senses when it is pushing too hard. Variable-Stiffness Nasotracheal Intubation Robot with Passive Buffering: A Modular Platform in Mannequin Studies changes stiffness mid-procedure and absorbs shock from unexpected contact.
  • ③ Connect what the robot sees to what it does, without delay. Perception alone is useless — the robot must turn seeing into action fast and accurately. RoboNurse-VLA: Robotic Scrub Nurse System based on Vision-Language-Action Model links language understanding to vision to robotic control so surgeons can request tools naturally. MEDiC: Autonomous Surgical Robotic Assistance to Maximizing Exposure for Dissection and Cautery figures out how to pull tissue to give the surgeon the best view. Ultrasound-Guided Robotic Blood Drawing and In Vivo Studies on Submillimetre Vessels of Rats uses ultrasound to find a vein less than a millimeter wide and guides a needle into it in real time.
  • ④ Train without putting anyone at risk. A medical robot cannot learn by trial and error on patients. Sim4EndoR: A Reinforcement Learning Centered Simulation Platform for Task Automation of Endovascular Robotics lets agents learn to guide wires through simulated arteries. CRESSim-MPM: A Material Point Method Library for Surgical Soft Body Simulation with Cutting and Suturing simulates cutting and stitching tissue accurately enough to train robots on complex deformations. Polyp-Gen: Realistic and Diverse Polyp Image Generation for Endoscopic Dataset Expansion generates synthetic endoscopy images so learning systems get more data without needing more patients.

The throughline: this theme is the field rethinking every single step of robot control for the hardest constraint in robotics — working inside a living, moving, irreplaceable body. That means everything has to be gentler, more adaptive, and more certain than in any other setting. Robots have to bend with anatomy, track tissue in real time, connect perception to action with no lag, and train only on simulations. Still open: most papers solve one piece well — a steerable tube that navigates the spine, a perception system that tracks moving tissue, a simulation for learning — but putting all these pieces together into a full surgical system that is safe, fast, and adaptable to each patient remains the frontier. Surgical robotics today is partly automated on narrow tasks. Making it autonomously capable across the full complexity of a real operating room with all the constraints that brings is the horizon.

The problem — in depth

Every robot faces the same challenge: take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do all of that fast, cheap, safe, and well enough to work in new situations. Medical robots face the harshest version of this challenge, because the "world" they work in is the inside of a living person.

The body is not a fixed space. Tissue stretches, shifts, and breathes. An organ that was in one place a moment ago is somewhere slightly different now. A needle meant to reach a precise layer in the retina of the eye will miss if the tissue moves even a fraction of a millimeter during insertion. That is not a small error — it can blind someone. In most robot settings, the world is relatively stable. In surgery, it never is.

Getting information about the world inside the body is also far harder than it sounds. The main tool is a tiny camera mounted on a thin tube that a surgeon feeds through a small cut (an endoscope). The view is cramped, the lighting is uneven, and the tissue glistens with fluid that causes false reflections. Building a 3D picture of what the camera sees — figuring out how far things are, what is moving, where the tool is — is genuinely difficult. Other ways of seeing inside the body, like X-ray or ultrasound, each have their own gaps and distortions. Before the robot can do anything useful, it has to assemble a good-enough picture of a scene that keeps changing and is hard to look at clearly.

Movement inside the body must be gentle above all else. A robot that presses too hard on the spine or the wall of a blood vessel can cause injury that is worse than doing nothing. Standard robot tools — rigid metal arms driven by powerful motors — are too blunt for this. The anatomy is curved in ways that a straight tool cannot follow: the pelvis curves, the arteries curve, the spinal canal curves. Getting a tool to the right place without damaging what is around it demands a different class of robot body altogether.

The margin for error is also much smaller than in almost any other setting. In a factory, a robot that grips slightly wrong drops a part. In surgery, the same mistake can cost a life. And unlike a factory, every patient is different. The same procedure done on ten people involves ten different anatomies. The robot cannot rely on the world being the same every time.

All of this means that the standard sense-decide-move loop still applies, but every single step of it must be rebuilt with these constraints in mind.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Tracking moving tissue in real time

The sub-problemIn surgery, tissue constantly moves, stretches, and shifts. A robot targeting a precise layer in the eye or following a structure in the abdomen will miss if it does not adapt, and small misses cause injury. The core challenge is keeping a live 3D picture of moving tissue so the robot can track and stay on target.
2D Gaussian Splatting with surface constraints — Advancing Dense Endoscopic Reconstruction with Gaussian Splatting-Driven Surface Normal-Aware Tracking and Mapping. Represents the surgical scene as flat discs in 3D space and forces them to agree on surface direction across frames, building consistent 3D maps from endoscope video.
Deformable point tracking — Deformable Gaussian Splatting for Efficient and High-Fidelity Reconstruction of Surgical Scenes. Tracks the full life span of each 3D point—adding new points where tissue appears, removing them where it disappears—to handle large deformations and tissue changes.
Real-time layer tracking via OCT — Real-Time Deformation-Aware Control for Autonomous Robotic Subretinal Injection Under iOCT Guidance. Continuously segments retinal layers from scan slices at real-time speed, tracks a target point as it moves with the tissue, and adjusts needle depth automatically to stay on target.

Robots can now build and maintain a live 3D model of moving tissue well enough to guide a needle to within fractions of a millimeter, even as the tissue shifts and deforms.

Robots built to bend and grow into the body

The sub-problemAnatomy is curved—pelvis, spine, blood vessels, airway. A straight, rigid tool cannot follow these paths without damaging the tissue around it. The challenge is building robot bodies that adapt to the shape of the patient, not the other way around.
Concentric tube bending — Towards Design and Development of a Concentric Tube Steerable Drilling Robot for Creating S-shape Tunnels for Pelvic Fixation Procedures. Builds a drill that bends in two places to match the curve of the pelvis, letting surgeons place screws along stronger bone paths without forcing straight tools through tissue.
Tip-growing robot with force sensing — Vine4Spine: A Steerable Tip-Growing Robot with Contact Force Estimation for Navigation in the Spinal Subarachnoid Space. Grows like a vine from its tip and has force sensors built in so it knows when it is pushing too hard and can stop before causing injury.
Variable stiffness with passive buffering — Variable-Stiffness Nasotracheal Intubation Robot with Passive Buffering: A Modular Platform in Mannequin Studies. Changes how stiff the tool is during a procedure and includes built-in shock-absorbing sections so that unexpected contact is cushioned rather than forceful.

Robots can now adapt their body shape and stiffness to match anatomy, bending with tissue rather than forcing their way through it.

Simulation and synthetic data to train safely

The sub-problemMedical robots cannot learn by trial and error on patients. Training must happen safely in simulation or with synthetic data, but making simulations realistic enough to transfer to the real world is hard—especially for deformable tissue, cutting, and stitching.
Physics-based vessel simulation — Sim4EndoR: A Reinforcement Learning Centered Simulation Platform for Task Automation of Endovascular Robotics. Builds a physics-based model of blood vessels and uses it to train an agent to guide a wire through arteries, letting the agent learn clinically correct behavior without touching a real patient.
Soft body simulation with cutting and suturing — CRESSim-MPM: A Material Point Method Library for Surgical Soft Body Simulation with Cutting and Suturing. Simulates the actual act of cutting through soft tissue and stitching it back together, tracking each tiny piece of material through large deformations.
Synthetic endoscopy images — Polyp-Gen: Realistic and Diverse Polyp Image Generation for Endoscopic Dataset Expansion. Generates realistic fake endoscopy images with accurate polyp boundaries, giving learning systems more data to train on without needing more patients.

Robots can now train safely on realistic simulations and synthetic data, avoiding the need for trial-and-error learning on real patients.

Connecting perception to robot action

The sub-problemSeeing is only half the problem; the robot must turn what it sees into precise action fast and accurately. Bridging the gap from perception to physical control—understanding surgeon intent, finding the target, and executing the move—requires linking multiple AI models together.
Vision-language-action linking — RoboNurse-VLA: Robotic Scrub Nurse System based on Vision-Language-Action Model. Connects a language model understanding surgeon requests with a vision model finding tools in the image, then drives the robot arm to pick up and hand over the correct instrument.
Tissue manipulation via differentiable simulation — MEDiC: Autonomous Surgical Robotic Assistance to Maximizing Exposure for Dissection and Cautery. Uses a simulation it can compute gradients through to figure out where to grip and how to pull tissue for the best surgical view, mathematically optimizing the pull rather than guessing.
Real-time ultrasound guidance — Ultrasound-Guided Robotic Blood Drawing and In Vivo Studies on Submillimetre Vessels of Rats. Uses ultrasound images in real time to find a vein less than one millimeter wide and guides a needle into it precisely enough to draw blood reliably.

Robots can now close the loop from perception to action, turning what they see into precise, real-time physical responses.

Reading the body from outside the skin

The sub-problemNot all medical robots work by opening the body. Some must control devices already inside from the outside, or help people whose bodies work differently. The challenge is extracting what you need to know from indirect measurements—magnetic fields, muscle bulges, camera views—without adding extra hardware.
Magnetic field shape reconstruction — Intraoperative 3D Shape Estimation of Magnetic Soft Guidewire. Reads the magnetic field produced by a wire's tip and works backward using physics to reconstruct the wire's full 3D curve, needing no extra sensors or imaging hardware.
Muscle-based joint estimation — Force Myography Based Torque Estimation in Human Knee and Ankle Joints. Measures how a muscle bulges through the skin using pressure sensors to estimate how hard a joint is working, information needed to control an exoskeleton.
Camera-driven prosthetic wrist — Continuous Wrist Control on the Hannes Prosthesis. Watches the target object with a camera and automatically rotates a prosthetic wrist to the right angle for the grab, so the user doesn't have to compensate with their shoulder.

Robots can extract precise information about the body's internal state from external signals—magnetic fields, skin bulges, camera views—without adding invasive sensors.

Where it stands

The field has made real progress on the individual pieces: robots that bend into curved anatomy, systems that track moving tissue well enough to guide a needle, simulators that can train agents safely, and tools that connect seeing to acting. What remains hard is putting all of these pieces together reliably in a real operating room, with all the noise, time pressure, and variation across patients that brings. Each paper solves one specific problem well; the gap is a full system that handles many problems at once, adapts to the patient in front of it, and does so with the safety and consistency medicine demands.

Paper by paper — the method

Real-Time Deformation-Aware Control for Autonomous Robotic Subretinal Injection Under iOCT Guidance

What it solves

When a robot needle is inserted into the eye for a subretinal injection, the tissue deforms and moves, so a fixed insertion depth will miss the target layer.

How it works

The robot continuously segments and reconstructs the retinal layers in 3D from dense OCT scan slices (iOCT B-scans) at real time speed, tracks a virtual target between tissue layers as they move, and adjusts needle depth automatically to follow that target.

Why it matters

A deformation-aware autonomous injection controller using dense iOCT 3D reconstruction that raises subretinal bleb generation success from 35% to 90% ex-vivo.

Advancing Dense Endoscopic Reconstruction with Gaussian Splatting-Driven Surface Normal-Aware Tracking and Mapping

Live run · we actually ran it

Reconstructing the inside of a colon from endoscope video

This is a SLAM system built on 2D Gaussian Splatting: as the endoscope moves, it simultaneously figures out where the camera is (tracking) and builds a 3D surface of the tissue out of tiny flat Gaussian discs (mapping) — using surface normals to keep neighboring discs agreeing on which way the tissue faces. We ran the authors' code on a C3VD colonoscopy sequence on an L4 (2D-GS rasterizer compiled from source). Below is the model's own evaluation output at one frame — top row = ground truth, bottom row = the reconstruction.

The rendered RGB (bottom-left, PSNR 23.5) recovers the haustral folds and the dark lumen tunnel going deeper into the colon; the reconstructed depth (bottom-middle) tracks the ground-truth depth closely, with error (bottom-right) concentrated only at the sharp fold edges. Average PSNR over the sequence was 19.4, at ~0.7 s per frame for tracking + mapping.

the recovered 3D surface (normal map — each colour is a surface orientation)

The "surface-normal-aware" part of the method, made visible: the reconstruction isn't just colored points, it's a coherent 3D surface — you can read the curved tunnel walls and the ring-like folds from how the orientation (colour) flows across them.

Why it matters here: a surgeon (or an autonomous tool) needs a live, consistent 3D model of moving tissue to navigate safely. This runs that reconstruction from nothing but the endoscope's own video — no external tracker, no depth sensor.

What it solves

Building an accurate 3D map of the inside of a patient during surgery using a camera is hard because the tissue moves and deforms, causing the depth estimates to be inconsistent between frames.

How it works

The system represents the scene as a collection of flat 2D discs in 3D space (2D Gaussian Splatting) rather than point-like blobs, and constrains them to agree on surface direction across frames, with periodic global refinement of the camera position.

Why it matters

Applies 2D Gaussian Splatting with surface normal constraints to endoscopic SLAM, achieving sub-2mm RMSE depth reconstruction in surgical scenes with real-time rendering.

Polyp-Gen: Realistic and Diverse Polyp Image Generation for Endoscopic Dataset Expansion

What it solves

Generating realistic fake endoscopy images to expand training data is hard because existing tools cannot produce accurate polyp edges without a human drawing where the polyp should go.

How it works

Polyp-Gen uses an image-generation model (diffusion model) trained with a loss that penalizes blurry boundaries, combined with an automatic step that picks plausible polyp locations from the image's own spatial features — no human markup needed.

Why it matters

First fully automatic diffusion-based framework for endoscopic polyp image generation that requires no manual priors, achieving state-of-the-art realism and zero-shot generalization to new datasets.

RoboNurse-VLA: Robotic Scrub Nurse System based on Vision-Language-Action Model

What it solves

A robot surgical assistant needs to pick up and hand over the right surgical tool when the surgeon asks, but tools vary widely in shape and the robot may never have seen the exact one before.

How it works

When the surgeon speaks a tool name, a vision model (SAM 2) finds that tool in the camera image, and a language model (Llama 2) figures out the best grip point and handover angle. These two models are linked so the language reasoning drives the physical action.

Why it matters

A VLA system combining SAM 2 and Llama 2 for surgical scrub nurse robotics that achieves high handover success rates on previously unseen instruments.

SurgPose: a Dataset for Articulated Robotic Surgical Tool Pose Estimation and Tracking

What it solves

Teaching a computer to track the exact position and angle of jointed surgical tools is hard because getting labeled training data requires expensive and tedious geometric calibration of the robot arm.

How it works

They painted marker dots on surgical instruments with UV-reactive paint that is invisible under normal white light, then filmed them under both normal and UV light to get matching labeled pairs. A stereo camera (two cameras side by side) then converts the 2D dot positions into 3D positions.

Why it matters

SurgPose: a 120K-instance stereo dataset with instance-aware UV-keypoint annotations for 6 surgical instrument categories, enabling 3D pose estimation without relying on kinematic calibration.

To Ask or not to Ask: Human-in-the-loop Contextual Bandits with Applications in Robot-Assisted Feeding

What it solves

A robot that helps people eat sometimes meets food it cannot handle on its own, but asking the user for help too often is tiring—especially for people who cannot move their arms.

How it works

The robot uses a decision-making method (contextual bandit called LINUCB-QG) that learns when asking is worth the cost by predicting how much effort a question will place on a particular user before deciding to ask.

Why it matters

LINUCB-QG framework that adapts querying frequency to individual user workload in robot-assisted feeding, validated in a user study including participants with severe mobility limitations.

Variable-Stiffness Nasotracheal Intubation Robot with Passive Buffering: A Modular Platform in Mannequin Studies

What it solves

Inserting a breathing tube through the nose into the airway requires bending through tight curves with carefully limited force — something stiff, fixed-shape robotic tools struggle to do safely.

How it works

The team built a robot whose stiffness can be changed during the procedure and that has built-in shock-absorbing (passive buffering) sections to soften any unexpected contact, tested on a mannequin head.

Why it matters

Presents a modular robotic platform with variable stiffness and passive buffering specifically designed and validated for nasotracheal intubation procedures.

Deformable Gaussian Splatting for Efficient and High-Fidelity Reconstruction of Surgical Scenes

What it solves

Reconstructing video of soft tissue during surgery in 3D is hard when the tissue stretches or tears in ways that cannot be undone, and existing methods are either slow or produce blurry results.

How it works

EH-SurGS tracks the full life span of each 3D point in the scene, adding new points where tissue appears and removing them where it disappears, and it separates still parts of the scene from moving parts to keep the moving-part model small.

Why it matters

A Gaussian splatting method with life-cycle-aware deformation modeling and adaptive motion hierarchy that simultaneously improves reconstruction quality and rendering speed for surgical scenes.

Intraoperative 3D Shape Estimation of Magnetic Soft Guidewire

What it solves

Doctors cannot see the full 3D curve of a flexible magnetic-tipped wire inside a blood vessel during a procedure without adding sensors to the wire or extra imaging hardware.

How it works

A physics-based model links the shape of the wire's magnetic tip to the magnetic field it produces in the surrounding space; the system reads that field and works backward to reconstruct the wire's 3D curve, using physical rules to keep the answer realistic.

Why it matters

Sensor-free, fluoroscopy-compatible 3D guidewire shape reconstruction using magnetic field measurements with below-1mm accuracy, applicable to standard clinical workflows.

Robust and Accurate Multi-View 2D/3D Image Registration with Differentiable X-Ray Rendering and Dual Cross-View Constraints

What it solves

During surgery, aligning a pre-op 3D scan of a patient's anatomy to live X-ray images from multiple angles is difficult and error-prone.

How it works

A two-step process first trains a model to match poses by comparing simulated X-rays (rendered directly from the 3D scan) to real X-rays, penalizing disagreements across views. Then it refines the answer further at test time by minimizing those same cross-view errors.

Why it matters

Dual cross-view training losses for both pose and image similarity that explicitly enforce geometric consistency across views, achieving mTRE of 0.79±2.17 mm on the DeepFluoro dataset.
