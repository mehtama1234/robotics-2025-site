Farm robots — the real problem

A field robot has to cover every acre, stay in the lane, tell the crop from the weed, and hit a moving target with expensive chemical — each stage is a separate hard problem.

← the agricultural robot demo · the build lab · home
Each stage below pairs our measured demo with the real practical problem behind it: who actually hits it (the industry or system), what it costs when it fails (real failure modes and incidents), how it is solved in production today, and the honest gap between our clean sim and the real thing. The demo captures the essence; this is the world it lives in.
1

Cover the whole field — sweep every acre without gaps or overlap

Our demo: a systematic boustrophedon sweep covers all 400 cells (100%), while a naive path leaves gaps and re-covers ground, reaching only 28.5%.

Who actually hits it

Autonomous platforms plan full-field coverage before they start: Naio's Dino and Oz weeding robots let the farmer set the plot layout and then work rows and turn at the headland on their own, and Carbon Robotics' Autonomous LaserWeeder uses GPS-plus-vision guidance to stay in bounds, navigate furrows, and turn around for the next row.

What it costs when it fails

Gaps mean weeds survive or seed goes unplanted in the missed strip; overlap means the robot double-covers ground, wasting time, fuel or battery, and inputs. Poor coverage planning directly shrinks the hectares-per-day a machine can bill for.

How it's solved in production

Coverage-path planning lays the field out as parallel swaths driven in an ox-plow (boustrophedon) pattern, with the headland handled by automated end-of-row turns; RTK auto-steer keeps pass-to-pass spacing tight so swaths abut without gaps or overlap.

Toy demo → real system

Our sim is a clean rectangular grid; real fields have irregular boundaries, obstacles, terraces, waterways, and point rows that force decomposition of the field into sub-regions and much harder turn and re-entry planning.

2

Drive the crop rows — stay centered in the lane, not on the plants

Our demo: with 0.75 m row spacing, a GPS bias of 0.302 m runs the robot onto the crop, while vision-based row centering holds lateral error to 0.013 m.

Who actually hits it

Naio's Dino is guided by RTK-GPS plus a set of cameras to stay between the rows; Carbon Robotics combines GPS with real-time camera guidance; and RTK auto-steer tractors follow pre-recorded AB lines for planting and spraying.

What it costs when it fails

If the machine drifts more than half a row, it crushes seedlings or shears the crop it is meant to protect. GPS-only guidance can't fix drift when rows weren't planted under correction, and drift varies with soil, payload, and satellite geometry.

How it's solved in production

RTK-GNSS corrections pull GNSS from meters down to roughly 2 to 3 cm pass-to-pass; on top of that, machine-vision row detection segments the visible crop rows and steers to the lane center, so guidance references the plants actually in front of the wheels rather than a possibly-biased map.

Toy demo → real system

Our vision model is idealized; real row-following fights variable lighting, leaf occlusion of the ground, crop residue, weeds masquerading as rows, and changing appearance as the crop grows and closes canopy.

3

Tell crop from weed — separate the plant to keep from the plant to kill

Our demo: classifying on color alone misclassifies 45.5% of plants (crop and weed are both green), but adding leaf shape and a two-feature boundary drops the error to 2.2%.

Who actually hits it

This is the core of John Deere's See & Spray (built on Blue River Technology, which Deere acquired in 2017 for $305M), which uses computer vision to recognize plants and spray only the weeds, and of Carbon Robotics' LaserWeeder, whose deep-learning perception model discriminates crop, beneficial, and weed plants in milliseconds while rolling.

What it costs when it fails

A false negative leaves a weed to compete for water, light, and nutrients; a false positive sprays or lasers the crop itself. Both errors cost yield, and mistargeting expensive herbicide or laser time is money and damage.

How it's solved in production

Modern systems use deep convolutional classifiers and detectors trained on large labeled crop-and-weed image sets running on onboard GPUs, going well beyond simple color thresholds to leaf shape, texture, and context to separate look-alike green plants.

Toy demo → real system

Our classifier uses two hand-picked features on clean plants; production models must generalize to novel weed species, growth stages, overlapping and occluded leaves, dust, and mud, and still decide in real time at working speed.

4

Hit the moving weed — fire the nozzle so the spray lands on target

Our demo: at 1.5 m/s with 0.2 s actuation latency the machine travels 0.3 m before the spray fires; firing on detection lands every shot behind the 0.05 m weed (0% hits), while triggering early by speed times latency lands all of them (100%).

Who actually hits it

See & Spray fires individual nozzles as the sprayer moves across the field, likened to an inkjet printer hitting each weed; Carbon Robotics' LaserWeeder aims laser shots at each weed's meristem while the machine keeps rolling.

What it costs when it fails

If the trigger ignores the delay between detection and actuation, the shot lands behind the weed, so the weed survives and the chemical or laser energy is wasted on bare soil. At higher speeds this error grows, forcing the machine to slow down and cover fewer acres.

How it's solved in production

Systems compensate for latency by predicting where the weed will be when the actuator fires, offsetting the trigger by ground speed times the total detection-to-spray delay, so the droplet or laser pulse meets the target at the right instant.

Toy demo → real system

Our timing model assumes constant speed, known latency, and a still target on flat ground; real rigs jolt over rough, wet fields where speed, boom height, and nozzle timing all vary, and wind can push the droplets off the moving target.

5

Dose to the need — apply just enough input where the field actually needs it

Our demo: a uniform dose sized for the worst cell totals 4441 units across 900 cells, while a variable rate matched to a need map totals 894 units — a 79.9% reduction in input used.

Who actually hits it

See & Spray targets herbicide only onto detected weeds instead of blanket-spraying, and Deere reported it saved farmers an estimated 8 million gallons of herbicide in 2024 at an average of roughly 59% less herbicide; variable-rate application maps are standard practice on RTK-guided sprayers and spreaders.

What it costs when it fails

A single uniform rate must be big enough for the worst spot, so it over-applies almost everywhere: wasted, costly chemical or fertilizer, plus runoff and environmental load. Under-dosing the tough patches to save money instead lets weeds or deficiency win there.

How it's solved in production

Variable-rate technology drives application from a georeferenced prescription (need) map — from weed detection or soil and yield data — and modulates rate section-by-section or nozzle-by-nozzle in real time as the machine moves.

Toy demo → real system

Our need map is exact and static; real prescription maps carry sensing error and go stale, and the sprayer's response, drift, and section-control resolution limit how precisely the commanded rate actually reaches each spot in the field.
