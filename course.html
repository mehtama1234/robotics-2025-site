Robotics course spine

Robotics From First Principles

A plain-language course spine for reading ICRA, IROS, CoRL, and RSS as one subject.

1. Start2. Body3. Space4. Seeing5. Memory6. Choice7. Motion8. Contact9. Learning10. Practice11. People12. Fields

A robot is a machine that must make choices in the physical world. It has a body, a limited view, limited power, moving parts that can slip or break, and people or objects around it that may move in ways it did not expect.

The whole field can be read from one question: how does the machine use incomplete information to take a useful action without causing harm? Every conference paper is one answer to part of that question.

This course page is meant to be read slowly. It avoids shorthand. When a technical word is needed, it says what the word means in everyday terms and why it matters.

Start

1. The Whole Subject In One Loop

A robot repeats one loop: sense, understand where things are, choose, move, and learn from what happened.

The first job is sensing. A camera, lidar, microphone, joint encoder, force sensor, or soft fingertip gives the robot numbers. Those numbers are not yet knowledge. They are raw readings, often noisy, late, partial, or blocked by the robot's own hand.

The second job is keeping track of the situation. The robot needs a useful estimate of itself and the world: where its joints are, where the object is, which space is open, which space is blocked, and what may happen next.

The third job is choosing an action. The action may be a steering command, a grasp, a walking step, a tool motion, a camera move, or a request for help. The choice is only useful if the robot can actually perform it with its body.

The fourth job is checking the result. The world pushes back. A wheel slips, a cup moves, a person steps into the path, a soft object bends. The robot senses again and updates its choice. This repeated loop is the center of robotics.

Where this shows up

  • Warehouse robots use the loop to pick items, avoid people, and recover when a box is not where the system expected.
  • Surgical robots use the loop to steady small motions and keep tools inside safe areas.
  • Farm robots use the loop to see plants under changing light, move through soil, and handle fruit without bruising it.
Body

2. A Robot Is Not Just Software

The body sets what can be done: reach, speed, force, weight, battery, heat, wear, and safety.

Software can ask for a motion, but the body decides whether that motion is possible. A small arm cannot lift a heavy box. A wheeled robot cannot climb stairs without the right shape. A soft gripper can wrap around an object, but it may be hard to know exactly where its surface is.

Every joint has limits. Every motor has a highest force and a highest speed. Every battery runs out. Every sensor has blind spots. Good robotics papers respect these facts instead of pretending the machine is an abstract point on a screen.

This is why hardware, control, and learning cannot be separated. A better hand changes the problem. A better sensor changes the plan. A faster motor changes what can be recovered after a slip.

Where this shows up

  • Manufacturing robots need bodies that repeat motions for years without drifting out of alignment.
  • Assistive robots need bodies that are strong enough to help but soft and predictable around people.
  • Space robots need bodies that survive dust, cold, radiation, and long delays from Earth.
Space

3. Topology Means What Is Connected, Blocked, Or Looped

Topology is the study of the shape of possible motion: which places connect, which paths wrap around, and which barriers split the world.

In everyday words, topology asks questions like these: can the robot get from here to there without crossing a wall? Does a cable wrap around a pole? Is a hand trapped inside a handle? Does a room have one doorway or two? These questions matter even before exact distances matter.

A map is not just a picture. It tells the robot which free spaces touch each other. A narrow doorway can connect two rooms. A closed door can split one reachable world into two separate pieces. A looped hallway gives two ways around; a dead end gives one way in and one way out.

Hands and tools have topology too. A ring around a peg cannot simply pass through the peg. A hook can catch on an edge. A cloth can fold over itself. A knot, loop, or wrap changes what actions are possible.

Many failures in robotics are topology failures. The robot planned a short path that crosses a barrier. It tried to pull a cable straight through a loop. It treated a cup handle as empty space. It thought an object could slide away when contact had actually trapped it.

Where this shows up

  • Navigation uses topology to know whether two places are connected by a safe route.
  • Manipulation uses topology to reason about handles, hooks, rings, cables, knots, and containers.
  • Surgery uses topology to keep tools inside allowed openings and avoid crossing tissue boundaries.
  • Multi-robot planning uses topology to decide whether several robots can pass through a corridor without blocking each other.
Seeing

4. Perception Turns Sensor Readings Into A Usable World

Perception is the work of turning pixels, points, sound, and touch into objects, surfaces, motion, and meaning.

A camera gives color at each pixel. That does not directly say how far away something is, what object it is, whether it is moving, or whether it can be touched. Perception is the work of turning readings into facts the rest of the robot can use.

Three-dimensional perception asks where surfaces are in space. Tracking asks what moved and where it went. Recognition asks what kind of thing the robot is seeing. Open naming asks whether the robot can connect a human word, such as 'mug' or 'door handle', to the right part of the scene.

Touch is part of perception. When a finger covers the object, the camera may lose the exact place where contact happens. A tactile sensor can report pressure, slip, edge shape, or surface texture at the point where the decision matters most.

Where this shows up

  • Autonomous vehicles use perception to find lanes, cars, people, signs, and free space.
  • Medical robots use perception to reconstruct tissue shape from limited camera views.
  • Inspection robots use perception to find cracks, corrosion, loose parts, and surface changes.
Memory

5. The Robot Needs A Working Picture Of The World

The machine needs memory because the world is only partly visible at any one moment.

A robot rarely sees everything it needs. An object can pass behind a shelf. A person can leave the camera view. A map can be incomplete. The robot therefore keeps a working picture that combines old readings with new ones.

This working picture may be a map, a list of objects, a set of likely positions, or a learned prediction of what may happen next. The exact form changes by paper, but the reason is the same: action needs more context than the current sensor frame provides.

A good working picture is not only accurate. It also says what is uncertain. If the robot is not sure whether a person is behind a corner, that uncertainty should change the speed and path it chooses.

Where this shows up

  • Home robots need memory to find objects after they leave view.
  • Drones need memory to fly through smoke, trees, or clutter where sensors lose track.
  • Search-and-rescue robots need memory to avoid revisiting the same area and to mark places that remain unchecked.
Choice

6. Planning Is Choosing A Path Through Consequences

Planning asks what action now will make later actions easier, safer, or possible at all.

The best immediate move is not always the best move. A robot may need to back up before turning, move an object before reaching another one, open a door before entering a room, or slow down now to avoid a hard stop later.

Planning searches through possible futures. Some plans are paths through space. Some are task steps, such as open, pick, place, tighten, clean, or inspect. Some plans are contact sequences, such as push, slide, pinch, lift.

The hard part is that the number of possible futures grows quickly. Robotics papers deal with this by using maps, learned hints, short repeated plans, human demonstrations, or rules that throw away clearly bad choices early.

Where this shows up

  • Warehouses use planning to route many robots without jams.
  • Construction robots use planning to choose build order and tool motion.
  • Underwater robots use planning because they cannot rely on constant communication or perfect positioning.
Motion

7. Control Turns A Plan Into Real Movement

Control is the repeated correction that keeps the body close to the intended motion.

A plan says what should happen. Control deals with what is happening. If the robot arm is behind the desired path, control increases effort. If a leg slips, control changes the next force. If the body tilts, control acts before the fall grows.

The simple idea is feedback: measure the gap between wanted and actual motion, then push in the direction that shrinks the gap. This sounds small, but it is the reason robots can balance, steer, grasp, and recover from disturbances.

Control must run on the clock of the body. A controller that chooses a beautiful motion too late is not useful. Many papers are about making better choices under strict time limits.

Where this shows up

  • Legged robots use control to keep balance while the ground changes.
  • Robot arms use control to follow paths while carrying weight.
  • Self-driving systems use control to turn a planned route into steering, braking, and acceleration.
Contact

8. Touching The World Changes The Rules

When contact happens, forces matter and the future can change suddenly.

Moving through empty space is easier than touching something. At contact, the object may stick, slip, roll, bend, break, or tip. A tiny change in finger position can change the whole result.

Grasping is not just closing a hand. The robot must choose where to touch, how hard to press, which direction to pull, and how to notice slip. If the object is soft, transparent, heavy, wet, or oddly shaped, the problem changes again.

Contact is also useful. A robot can use a table edge to align an object, push instead of pick, slide a finger along a surface, or use a tool to reach where the hand cannot.

Where this shows up

  • Factories use contact reasoning for assembly, insertion, polishing, and fastening.
  • Kitchens need contact reasoning for stirring, pouring, cutting, wiping, and opening containers.
  • Medical robots need careful force control because small contact mistakes can matter.
Learning

9. Learning Lets Robots Reuse Experience

Learning is how the robot turns examples, trials, video, or human teaching into better future behavior.

Some tasks are too varied to program one rule at a time. Learning gives the robot a way to improve from data. The data can be a human demonstration, a video, a simulator trial, a previous failure, or a reward signal that says whether the result was good.

Learning is not magic. It only learns from the examples and signals it receives. If the examples miss important cases, the robot may fail in those cases. If the reward measures the wrong thing, the robot may learn the wrong behavior.

The value of learning is reuse. One set of experiences can help the robot recognize objects, predict motion, choose grasps, walk over new ground, or understand instructions in a way that hand-written rules would not cover.

Where this shows up

  • CoRL papers often focus on learning from demonstrations, trial data, and contact-rich practice.
  • Service robots use learning to adapt to homes, people, and object layouts that differ every day.
  • Industrial robots use learning to reduce setup time when a product or fixture changes.
Practice

10. Simulation Helps, But The Real World Has The Final Vote

Simulation is cheap practice; the real machine reveals what the simulation missed.

Real robot data is expensive. Machines wear out, people must supervise, and mistakes can damage objects or hurt someone. Simulation gives the robot many practice attempts without paying the full physical cost.

The problem is that simulation is always a simplification. Friction, lighting, soft materials, sensor noise, cable drag, backlash, and small manufacturing differences can change the result. A behavior that works in a simulated world may fail on the real robot.

A large part of modern robotics is closing this gap. Papers change the simulator, randomize conditions, collect small amounts of real data, use real videos, or train the robot to notice when its practice world no longer matches reality.

Where this shows up

  • Humanoid robots use simulation to practice falls and recoveries that would be costly on hardware.
  • Driving systems use simulation to test rare road events before they happen in public.
  • Manipulation systems use simulation to generate many object poses before trying a smaller set on a real arm.
People

11. Language, Trust, And Safety Are Part Of The Machine

A useful robot must understand goals, explain limits, and act within clear safety boundaries.

Language matters because people do not want to command every motor. They want to say what outcome they need: bring the cup, clean the spill, inspect the valve, hold the camera steady. The robot must connect those words to objects, places, actions, and limits.

Trust matters because a wrong action in the physical world has cost. The robot should know when it is unsure, slow down near people, refuse unsafe requests, and hand control back when the situation leaves its training or sensors behind.

Safety is not a final polish step. It changes the whole design. The robot may need a slower path, an allowed area, a force limit, a backup controller, a monitor, or a rule that stops an action before the danger becomes visible.

Where this shows up

  • Hospitals need robots that can explain uncertainty and respect strict boundaries.
  • Public delivery robots need predictable motion around pedestrians.
  • Home robots need simple instructions, safe defaults, and clear failure behavior.
Fields

12. Why Robotics Matters Outside Robotics

Robotics connects computer vision, search, language, hardware, medicine, work, and public safety because it is where information becomes action.

Computer vision asks what is in the scene. Robotics asks what can be done in that scene. Search and information retrieval ask how to find the right knowledge. Robotics asks how that knowledge changes a physical decision. Hardware asks what can run fast and cheaply. Robotics asks whether it can run on a body with power, heat, and weight limits.

Topology connects to many fields because it studies structure that remains even when measurements change. In biology, it can describe folded shapes and connected surfaces. In city planning, it can describe neighborhoods, roads, and barriers. In networking, it can describe which nodes remain connected when links fail. In robotics, it describes the possible motion of bodies through space and contact.

The importance of robotics is not that every machine will look human. It is that more work is becoming work done by systems that sense, decide, and act. Understanding robotics gives a simple way to read many fields at once: what does the system know, what can it do, what blocks it, what could go wrong, and how does it recover?

Where this shows up

  • Medicine gains better tools for imaging, surgery, rehabilitation, and lab automation.
  • Disaster response gains machines that can enter places too dangerous for people.
  • Transportation gains systems that must connect perception, planning, control, safety, and human rules.
  • Science gains instruments that can explore oceans, farms, space, mines, and remote habitats.
