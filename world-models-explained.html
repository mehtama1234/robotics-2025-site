01 · the idea

What a world model is: a learned “what happens next”

Most learned agents are a reflex: show them the situation, they output a move. That works, but it is blind — the agent cannot ask ‘what would happen if I did this instead?’ before committing.

A world model adds exactly that missing piece. It is a learned answer to one question: given where things are now and what I do, what happens next? Once a system can predict consequences, everything opens up — it can look ahead, weigh options, and even practise entirely inside its own imagination.

That is the whole premise of this page. A world model is a learned simulator of reality, built from data instead of hand-written physics, and the four sections that follow are just how you build one and what you do with it.

the actual math — one level deeper

A world model learns the environment’s dynamics (and reward): p(sₜ₊₁ | sₜ, aₜ) and r̂(sₜ, aₜ).

A model-free agent learns only a policy π(a|s) by trial and error. A model-based agent learns the dynamics too, then plans or learns against it.

Given the model you can evaluate a plan without acting: expected return J = E[ Σₜ γᵗ r̂(sₜ, aₜ) ] rolled out through p̂.

02 · the state

Compress the world to a small state

You could try to predict the future frame-by-frame in raw pixels, but pixels are a terrible place to think. They are huge, and two completely different situations can look nearly identical while a tiny, invisible difference (which way the ball is spinning) decides what happens next.

So the world model first encodes each observation into a small latent state — a handful of numbers meant to capture the true situation, throwing away lighting, texture, and pixel noise. Prediction then happens in this compact space: cheaper, and forced to be about what actually drives the dynamics rather than surface appearance.

This is the single most important design choice in the field, and where the two schools later split: predict in a tidy latent state, or bite the bullet and predict the pixels themselves.

the actual math — one level deeper

Encode observation oₜ to a latent state: zₜ ~ q(zₜ | oₜ) (an encoder / recognition model).

Predict in latent space: p(zₜ₊₁ | zₜ, aₜ). Optionally decode back to check: ôₜ = d(zₜ).

The Dreamer-style RSSM splits the state into a deterministic path hₜ = f(hₜ₋₁, zₜ₋₁, aₜ₋₁) and a stochastic zₜ ~ p(zₜ | hₜ) — memory plus uncertainty.

03 · the rollout

Imagine the future by rolling it forward

Once you can predict one step, you can predict many: feed the predicted next state back in, apply the next action, and roll forward. A few dozen steps of this and you have an entire imagined trajectory — a guess at how the whole episode would play out.

Try a different action sequence and you get a different future. The world model lets the agent branch out and explore dozens of these hypothetical rollouts in parallel, in milliseconds, without taking a single real step — no broken dishes, no crashed drones, no wasted hours.

The catch is that small prediction errors compound over a long rollout, so much of the craft is keeping imagined futures accurate far enough ahead to be useful.

the actual math — one level deeper

Roll the dynamics forward H steps from zₜ: ẑₜ₊₁, ẑₜ₊₂, …, ẑₜ₊ₕ by iterating p(z₊₁ | z, a) under a chosen action sequence.

Prediction error compounds roughly geometrically with H, so accuracy far ahead is the hard part.

Branch over action sequences to get many candidate futures in parallel — the search space for planning.

04 · planning

Use the dream: plan, or learn inside it

Now cash in the imagination. The first way is planning: roll out many candidate action sequences, score each by the reward the model predicts it earns, and execute the first step of the best one — then replan next tick. This is model-predictive control with a learned model.

The second way is learning in imagination (the Dreamer idea): treat the imagined rollouts as free training data and update an ordinary policy on them. The agent gets millions of practice episodes for the cost of GPU time, and only occasionally touches the real world to keep the model honest.

Either way, the payoff is the same: foresight and practice become cheap, which is exactly what real-world robotics is starved for.

the actual math — one level deeper

Planning (MPC): a⃗* = argmax over action sequences of Σ γᵗ r̂(ẑₖ); solved by sampling (CEM / MPPI), execute step 1, replan.

Learning in imagination (Dreamer): train an actor-critic on imagined rollouts, backpropagating value gradients through the differentiable dynamics.

Real interaction is used only to keep the model calibrated, so sample efficiency can improve by 10–100× over model-free RL.

05 · pixels vs latent

Two kinds of world model

Everything above leaves one fork open: what exactly does the model predict? Two answers, two research cultures.

Video world models predict the next frame. They are the video-generation lineage (diffusion transformers), and their gift is that the imagined future is a watchable, photoreal video — perfect for generating driving scenarios or training data, at the cost of being heavy and hard to run in a fast control loop. Latent-dynamics models predict the next compact state. You cannot watch the dream, but it is cheap enough to roll hundreds of steps ahead, which is what closed-loop control needs.

The most-quoted insight of the moment is that these are converging: the best video generators are turning out to be implicit world models, and the best world models increasingly learn to generate video as a side effect of understanding dynamics.

the actual math — one level deeper

Video world model: p(oₜ₊₁ | o≤ₜ, aₜ) — predict pixels directly, usually a diffusion transformer. Photoreal, heavy, watchable.

Latent dynamics: p(zₜ₊₁ | zₜ, aₜ) with a small z — predict state. Cheap to roll far ahead; not directly viewable.

Convergence: video models are implicit world models; latent models often add a decoder to generate video as a check.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every family below is chasing one or more of these — the recurring reasons robotics and vision reach for a world model, in the words the papers keep using.

Learn in imagination

Once the model exists, the agent practises on imagined rollouts for the price of GPU time. Papers cite cutting real-robot data from 5 hours to 1.5, or learning a gait from 3 minutes of real data.

Foresight by lookahead

Roll out candidate actions and pick the best before committing — model-predictive control with a learned model, even over ‘infinite’ horizons for legged robots.

Safety without real trials

Rehearse dangerous situations in the dream. Safety filters ‘play out’ hypothetical adversaries; anomaly detectors flag when the real world diverges from what the model expected.

Generalisation & transfer

A model of dynamics transfers where a memorised policy won’t — across objects, embodiments, and sim→real, because it learned how the world works, not one reflex.

One model, many jobs

The same learned model serves planning, policy training, data generation, and anomaly detection — and, when it predicts pixels, doubles as a photoreal simulator.

01

Latent-dynamics model-based RL (Dreamer-style)

robotics
the problem

Reinforcement learning is brutally data-hungry — a robot might need millions of trials to learn a skill, and every trial happens on real, breakable hardware in real, slow time. You simply cannot afford that many real attempts.

the approaches

The purest form: encode pixels to a latent state, learn the dynamics in that space, and train the whole policy inside imagined rollouts — touching the real robot only to keep the model honest. The prize is sample efficiency, which is what real hardware is starved for.

The 2024–25 work pushes it onto real bodies and hard settings: legged locomotion, sparse rewards from pixels, and estimating hidden physics (mass, friction) so the dream matches reality.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Act in the real world briefly and record what the robot saw and did.
  2. Train the world model on that log: predict the next latent state from the current state and action.
  3. Now stop touching reality. From a remembered state, imagine thousands of rollouts under the current policy — all on the GPU.
  4. Improve the policy on those imagined rollouts, nudging it toward higher predicted reward.
  5. Return to the real robot only occasionally for fresh data to keep the model honest. Most of the learning happened in the dream.
  • ROBO
    Privileged-Dreamer — a dual world model explicitly imagines hidden parameters (mass, friction) to adapt fast in sim-to-real

    Real robots have properties (mass, friction, motor compliance) that simulators guess wrong. This paper extends Dreamer with privileged state: the world model explicitly imagines hidden physics parameters (inferred from real data) and uses those to adapt its dynamics predictions, bridging simulation and reality without changing the policy.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Fit a physics simulator to real robot data and extract inferred parameters (mass, friction, spring constant). (2) Extend the world model to include these as latent state variables, predicted alongside position/velocity. (3) During imagination, the model predicts both the standard state and the hidden parameters as they evolve. (4) A policy trained on these 'privileged' rollouts learns to be reliable to parameter variation. (5) On the real robot, the learned policy works because its training used realistic parameters.

    Why it works: Explicitly imagining hidden physics lets the model capture the real robot's behavior without explicit sim-to-real transfer. The policy learns on rollouts that already account for what makes reality different from the simulator.

  • ROBO
    PIP-Loco — a Dreamer module solves infinite-horizon MPC for quadruped locomotion over rough terrain

    Legged robots walking on rough terrain face infinite-horizon problems: one misstep cascades into a fall. Standard model-predictive control re-plans every tick over a short horizon, but a quadruped needs to see farther ahead—past the next footstep—to commit safely. This paper integrates a learned world model into infinite-horizon MPC, letting the planner look as far as the model stays accurate.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encode proprioception and terrain height maps into a latent state; train a Dreamer-style RNN to predict the next state given action and hidden stochastic variables. At control time, sample thousands of candidate action sequences (gaits, step timings, foot placements) and roll each through the dynamics. Score each by the predicted reward (reach goal, no fall). Solve the infinite-horizon cost via dynamic-programming value-iteration in imagination.

    Why it works: The learned model captures terrain-dependent stability that hand-tuned physics engines miss. By planning over true dynamics instead of a simplified model, the planner commits to longer horizons and recovers from partial missteps—cutting replanning overhead and enabling smooth, energy-efficient gaits on unmapped terrain.

  • ROBO
    SR-AIF — active inference + a world model cracks sparse-reward continuous control straight from pixels

    Sparse-reward continuous control is hard: the robot gets reward only on success, and random exploration takes forever to stumble onto it. Most learning algorithms collapse. This paper marries active inference—choosing actions to minimize uncertainty—with a learned world model. The robot doesn't just learn what to do; it actively explores what it's unsure about, using the dream to probe hypothetical futures and navigate toward high-information regions.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Maintain a learned generative world model (encoder, dynamics, decoder) and a distribution over latent state. At each step, compute the mutual information between candidate actions and reducing state uncertainty (active inference). Prefer actions that would most tighten the belief. Roll those actions through the model; sample the stochastic component; use the predicted observation to tighten belief. With high certainty, greedily maximize predicted reward.

    Why it works: Active inference turns uncertainty into signal instead of noise. Sparse-reward environments are informationally starved—the robot must learn that touching the goal is reward. By prioritizing high-information actions (e.g., "is the goal here or there?"), the agent builds accurate models much faster than random exploration, and sparse rewards guide the learned model to reward-bearing regions.

  • ROBO
    LUMOS — a language-conditioned latent world model learns visuomotor policies offline, then transfers zero-shot to a real robot

    Vision-language models can understand "pick up the red cube" from pixels, but they are trained on diverse internet data—not your robot's embodiment or gripper. This paper decouples perception from action: the language model reasons abstractly ("the goal is to move red near blue"), while a latent world model handles the robot's actual dynamics and morphology. Language priors guide the learning, but physics is learned from data.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encode observations into a latent state space with learned dynamics. Use a frozen vision-language model to extract goal predicates from the language instruction. In a latent-space MPC loop, optimize for configurations predicted to satisfy those predicates, subject to the learned dynamics. Pretrain on offline demonstrations, then fine-tune on real interaction—minimally, because language priors compress the learning problem.

    Why it works: Language provides high-level task structure (goals, relationships) without entangling it to any embodiment. The learned world model captures the specific robot's dynamics and morphology offline, then policy search (in imagination) aligns language goals to real actions. This separation allows zero-shot transfer: the same language objective and world model work on different robots.

  • ROBO
    Mastering the Labyrinth — selective reconstruction of just the physical state cuts real data from 5 hours to 1.5

    Standard world models reconstruct everything: every detail in the scene, every light bounce, every texture. But for control, most of that is noise. This paper asks: reconstruct only what moves. Mask away static walls, only model the agent's position and velocity (the part that matters for planning). The result: a model that needs only 1.5 hours of real robot data instead of 5.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Learn a scene decomposition: static background and dynamic foreground (the agent). Train the encoder to produce a latent state that recovers only the dynamic part on decoding; skip the walls. Dynamics predict the next position and velocity from action. During imagination, roll the agent forward without reconstructing irrelevant detail. Use the learned latent model for planning, and only periodically touch the real robot to refine the decomposition.

    Why it works: By ignoring static scene details, the model has fewer degrees of freedom to fit, so its gradient signals are stronger and its sample efficiency rockets. The reconstruction bottleneck is broken: instead of modeling photorealism, the model focuses on the one thing that changes, making real-world learning practical for long-horizon tasks.

02

Planning with learned dynamics (MPC)

robotics
the problem

You have a decent model of how things will unfold, but the situation keeps changing under you. You need to decide what to do right now, act, and then reconsider the instant the world moves — not commit to a long plan blindly and hope.

the approaches

Instead of training a policy, keep the model and search: at every tick, roll many action sequences through the learned dynamics, score them, execute the best first step, and replan. This is model-predictive control with a neural (or physics-plus-residual) model.

The recurring worry is the model wandering outside what it was trained on, so several papers bolt on novelty or uncertainty checks that veto risky imagined actions.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. At this instant, sample ~1,000 candidate action sequences for the next H steps.
  2. Roll each one through the learned model and add up the reward it predicts.
  3. Keep the best sequence; execute only its first action.
  4. One tick later, discard the rest and replan from the new state (receding horizon).
  5. If a novelty check says the model is guessing outside its training data, fall back to a cautious action.
  • ROBO
    Planning with Adaptive World Models — predicts agent-specific motion models, then rolls them out with MPC for driving

    Every agent moves differently: a car has momentum, a drone is nimble, a robot arm is ponderous. A world model trained on one agent's behavior won't predict another's. This paper learns agent-specific motion models on the fly, allowing a single world-model architecture to adapt its predictions to whichever embodiment is doing the acting right now.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encode ego motion (the agent's trajectory) and the scene layout separately. Train a dynamics model conditioned on both the latent state and an agent descriptor (e.g., a learned embedding or observed velocity profile). During planning, roll out action sequences using the appropriate agent descriptor. For a new agent, collect a few seconds of data (or infer descriptors from initial actions) and adapt the model on the fly.

    Why it works: Agent descriptors capture the embodiment's mass, actuators, and control authority without explicit specification. By factoring dynamics = scene + agent-specific kinematics, the model generalizes across embodiments. Planning with MPC then respects each agent's true dynamics instead of forcing a one-size-fits-all model.

  • ROBO
    Bounding Distributional Shifts through Novelty Detection — a VAE flags out-of-distribution actions before the MPC trusts the model on them

    A learned world model is dangerous when asked to predict far outside its training data. The robot could confidently hallucinate an action that works in the dream but fails catastrophically in reality. This paper bolts a VAE-based unfamiliarity detector onto the MPC loop, flagging out-of-distribution state-action pairs and vetoing risky imagined actions before they're executed.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Train a VAE on state-action pairs from the training dataset. During planning, for each candidate action sequence, roll it through the dynamics and compute the VAE reconstruction loss at each imagined step. High loss = anomalous. If the sequence ventures into high-anomaly regions, downweight or discard it. Keep only sequences that the model was trained to understand.

    Why it works: Unfamiliarity detection grounds planning in reality by refusing to commit to predictions the model was never trained on. The VAE reconstruction loss is a principled OOD signal. MPC becomes conservative exactly where it should: when the dream encounters configurations unlike the training set, it retreats to safer, more-practiced behaviors.

  • ROBO
    Online Residual Model Learning — learns a residual on top of physics so MPC adapts online to changing dynamics

    Physics changes during deployment: a robot's joints wear, the floor gets wet, wind picks up. A world model frozen at training time becomes stale. This paper keeps the base model static but learns a learnable residual on top—a small network that corrects for drift. The robot adapts online, in the control loop, without retraining.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Train a base world model offline (e.g., neural network or physics simulator). At runtime, maintain a learned residual model (another small network) that predicts the error: (true next state) − (predicted next state). Update the residual online using recent experience. When rolling out for MPC, add both predictions: ŷ = base(s,a) + residual(s,a). Adapt the residual every few steps.

    Why it works: The residual captures slow, smooth changes (wear, environmental shift) without destabilizing the base model. Continuous adaptation keeps MPC aligned with current dynamics. Because the residual is small, online learning is cheap and stable—no catastrophic forgetting, no need for a fresh dataset.

  • ROBO
    Learning Active Tactile Perception through Belief-Space Control — a generative world model drives information-seeking touch via belief-space MPC

    Touch gives the robot information that vision can't: compliance, friction, whether the object is hollow. But the robot must decide where to poke before it knows what it will learn. This paper fuses a generative world model with belief-space MPC: the robot plans touches that most reduce uncertainty about the object's properties, using imagination to score which poke is most informative.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Learn a generative dynamics model (encoder-decoder RNN) that predicts the next tactile observation (force, skin deformation) given action and belief state. Maintain a Bayesian belief over object properties (compliance, mass, friction). At each step, enumerate candidate touch locations and roll forward the consequences for each, computing the posterior uncertainty about properties under each trajectory. Choose the action that most tightens the posterior.

    Why it works: Touch is information-seeking: the robot's goal is not immediate success but learning quickly. By planning in belief space—where trajectories reduce uncertainty—the robot converges on object properties much faster than reactive touching. The learned model predicts what new touch data will teach, enabling the robot to self-supervise its own exploration.

03

Video diffusion as a world simulator

vision
the problem

The richest thing a camera gives you is video, and the vision field already has models that generate stunning video. The question: can that generator double as a simulator — showing you what the world would look like a second from now if you did X?

the approaches

The vision lineage attacks the same problem from pixels: a video diffusion model, conditioned on an action or a prompt, generates the next frames — a watchable, photoreal simulation of the future. The insight driving the field is that a good enough video generator is a world model.

Because the models are enormous, much of the work is making them fast and long enough to be usable — linearised attention, caching, and structured attention to cut the quadratic cost.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Take the last few real frames plus the action you are weighing.
  2. Start the future as pure noise.
  3. Denoise it step by step, conditioned on those frames + the action, into a plausible next clip.
  4. Watch it — a photoreal guess at what that action produces.
  5. Use it as a test scenario, or as free training data for a policy.
  • CVPR
    Attention Surgery — linearises a video diffusion transformer for ~6× faster generation

    Video diffusion models generate stunning frames but run in real-time only on GPUs with massive memory. The bottleneck is attention's quadratic cost: O(n²) in sequence length. This paper performs linearised attention on the diffusion transformer's cross-attention—keeping the model's quality but slashing computation by ~6×.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Replace standard dot-product attention (softmax(QK^T)V) with linear-attention approximations: replace the softmax with a learned gating function, or use kernel methods to compute attention in linear time. In a video-generation model, apply this primarily to the cross-attention between keyframes and denoised content, keeping self-attention exact where coherence matters most.

    Why it works: Linear attention trades some expressiveness for speed, but for video diffusion (where the model is overly expressive anyway), the loss is imperceptible. The 6× speedup comes from reducing quadratic distance computations to linear passes. Fast generation means longer imagined rollouts in planning loops and more practical sim-like interactions.

  • CVPR
    VMonarch — structured Monarch-matrix attention gives sub-quadratic video diffusion

    Transformers attend to every token with every other token, O(n²) cost. For video, n can be thousands. This paper replaces the dense attention matrix with structured Monarch-matrix patterns—a learned, data-dependent factorization that keeps 90% of expressiveness while cutting the cost to sub-quadratic.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Decompose the attention weight matrix as a product of learned permutation and block-diagonal matrices (Monarch matrices). Instead of computing full attention, compute: A = P₁ ⊙ (D₁ ⊗ D₂) ⊙ P₂, where Ps are permutations and Ds are diagonal. This factorization is learned end-to-end. For video generation, apply it to both spatial and temporal attention.

    Why it works: Monarch factorizations are surprisingly expressive—they capture the key dependencies (nearby tokens, temporal continuity) while skipping the long-tail quadratic cost. Applied to video diffusion, this sub-quadratic pattern keeps the generated frames coherent and detailed but runs on smaller hardware, enabling longer imagined episodes.

  • CVPR
    D2Cache — caches the smooth denoising trajectory for ~10× acceleration

    Video generation is iterative: the model denoises step-by-step from noise to a frame. Every denoising step recomputes attention from scratch. But the attention map changes smoothly—a tiny increment at each step. This paper caches the denoising trajectory and interpolates, amortizing computation across steps by ~10×.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: After the first full denoising pass, store the attention maps and feature activations. On subsequent refinement steps, interpolate (or extrapolate) the cached trajectory forward slightly, recomputing only the incremental changes. For video, cache across time: frame t's attention is similar to frame t−1's, so predict the delta and add it.

    Why it works: The denoising trajectory is smooth, so cached + delta is nearly exact. By skipping redundant computation in tight loops, the decoder runs 10× faster. Faster decoding multiplies the number of imagined steps the robot can roll during planning, or trades latency for longer videos per tick.

  • CVPR
    VideoWorld 2 — decouples appearance (video diffusion) from latent action tokens for cross-domain transfer

    Video diffusion models entangle appearance (textures, colors) with dynamics (how things move). A model trained on driving will bias toward driving styles it saw, failing to transfer to new domains. This paper explicitly decouples them: learn appearance tokens (where do the pixels come from?) separately from action tokens (how do things move?), enabling cross-domain transfer without retraining.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Factorize the latent representation into appearance codes (VQ-encoded frames) and action tokens (discrete action embeddings). Train the diffusion model to predict: given appearance and action history, what's the next frame? The key: train on mixed domains (driving, robotics, animation) so the action space is unified. At test time, swap appearance tokens to change domain while keeping actions the same.

    Why it works: Decoupling allows a single action semantics ("move forward", "turn left") to apply across embodiments and environments. Appearance transfer is straightforward (swap the visual texture). This eliminates the need for domain-specific world models, and one learned model can generate realistic consequences for the same action across driving, manipulation, and locomotion.

04

Controllable & interactive (‘playable’) video

vision
the problem

A generated video you can only watch is a movie, not a simulator. To plan or play inside it, you have to be able to steer it — say what should happen, and have the generated future obey.

the approaches

A simulator you can’t steer is just a movie. This family makes the generated future controllable — per-object trajectories, camera moves, forces, or an agent’s actions all become inputs the model obeys, turning video generation into something you can play like a game.

The control signal ranges from language and points to explicit physics overlays, all mapped to what the model should render next.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Give the model a starting image.
  2. Draw a path (or press a direction) for the object or the camera.
  3. The model treats that path as a condition and generates frames where the object follows it.
  4. Change the path → get a different future, on demand.
  5. Chain these to ‘play’ the world like a game.
  • CVPR
    FlexTraj — point-trajectory control — dense or sparse — steers image-to-video generation

    To steer a video diffusion model, you need to tell it where each object should go. But specifying dense keyframes is tedious. This paper lets users draw sparse or dense trajectories (point-and-click paths), and the model generates frames where the objects obey those paths—turning video diffusion into an interactive tool you can play like a game.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encode trajectory information as a spatial guidance map (one channel per object, or a heatmap of the path). Condition the diffusion model on this guidance via cross-attention or concatenation. During generation, each denoising step respects the guidance: if the trajectory says the ball should be at (x, y) at frame t, the model learns to place it there.

    Why it works: Trajectory conditioning is geometrically natural: humans reason in paths and positions, not pixel values. By embedding path constraints into the denoising loop, the model balances generation quality (photorealism) with control fidelity (object follows the path). The result is a simulator users can steer intuitively.

  • CVPR
    TGT: Text-Grounded Trajectories — text specifies per-object motion paths for local control

    Natural language describes what things should do ("the car turns left, the pedestrian walks forward"), but images don't store language. This paper bridges them: text specifies per-object motions, which are compiled into spatial motion patterns that guide image-to-video generation, letting language directly control physical outcomes.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Parse language instructions into object-motion graphs (entity, verb, direction, duration). Render these into optical-flow-like fields or trajectory heatmaps. Condition the video diffusion model on these fields via cross-attention to text embeddings and guidance maps. During generation, the model learns to generate frames where objects follow the language-specified trajectories.

    Why it works: Language is compositional—"A moves left, B moves right"—and spatiotemporal patterns (flow fields, trajectories) are the natural intermediate representation. By parsing language to geometry and then to pixels, the model respects both linguistic intent and visual plausibility. One text prompt steers multiple objects coherently.

  • CVPR
    LAMP — an LLM turns a request into a camera/motion DSL that deterministically drives 3D trajectories

    Video diffusion is powerful but raw: it needs precise spatial coordinates or flow fields. Language models are powerful but abstract: they reason in tasks and goals. This paper stacks them: an LLM translates a user's request into a domain-specific language for camera and object motion, which is then fed to the video model, making it a programmable, deterministic simulator.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Define a DSL with primitives: camera(pan, zoom, position), object(trajectory, rotation, scale). Train an LLM on examples to map natural-language instructions to DSL programs. At generation time, parse the DSL into guidance signals (camera matrices, per-object paths, 3D transforms). Condition video diffusion on these structured signals.

    Why it works: The DSL bridges semantic understanding (LLM's strength) and visual control (diffusion's strength). Determinism comes from precise DSL execution, not model sampling. Users get the fluidity of language input with the reproducibility of code, and the video model generates frames consistent with the structured plan.

Object-centric & relational dynamics

robotics
the problem

A single blob of latent state smears many objects together, so it generalizes badly — rearrange the objects and it is lost. But the world is made of things that interact by shared rules.

the approaches

Instead of one monolithic state, factor the scene into objects and learn how they interact. A graph of objects-and-relations predicts the next state and generalises to arrangements never seen, because the rules of interaction are shared.

This is how world models reach long-horizon, multi-object tasks that a flat latent can’t hold.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Split the scene into objects — each a node with its state (pose, velocity).
  2. Draw edges between objects that can affect each other (touching, near).
  3. Predict each object’s next state by passing messages along its edges (a graph net).
  4. Because the interaction rules are shared, a brand-new arrangement of the same objects just works.
  5. Chain the predictions to plan a multi-object, long-horizon task.
  • ROBO
    Points2Plans — composable relational dynamics turn point clouds into long-horizon manipulation plans

    To stack blocks or rearrange objects, a robot needs to predict how each object will move when it acts, not just the aggregate scene. But a flat latent state smears all objects together, so rearranging them confuses the model. This paper factors the world into composable relational dynamics—each object is a node, edges capture which objects interact, and a graph network predicts how each object responds. Rearrange the objects and the same interaction rules apply; the model generalizes to long-horizon multi-object tasks.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A monolithic latent world model can't generalize to new object arrangements; it memorized one spatial configuration, not the rules of interaction.

    The mechanism: 1. Represent the scene as a point cloud (one 3D point per object) plus per-object properties (mass, color). 2. Train a graph neural network to predict the next point cloud from the current points + action, passing messages along edges (each object updates based on its neighbors). 4.     Why it works: Shared interaction rules are the key to generalization. If the model learns "when two objects touch, they collide," that rule works whether there are 3 objects or 30, and whether they are in a line or a grid. A graph net factorizes the dynamics so the model learns reusable rules, not the memorized layout of one scene. This is what allows zero-shot transfer to new configurations and long-horizon planning.

As world models proliferate, telling a good one from a plausible-looking one becomes its own problem. Do they respect geometry, physics, and causality — or just paint convincing frames? This family builds the benchmarks that ask.

The recurring finding is sobering: many models that render beautifully fail the moment you perturb the action or check causality.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Take a trained world model and a starting state.
  2. Feed it action A; record the predicted future. Feed it a different action B; record that future.
  3. A real world model gives genuinely different, correct futures for A and B.
  4. A model that only learned to paint frames gives nearly the same future regardless — it failed the causality test.
  5. Also perturb the camera and check geometry stays consistent; score across many such probes.

    Beyond Simulation — shows world models fail under ego perturbation; proposes causality-aware metrics

    Most world models look great in videos but fail at the core task: predicting how the world responds to causal interventions. If you change the action, does the future meaningfully change, or does the model paint the same frames regardless? This paper builds metrics that catch models that look good but are actually just painting.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Take a world model and a test scenario. (2) Feed it action A and record the predicted future. (3) Feed it a different action B and record that future. (4) Compute the difference: if the model is causal, A and B should produce significantly different futures. (5) If the futures are too similar, the model is not actually modeling causality, only painting frames. (6) Perturb the camera and check whether geometry is consistent across views. (7) Score across many perturbations to get a causality score.

    Why it works: Causality is the core requirement for a world model (not just a video generator). Testing whether action changes lead to different futures reveals whether the model learned dynamics or just learned to paint plausible frames.

    WorldLens — full-spectrum driving-world-model evaluation exposes texture-vs-geometry-vs-behaviour trade-offs

    Driving world models trade off between texture realism, geometric accuracy, and behavioral causality. A model might render pretty frames but place cars in the wrong lanes, or predict unrealistic motion. This paper builds a 12-metric suite that measures each of these independently, so you know exactly what each model trades off.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Collect ground-truth driving videos with annotations (lane positions, object trajectories, actions). (2) For each world model under test, generate future video. (3) Evaluate texture fidelity (LPIPS, FVD, etc.), geometry (object bounding box accuracy, camera consistency), and behavior (does the action cause the predicted changes?). (4) Report a score for each. (5) Visualize the trade-offs: a model might score high on texture but low on geometry.

    Why it works: A single metric hides trade-offs. By evaluating texture, geometry, and behavior separately, you see exactly where each model strengths and weaknesses lie, guiding which to choose for which task.

    Ref4D-VideoBench — a 12-metric suite across semantics, motion, temporal and world-knowledge consistency

    Video world models are proliferating, but there is no standard way to evaluate them. This paper proposes a 12-metric suite that tests semantics (do objects stay labeled correctly?), motion (is optical flow consistent?), temporal consistency (do changes persist?), and world knowledge (do physics and occlusions work?), grounding evaluation in what actually matters for planning and simulation.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Create or use existing video datasets with multi-level annotations (instance masks, optical flow, depth, action labels). (2) For each video model, generate a multi-frame prediction. (3) Compare semantics: do segmentation maps stay consistent? (4) Compare motion: does optical flow match? (5) Compare temporal consistency: do objects persist without popping? (6) Compare world knowledge: do physical constraints hold (occlusions, gravity)? (7) Report scores for all 12 metrics, so different models' strengths are visible.

    Why it works: Benchmarking reveals what models actually do. Different tasks (planning, data augmentation, rendering) weight these metrics differently, so transparent evaluation guides model selection.

  • CVPR
    TiViBench — benchmarks ‘think-in-video’ reasoning across structural, spatial, logical and planning axes

    Video world models need to support reasoning: a human watches a video and understands the structure of a plan, the geometry of a space, or the logic of a scenario. Can the model? This paper builds TiViBench to score how well video models support 'think-in-video' reasoning across structural, spatial, logical, and planning dimensions.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: (1) Collect diverse video reasoning tasks: compositional structure (does the model understand scenes are made of objects?), spatial reasoning (can it track occlusions and depth?), causal logic (do changes follow from actions?), and planning (can imagined futures support plan validity checks?). (2) For each task, generate a video prediction from the world model. (3) Run a reasoning test on it: can a classifier or a human judge whether the reasoning holds? (4) Report scores on each dimension. (5) This reveals whether the model predicts pixels that support reasoning, not just realistic-looking frames.

    Why it works: Reasoning benchmarks measure utility, not just visual quality. A model might score high on FVD (realism) but low on spatial reasoning because it averages over object positions. TiViBench catches that disconnect.

Part IV

Where it stands

World models are the fastest-moving idea in embodied AI right now. What is still hard:

Compounding error. Predictions drift over long rollouts; staying accurate far enough ahead to plan on is still the core hard problem.
Pixels vs control. Photoreal video models are heavy and slow; compact latent models are fast but can’t be watched or easily debugged. Bridging the two is open.
Causality, not correlation. Many models paint plausible frames but break when you change the action — they learned appearance, not cause and effect.
Real physics. Contact, fluids, deformation, and long-tail events remain hard to predict reliably.
Honest evaluation. A model can look stunning and still be a poor simulator; benchmarks that measure controllability and physics are only now arriving.
One model, every task. Whether a single foundation world model can serve driving, manipulation, and navigation at once is the open bet of 2025.
