Grasping and dexterous hands

242 papers · move — the physical act of touching, gripping, and handling objects

The big picture — connecting the dots

A robot needs to pick up things—thousands of different objects, in cluttered piles, with materials ranging from cloth to glass to fragile ceramics. The core problem: every object is different. Shape, weight, texture, transparency all change where and how the hand should grip. For decades, grippers were crude tools (two fingers pinching or a suction cup) that only worked in factories where every item is identical. The deeper challenge is that the robot must grip not just anything, but the right part of the thing for the job ahead — gripping a knife by its blade is mechanically possible but wrong. Multi-fingered hands that can do fine work like humans exist in labs but break often, cost far more, and require expert engineering. Training robots to manipulate with those complex hands also means collecting thousands of demonstrations—and getting humans to teleoperate two hands with dozens of joints simultaneously at high quality is brutally hard.

Three intertwined obstacles the 2025 papers are attacking:

  • ① Seeing the object clearly enough to grip it. Transparent materials — glass, plastic — break depth sensors. The robot sees the glass but not where it actually is in 3D space. Monocular Depth Estimation and Segmentation for Transparent Objects with Iterative Semantic and Geometric Fusion solves this by running shape and depth estimation in parallel and letting each branch correct the other. Cluttered bins are another visibility trap; GraspSAM borrows from image segmentation and adds a prompt-based interface so users can say "grab the thing in the red box" without retraining.
  • ② Collecting enough training data without burning out humans. Fine manipulation is too complex to program by hand — it must be learned from demonstrations. But teleoperation of two multi-finger hands is punishing. EgoMimic and Bunny-VisionPro both solve this by bridging human and robot: one uses egocentric glasses to capture human hand motion, the other uses VR+haptics to make controlling two robot hands real and tactile. DexMimicGen goes further — it takes a handful of human demos and generates a large synthetic set by moving objects around and retargeting the motion, amplifying one person's effort many times over.
  • ③ Choosing the grip that serves the task, not just any grasp. A robot that only asks "where can I close my fingers?" picks the wrong grip. Catch It! trains a full whole-body controller in simulation for catching tasks, General-Purpose Clothes Manipulation with Semantic Keypoints learns semantic points on garments so a language model can plan multi-step folding and handling, and ViViDex learns task-aware grasping from ordinary human video — no special equipment, no labels — by inferring reward from how the object moves.

The throughline: the field is converging on systems that see objects accurately (especially hard ones like transparent or cluttered items), collect and generate training data at human-affordable scale, and make grasping choices that respect both the object and the job ahead. The 2025 work is moving away from hand-coded grippers and task-specific training toward unified models — like GraspSAM — that generalize to new objects and prompt types, and toward learning systems — like ViViDex and EgoMimic — that can absorb diverse human demonstrations without frame-by-frame labels. Still open: hands that are cheap, durable, and practical enough for deployment outside research labs; the integration of all three pieces (seeing, learning, reasoning) into one robust pipeline; and the inverse problem: making robots understand what humans intend when they ask for an unfamiliar grasp of an object never seen before.

The problem — in depth

Pick up a mug. Your hand knows, without thinking, where to close around it, how hard to squeeze so it doesn't slip, and how to hold it if you are about to drink versus about to hand it to someone. You do this in a pile of dishes, for mugs you have never seen before, for ceramic or plastic or paper cups, and you adjust the moment the mug starts sliding. A robot does none of this naturally.

The core difficulty is that every object in the world is different. Shape, weight, slipperiness, and fragility all change how the hand should close. Most robot hands today use simple two-finger grippers that either pinch or suction. They work in warehouses, where every item is the same, in the same place, every time. Move them to a kitchen, a hospital, or a grocery aisle and they fail constantly. Objects are piled on each other, tucked behind other things, or made of glass, cloth, or foam that the sensors cannot read clearly.

Transparent objects — glass bottles, plastic cups — are a special trap. The cameras that measure depth do so by bouncing light off surfaces. Glass and clear plastic let light through or bend it in unpredictable ways. The robot sees the object but gets a wrong or missing measurement of where it actually sits in space. It reaches, and misses, or crushes.

Then there is the question of intent. Picking up a knife by the blade is wrong even if it is technically graspable. The right grip depends on what comes next — cutting, passing to a person, storing. A robot that only asks "where can I close my fingers?" will choose the wrong grip for the job. This connects to the deep problem every robot faces: it is not enough to move the body; the movement must serve a purpose that the robot understands.

Multi-fingered hands, which look like human hands and can do the fine work humans do, have existed in labs for decades. The mechanical design is hard, the control is harder, and they break often. They cost far more than simple grippers and require engineers to maintain them. This is why they almost never leave the lab, even though fine tasks — folding clothes, peeling vegetables, opening jars — require them.

Collecting training data is its own problem. You cannot program a multi-fingered hand to fold a shirt by writing down the exact finger positions step by step. The motion is too complex and too sensitive to small differences in the shirt's shape. Researchers instead have humans perform tasks and record the motions so the robot can learn from the recordings. But teleoperation of two hands with many fingers at once is very hard to do well, and getting thousands of recordings takes months.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Reasoning about purpose to choose the right grip

In plain termsWhen you pick up a knife to cut, you grab the handle—not the blade, even though that's graspable. The robot needs to ask itself: what am I doing next? Only then pick where to grip.
The sub-problemA robot that only looks at shape will pick a grip that is easy to reach but wrong for the job. It must first understand what the object is for and what task comes next, then choose where and how to grip accordingly.
Large language model to find the right part — AffordGrasp. Uses an LLM to work out what the user wants, then finds the right part of the object to grip before computing the hand position.
Memory of human grips — RTAGrasp. Stores how humans grip objects in video, finds the most similar stored example for the given task, and maps that grip onto the new object without task-specific training.
Learning from task labels — Task-Oriented 6-DoF Grasp Pose Detection in Clutters. Built a large labeled dataset of over 2 million grasps across 6 task types and 198 objects, showing that training with task labels makes the robot pick hand positions that serve what comes next.
Physics-aware grip — PhyGrasp. Adds material properties—weight, slipperiness—as text alongside the object's 3D shape, so the grip accounts for physics, not just geometry.

The robot learns to reason about purpose before grasping, moving beyond shape alone to pick grips that actually work for the job ahead.

Seeing through clutter and transparent materials

In plain termsYour eyes see glass because light bounces off it differently than plastic. A robot camera gets fooled—it bounces light back from the wrong spot. Use color and depth together, let one fix the other's mistakes, and you see through the confusion.
The sub-problemBefore a hand can go anywhere, the robot must know exactly where the object is. In messy bins and with see-through materials—glass, clear plastic—that is far harder than it sounds because sensors bounce light off surfaces and get confused.
Merging color and depth streams — DA-Fusion. Runs color and depth images through separate processing streams, then uses flexible attention to merge them automatically, beating either channel alone for pulling objects out of cluttered bins.
Filling in missing depth — TransDiff. Takes noisy depth from a sensor on a transparent object, combines it with edges and outlines from the color image, and feeds the whole thing to a model that gradually fills in a correct depth map.
Iterative shape and depth correction — Monocular Depth Estimation and Segmentation for Transparent Objects with Iterative Semantic and Geometric Fusion. Runs two branches—one for shape, one for depth—that correct each other in several passes, rather than making one prediction and stopping.
Confidence scores for grasp points — vMF-Contact. Adds a confidence score to every predicted grasp point and distinguishes between "sensor was noisy" and "I have never seen this type of object," helping the robot know when to trust itself and when to ask for help.

Robots can now see through clutter and transparent materials by combining multiple cues and letting branches correct each other.

Learning from human demonstrations at scale

In plain termsA robot can't fold a shirt if you give it rules—too many tiny details. So watch a human do it, record it, and let the robot learn. The hard part: getting thousands of recordings is exhausting. Use VR, cameras on glasses, or make up extra examples in fake worlds.
The sub-problemThe motions needed for fine grasping are too complex to write by hand. The solution is to watch humans do it and learn from their demonstrations. But getting enough recordings is the bottleneck because teleoperation of two multi-finger hands is hard.
Egocentric glasses plus robot recordings — EgoMimic. A person wears glasses with built-in cameras, records tasks, and those recordings are aligned to the robot's arm geometry and mixed with a small set of actual robot recordings, dramatically increasing training data at low cost.
Synthetic data multiplication — DexMimicGen. Starts with just 60 human demonstrations and automatically generates 21,000 synthetic variants in simulation by moving objects around and adapting the motion to each new position.
VR teleoperation with haptics — Bunny-VisionPro. Lets an operator use a VR headset to control two robot hands in real time, with touch feedback through small haptic devices, making collection of two-handed demonstrations safer and more precise.
Learning reward from motion — ViViDex. Watches ordinary human video (no special equipment), tracks the object being handled, builds a reward signal from how the object moves, and uses reinforcement learning to train the robot hand without needing exact finger-position labels.

Humans can now feed their demonstrations to robots through multiple channels—egocentric video, VR, synthetic expansion—making large-scale training data collection affordable.

Unified models that work across objects and hand types

In plain termsOne gripper, one object, one task = one trained robot. That breaks down fast. Instead, build one brain that works on any gripper shape, any object shape, any task—like how humans use one brain on a thousand different tools.
The sub-problemTraining a separate model for each object, each task, or each robot hand does not scale. The field needs single systems that generalize across all three dimensions.
Hand-agnostic gripper model — D(R,O) Grasp. Learns one model that takes the hand's joint layout and the object's 3D shape as inputs and outputs where and how to grip—the same model runs on hands with different numbers of fingers without retraining.
Prompt-based grasp detection — GraspSAM. Takes a large image-segmentation model, adds small extra layers, and turns it into a grasp predictor that accepts a point, a box, or a word as a user prompt and works on objects it was never trained on.
Unified tool and articulated object handling — UniAff. Builds one system covering both tools and jointed objects (drawers, scissors) using a large multimodal model to read images and say where to touch and how to move, replacing two separate incompatible pipelines with one.
Skills that transfer to new homes — Robot Utility Models. Trains five manipulation skills on large, high-quality demonstration sets, then deploys them in new homes without retraining, using a language model to watch the robot and call for a retry when something goes wrong.

Unified models generalize across hand morphologies, objects, and new environments, scaling deployment beyond hand-coded, task-specific systems.

Fine multi-step manipulation with multi-fingered hands

In plain termsPeeling a carrot takes three moves: grip it, slide to the middle, peel it, then grip again. Many fingers give you the power—but planning ten fingers across five steps gets complicated fast. Break it into chunks, train each chunk, string them together.
The sub-problemSome tasks require more than a single grip: repositioning an object between the fingers, peeling, folding, catching. Multi-fingered hands can do this, but controlling all the fingers and planning the finger movements in sequence is hard.
Grip repositioning for next action — Vegetable Peeling. Designs the repositioning step and the final holding step together, so the hand ends in a grip suitable for the next action rather than any stable grip.
Real-time finger search — DROP. Skips pre-training entirely and searches for good finger movements in real time at each step, guided by a camera estimate of the object's current pose.
Whole-body catching controller — Catch It! Trains a whole-body controller—moving base, arm, and twelve-finger hand—in simulation across many thrown object shapes and throw angles, then moves it directly to a real robot.
Semantic keypoints for deformable objects — General-Purpose Clothes Manipulation with Semantic Keypoints. Identifies named points on garments from a depth camera and lets a language model plan what sequence of steps to take, reaching well beyond fixed-shape objects to soft, deformable ones.

Multi-fingered hands can now plan and execute sequences of repositioning, peeling, folding, and catching by breaking tasks into steps and learning each from data.

Where it stands

The field has made real progress on several hard pieces: robots can now pick unfamiliar objects from cluttered bins more reliably, transparent objects are no longer invisible to the pipeline, and task-aware grasping is moving out of theory into working systems. The biggest remaining gaps are in hands that are cheap and tough enough for real use outside a lab, in collecting enough training data for complex two-handed fine tasks without enormous human effort, and in combining all the pieces — seeing, reasoning about purpose, and executing precise finger motion — into one system that handles the full range of objects a person deals with every day.

Bunny-VisionPro: Real-Time Bimanual Dexterous Teleoperation for Imitation Learning

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Teaching robots to manipulate with two multi-fingered hands requires collecting thousands of human demonstrations, but controlling two separate hands with many joints each through teleoperation is exhausting and error-prone. The human operator struggles to see and feel what the robot is actually doing.

The approach. Let the operator see through a VR headset and feel through vibration motors on their own hands, so the robot mirrors exactly what the human does. The headset tracks the human's hand motions, IK converts them to robot poses, and tactile feedback loops back from the robot's touch sensors.

What's unique. Haptic feedback closes the sensorimotor loop—the operator feels the robot's contacts in real time, not from video alone. This dramatically improves demonstration quality and reduces failed teleoperated trials that corrupt training data.

IROS 2025 · 11 citations · learn-from-human (bimanual teleoperation)

What it does

This is a VR-based teleoperation system that lets a human operator control a robot with two dexterous hands—each with multiple fingers—in real time. The operator wears an Apple Vision Pro headset which tracks their hands and fingers, and these poses are instantly mapped onto the robot's hands. The system gives the operator haptic (touch) feedback through low-cost vibration motors, and it automatically stops the robot if it's about to collide or reach an unstable position. The high-quality demonstrations collected this way are then used to train imitation learning policies for complex, long-horizon bimanual dexterous tasks—a sub-approach called learn-from-human (specifically bimanual teleoperation, which is a variant combining learning from human video/teleoperation with two-arm coordination).

The method, in detail

The system operates in a closed loop:

Input: The Apple Vision Pro headset tracks 26 hand keypoints per hand (finger joints, palm, wrist) at 90 Hz using visual hand pose estimation.

Retargeting: These human hand poses are not directly sent to the robot. Instead, a retargeting algorithm (using the dex-retargeting library) maps the human hand configuration into the robot's hand configuration. This is necessary because the robot hands have different finger lengths, joint ranges, and kinematic structures than human hands. The retargeting optimizes for hand-object compatibility (grasp quality) while staying within the robot's joint limits.

Safety layer: Before executing any motion, the system checks two things: (1) collision detection—will the robot arms or hands hit the object or environment?—and (2) singularity detection—is the arm configuration unstable or unreachable? If either check fails, the motion is blocked and the operator is notified.

Haptic feedback: Tactile sensors on the robot hands (force/touch feedback) are read continuously. These signals are processed and mapped to vibration motor patterns on the operator's hands (using ERM—eccentric rotating mass—actuators), creating a tactile loop so the human feels when the robot touches something.

Real-time constraint: All of this—hand pose capture, retargeting, collision/singularity checking, haptic mapping, and robot motor commands—happens at 10 Hz for the teleoperation control loop, with hand pose estimation running at 90 Hz.

Data collection: When the operator performs a task (e.g., pick-and-place or in-hand manipulation), the entire trajectory—robot arm and hand joint angles, camera images, object poses—is recorded as a demonstration.

Imitation learning: After collecting demonstrations (from ~60 human teleoperated trials), these are used to train a policy using methods like ACT (Action Chunking with Transformers), Diffusion Policy, or DP3. The policy learns to execute the same task from visual input (depth camera) without needing the VR headset or teleoperation.

The key trick that makes this work: haptic feedback eliminates the disconnect between what the operator intends and what the robot actually does, reducing task failure rates during teleoperation. And the safety layer ensures that the human does not accidentally command the robot into a crash, which would corrupt the demonstration.

EgoMimic: Scaling Imitation Learning via Egocentric Video

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Robots learn to manipulate by watching demonstrations, but robots and humans have different bodies—different arm lengths, gripper shapes, and sensing. Teaching from human video is wasteful because you have to manually record robot-specific data anyway.

The approach. Build a robot whose arms are shaped like human arms, then train a single neural network on both human and robot demonstrations side-by-side. The network learns shared motor patterns that work for both embodiments.

What's unique. Rather than treating human video as high-level intent to be abstracted away, it treats human and robot trajectories as equivalent. This lets the policy absorb richer motion diversity from humans while staying grounded in real robot kinematics.

ICRA 2025 · 9 citations · learn-from-human

What it does — 2-3 plain sentences, and which sub-approach it is

EgoMimic teaches a robot to perform long-horizon bimanual manipulation tasks by learning from egocentric (first-person) human videos paired with robot demonstrations. Instead of extracting high-level intent from human video, it treats human hand motion and robot arm motion as equivalent embodied demonstrations and trains a single policy on both simultaneously. This is the learn-from-human sub-approach: the system learns manipulation from human videos captured with Project Aria glasses, then transfers that knowledge to control a bimanual robot arm.

The method, in detail — step by step, plain words but technical

Data capture: Operators wear Meta Project Aria glasses (smart glasses with wide egocentric cameras and hand tracking) while performing manipulation tasks. The glasses record the operator's viewpoint and automatically track their hand poses in 3D. Separately, a low-cost bimanual robot (called Eve, with two 6-DoF ViperX arms) is teleoperated to perform the same tasks, and its arm joint angles, wrist camera images, and gripper states are recorded.

Robot design: The bimanual manipulator is purpose-built to minimize the kinematic gap between human arm morphology and robot kinematics. This means the arm lengths, joint ranges, and gripper design are tuned to match human hand capabilities, making the task dynamics similar for both.

Cross-domain alignment: A technical pipeline aligns the human hand trajectories with the robot arm trajectories. This involves computing the 3D hand poses from Aria glasses, projecting them into the robot's coordinate frame, and matching them to robot joint actions. The system handles the mismatch in appearance (human skin vs. robot metal) by learning a shared visual representation that ignores superficial differences.

Co-training architecture: A single neural network backbone (a shared vision encoder + policy network) processes both human and robot data. For human data, the network predicts the original 3D hand poses (pose prediction task). For robot data, it predicts both hand poses and robot joint actions (dual output). This forces the network to learn representations that work for both embodiments. The key trick: masked images during training hide irrelevant visual differences (human vs. robot appearance) and encourage the network to focus on the manipulation semantics—the actual motion and strategy.

Training: The policy is trained end-to-end with supervised learning on the aligned trajectory data. The scaling effect emerges: 1 hour of human hand data provides richer motion diversity than 1 hour of robot data (because human hands move continuously and expressively), so human data is weighted more heavily during training. After training, only the robot data branch is used for inference.

Key insight: Treating human and robot data symmetrically (rather than as intent-only guidance) allows the network to learn richer motor primitives and generalizes better to unseen objects and scenes. The system achieves zero-shot generalization to new task variations without task-specific retraining.

Monocular Depth Estimation and Segmentation for Transparent Object with Iterative Semantic and Geometric Fusion

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Depth cameras bounce light off surfaces to measure distance. Glass and clear plastic let light through or bend it, so the robot cannot see where transparent objects actually are in 3D. It reaches out and hits empty air or a distorted position.

The approach. Run shape prediction (semantic) and depth prediction (geometric) as two separate branches that correct each other. Shape estimation finds the object's outline; depth estimation gets its distance. Pass the results back through the network a few more times to refine both.

What's unique. Earlier work tried to merge these signals once. Here they iteratively bounce between branches—each helping the other converge on the right answer—so the final depth map respects both the object's geometry and the camera data.

ICRA · 9 citations · grasp-detection: perception layer

What it does — 2-3 plain sentences, and which sub-approach it is

This paper tackles the perception challenge in robotic grasping of transparent objects—glass, plastic, acrylic—by estimating both their 3D shape (depth) and exact boundaries (segmentation) from a single RGB camera image. The novel contribution is that it does both tasks at once using a neural network that learns semantic (shape-classification) and geometric (depth-boundary) features jointly, refining the predictions iteratively to handle the hard cases where standard methods fail on transparent materials. Sub-approach: grasp-detection (the perception layer that identifies where and what shape an object is before a gripper approaches).

The method, in detail — step by step, plain words but technical

Input: A single RGB image of a scene containing transparent objects (no extra depth camera, no 3D sensor, no multiple views).

Backbone: A Vision Transformer (ViT) extracts tokens from the RGB image by treating it as non-overlapping 16×16 patches. The model selects 4 layers of transformer blocks (out of 12 total) at different depths to capture multi-scale features, from shallow edges to deep semantic meaning.

Multi-scale feature pyramid: Features are reshaped from transformer tokens into two parallel feature pyramids—one branch for depth prediction, one for segmentation prediction—each with 4 levels (coarse to fine).

Semantic and geometric fusion module: This is the core novelty. At each scale, the two branches (depth and segmentation) are fed through channel attention and spatial attention mechanisms that compute which features matter most. The two branches then "multiply" their attention weights symmetrically, so that depth features benefit from the segmentation branch's learned shape boundaries, and segmentation features benefit from the depth branch's geometric cues. This cross-talk happens at every scale.

Iterative refinement strategy: Instead of one forward pass, the predictions are refined multiple times (3 iterations). After the first pass produces rough depth and segmentation maps, those results feed back into the network through lightweight gated units, allowing the model to "look again" and refine blurry or uncertain regions. Each iteration applies stronger supervision losses to push predictions toward ground truth.

Training losses: The network minimizes two objectives:

  • Depth loss: L2 pixel-wise error + L1 gradient penalty (penalizing jagged edges) + surface normal error (penalizing shape inconsistency)
  • Segmentation loss: standard cross-entropy classification loss for each pixel (transparent vs. background)
  • Total loss: weighted sum (depth loss weight = 1.0, segmentation weight = 0.1)

Datasets for training: Syn-TODD (113,000+ synthetic RGB-depth pairs of transparent objects in 3D scenes) and ClearPose (350,000+ real-world frames with hand-annotated segmentation). The synthetic data provides scale; the real data ensures generalization.

Output: Depth map (per-pixel 3D distance) + segmentation map (per-pixel class label). A robotic arm's grasp planner uses these two outputs to find the object's 3D position, orientation, and surface normals—all needed to plan a stable grasp.

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Robots need thousands of demonstrations to learn dexterous two-arm manipulation, but collecting that many teleoperated examples is prohibitively expensive—each one requires a human sitting down to drive two arms with many fingers at once for minutes.

What it does — 2-3 plain sentences, and which sub-approach it is

This is a data-generation approach: it solves the bottleneck of expensive human teleoperation by synthesizing diverse trajectories in simulation that can then be used to train imitation-learning policies. The system includes a real-to-sim-to-real pipeline that successfully deploys trained policies to real humanoid robots performing complex two-arm manipulation tasks like can sorting and coffee preparation.

The method, in detail — step by step, plain words but technical

1. Human demonstration capture: Operators collect 60 high-quality teleoperated demonstrations of bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks. These recordings capture the full trajectory of both robot arms and all finger movements needed to accomplish each task (e.g., grasping, picking, placing, assembling).

2. Trajectory synthesis (the core innovation): Starting from each human demo, DexMimicGen automatically generates multiple synthetic trajectories by:

- Perturbation & retargeting: Applying small variations to object positions, sizes, and poses within the simulation environment. - Waypoint interpolation: Generating smooth intermediate waypoints between key poses to create complete feasible trajectories that a robot arm and hand controller can follow in simulation.

3. 4. Policy learning in simulation: The generated dataset trains behavioral cloning policies using imitation learning (via robomimic). Simple supervised learning directly from state observations to robot actions converges faster and more reliably than reinforcement learning for these high-DoF systems. - The policies are trained on diverse simulated trajectories, building robustness to environmental variation.

- The simulation (Robosuite/BiGym) accurately models bimanual kinematics, physics, and sensor readings (cameras, proprioception).

- Domain randomization across synthetic data reduces the visual and dynamic mismatch between simulation and reality.

Key trick that makes it work: Rather than require thousands of human teleoperation hours, DexMimicGen exploits the fact that most trajectory variation in manipulation tasks comes from *changes in object placement and task configuration*, not fundamental changes in hand control strategy. By reusing the core motion from each human demo and applying smart retargeting, the system gets massive data amplification (350x: 60 → 21,000 demos) while preserving the quality and physicality of demonstrated manipulation.

ViViDex: Learning Vision-Based Dexterous Manipulation from Human Videos

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Extracting hand motion from human video is noisy—hand tracking jitters, occlusions, and artifacts. Training a robot directly on that noise produces shaky, unphysical motions that fail on a real robot.

The approach. Use reinforcement learning as a denoiser: train a policy to follow the noisy trajectory while obeying physics. Successful rollouts become clean, physically plausible demonstrations. Then train a vision-based policy that only sees cameras, not ground-truth state.

What's unique. Two-stage pipeline bridges human video (inherently noisy) and robot control (requires smoothness). The intermediate RL stage cleans the data; the final stage adds vision, so the robot works with its onboard cameras.

ICRA 2025 · 7 citations · learn-from-human

What it does

ViViDex trains a multi-fingered robot hand to manipulate objects by learning directly from human video demonstrations. Instead of relying on noisy hand-tracking estimates or ground-truth object positions, it first uses reinforcement learning to "clean up" the human motion in simulation, then trains a vision-based policy that works on the real robot using only RGB-D camera input. This is a learn-from-human approach—it bridges the gap between how humans move their hands and how robots must move their arms and fingers to accomplish the same manipulation goals.

The method, in detail

ViViDex works in three stages:

Stage 1: Trajectory retargeting. The system extracts hand poses from human video using the MANO hand model (a standard representation of human hand geometry). These poses are then converted into robot joint angles via optimization—for each frame, the system finds robot finger positions that match the human hand's positions as closely as possible, while also smoothing out jittery motion.

Stage 2: State-based policy refinement. The retargeted trajectories contain noise and artifacts. To clean them up, a reinforcement learning policy (using actor-critic with PPO) is trained per-video to follow the noisy trajectory while obeying physics (no self-collisions, realistic forces). The reward function has two modes: in the pre-grasp phase (reaching toward the object), the robot is encouraged to match the human's hand trajectory with exponential distance penalties. In the manipulation phase (grasping and moving), rewards combine hand-motion tracking, object-motion tracking (both position and orientation), contact rewards (penalizing hand-object penetration), and lift bonuses (rewarding successful grasps). After training, successful rollouts from this policy generate clean, physically plausible demonstrations.

Stage 3: Vision-based policy training. Now the system discards the clean state trajectories and instead trains a final policy that takes only RGB-D point clouds as input. The key trick is coordinate transformation: the point cloud is represented in three coordinate frames simultaneously—world frame (absolute positions), target-frame (relative to the object's current pose), and hand-frame (relative to the palm and fingertips). These three point clouds are concatenated, giving the policy dense spatial context about how the robot hand relates to the object without needing the ground-truth object state. Features are extracted using PointNet, then fed to either a behavior-cloning network (ℓ2 action regression) or a diffusion policy (a denoising generative model that is more robust to distribution shift). The diffusion approach generally outperforms behavior cloning on real-world deployment.

The entire pipeline is trained end-to-end on a dataset of DexYCB human demonstrations, with trajectory augmentation (randomly varying initial object poses) improving generalization to unseen objects.

RTAGrasp: Learning Task-Oriented Grasping from Human Videos via Retrieval, Transfer, and Alignment

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A robot must choose where to grip an object to accomplish a task, not just find any graspable point. But every object looks different, every task has subtle requirements, and training a system that handles both is expensive.

The approach. Store human demonstration videos in memory. When faced with a new object and task, find the most similar stored example, then use vision foundation models to identify corresponding points on the new object and map the grasp there—no retraining.

What's unique. Pure in-context learning via similarity—retrieval handles task and object novelty at once. Foundation models (CLIP for retrieval, SAM for correspondence) do the heavy lifting with no task-specific training.

ICRA · 6 citations · task-oriented grasping (learn-from-human)

What it does

RTAGrasp learns where and how to grasp an object to accomplish a specific manipulation task by studying human demonstration videos. It retrieves the most similar grasping example from a memory of human videos, then transfers that grasp pose to a new target object without any task-specific training, making it a pure learn-from-human approach that uses foundation vision models to bridge the gap between human and robot hands.

The method, in detail

Input & Problem Setup: The system takes two inputs: a task instruction (e.g., "pick up a mug by the handle") and a single RGB image of a target object the robot must grasp. The robot has no prior training data for this specific object or task.

Stage 1 — Memory Construction: First, the system builds a robot memory by ingesting human demonstration videos. From each video, it extracts two things: (1) the precise 3D position where the human grasps the object, and (2) the 3D direction (orientation) of the grasp. This extraction is done by tracking the human hand in the video, localizing the contact point on the object, and computing the grasp vector. Both position and direction constraints are stored in the memory.

Stage 2 — Retrieval: Given a task instruction and the target object image, the system searches its memory for the human demonstration that is most similar to the current task. Similarity is measured using semantic embeddings from vision foundation models (like CLIP), which encode both the task description and the visual appearance of objects in the stored videos. The retrieval step finds the closest match without any fine-tuning.

Stage 3 — Transfer: Once the most similar human grasp is retrieved, the system uses a vision foundation model (SAM-like segmentation or CLIP-based features) to establish semantic correspondence between the human-grasped object in the stored video and the current target object in the input image. This correspondence tells the system where the analogous grasp location is on the new object. For example, if a human grasped a coffee mug by its handle in the memory, and the target object is a different mug, the transfer step identifies where the handle is on the target and maps the grasp there.

Stage 4 — Alignment: Finally, the transferred grasp position and direction are aligned with the robot's kinematic constraints and action space. The robot's gripper or hand must physically be able to reach and orient itself at the transferred grasp pose. GraspSAM: When Segment Anything Model Meets Grasp Detection

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Training grasp detectors requires thousands of labeled images per object type. A robot that works on only trained objects is useless; you need generalization.

The approach. Start with SAM, a massive foundation model already trained on millions of segmentation tasks across all objects. Freeze it and add only small adapter layers that learn to predict grasp angles and widths alongside segmentation masks. Train only on existing grasp datasets (Jacquard, Grasp-Anything).

What's unique. SAM's prior is so strong that minimal fine-tuning (via adapters, not full retraining) teaches grasp prediction. The model generalizes to unseen object types with almost no performance drop—the broad foundation does the heavy lifting.

ICRA · 6 citations · grasp-detection

What it does

GraspSAM predicts where and how a robot gripper should grasp an object by extending the Segment Anything Model (SAM) — a large vision model trained on millions of image segmentation tasks — to also output per-pixel grasp quality, angle, and width. It takes prompts (a point you tap, a bounding box, or text description) and outputs where to grip and what angle to use, without needing task-specific training data. This is grasp-detection — predicting grasp positions and directions on novel objects without expensive per-object labeling.

The method, in detail

The architecture freezes SAM's 2.5-billion-parameter foundation model and adds three small trainable components:

Input pipeline: Given an RGB image and a prompt (point, box, or natural-language text), SAM's image encoder extracts multi-scale visual features using a Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone. The prompt encoder converts the prompt into learnable token embeddings that are fused with image features.

Adapter layers: Instead of retraining the entire encoder, lightweight "Rein adapters" are inserted into each block of the frozen image encoder. Each adapter passes the original feature map through an MLP, then adds the enhanced features back residually to the original features — this keeps the model's general knowledge intact while teaching it task-specific grasp patterns. Only ~1/10 of total parameters are trainable.

Output head and grasp prediction: A modified lightweight decoder predicts four per-pixel outputs: (1) grasp confidence (quality heatmap), (2) gripper angle in radians, (3) gripper width in pixels, and (4) refined object segmentation mask (from which gripper candidates are selected). The full forward pass produces dense grasp maps — allowing the robot to choose among many candidate grasps ranked by confidence.

Training and fine-tuning: The system is trained on two benchmark datasets (Jacquard with 54K images, Grasp-Anything with 1.2K images and multiple object instances) for 50 epochs with batch size 8. The loss combines two terms with weights λ₁=2 and λ₂=1: (1) MSE mask loss for segmentation accuracy per prompt, (2) weighted grasp loss that penalizes foreground errors more than background (using a 10:1 weight ratio based on ground-truth masks). AdamW optimizer with learning rate 1e-5 ensures stable convergence. The method never sees task-specific labels at test time — prompts alone steer the detection.

The key trick is that SAM already generalizes across millions of object types, so minimal fine-tuning with adapters teaches grasp prediction *without* overfitting to training categories. When tested on unseen object types (Grasp-Anything++), performance barely drops, proving the learned grasp patterns transfer.

RL-GSBridge: 3D Gaussian Splatting Based Real2Sim2Real Method for Robotic Manipulation Learning

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Training RL policies in simulation breaks when you move to the real world because simulated images look too fake—pixelated, flat, wrong lighting. The policy learned on fake-looking scenes fails on real cameras.

The approach. Capture the real scene on video, reconstruct it as a photorealistic 3D Gaussian model (not blocky like traditional 3D), and train RL inside that reconstruction. The policy never sees synthetic-looking images, only renderings of the captured reality.

What's unique. 3D Gaussian Splatting renders photorealistically in real time, unlike slow NeRF-based methods. Train once in the captured sim, deploy directly to the real robot with zero fine-tuning—no domain randomization needed.

ICRA 2025 · 6 citations · sim-to-real transfer via photorealistic simulation

What it does

A method that takes video of a real robot scene, converts it into a photorealistic 3D model (using a technique called 3D Gaussian Splatting), trains a reinforcement learning policy to control a robot arm for grasping and pick-and-place tasks entirely inside that replica, and then runs that exact trained policy on the real robot without any fine-tuning. This is data-generation (synthetic demonstration via photorealistic sim) and sim-to-real transfer — it solves the core problem that simulated scenes look too fake for visual RL to work: the policy learns on renderings that match reality pixel-for-pixel.

The method, in detail

Input: Color-and-depth video of a real scene from multiple camera angles (or egocentric robot video).

Step 1: Photorealistic 3D Reconstruction

The pipeline captures multi-view images and depth data from the real scene. COLMAP (structure-from-motion) builds a sparse 3D point cloud and camera poses. MVS (multi-view stereo) densifies that. Then 3D Gaussian Splatting—a neural rendering technique that represents the scene as millions of tiny colored and semi-transparent 3D Gaussian ellipsoids—is optimized to match the photos. Unlike NeRF (which is slow to render), Gaussians render in real time because each tiny blob just needs a dot product and transparency multiplication.

Step 2: Mesh-Based Gaussian Binding

Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting can leave artifacts on unstructured or deformable objects (cloth, cables, loose items). The authors propose a mesh-based variant: they bind the Gaussian ellipsoids to a coarse mesh using soft constraints, anchoring them to mesh vertices but allowing local deformation. This keeps the rendering sharp when objects move or deform slightly, which is critical for a physics simulator that needs stable collision detection.

Step 3: Physics-Rendering Synchronization

The reconstructed 3D model is loaded into PyBullet (a fast physics simulator for robotics). Normally, a physics engine and a renderer are separate—the engine computes where things should be, and the renderer draws them. Here, both systems must stay synchronized: when the RL agent moves the robot arm in PyBullet, the Gaussian Splatting renderer must show the same arm position, lighting changes, shadows, and object deformations. The authors use a "GS editing" approach—real-time adjustments to Gaussian parameters (position, scale, rotation, opacity) as the simulation progresses—so the rendered view stays photorealistic as the scene evolves.

Step 4: Reinforcement Learning Training

A vision-based RL policy (taking RGB or depth input from the simulated renderer) is trained using a standard algorithm (DDPG with behavior cloning baseline). The agent learns to pick up objects or execute pick-and-place tasks. Because the simulated images look nearly identical to reality (not blocky or synthetic), the visual features learned by the policy's neural network carry over to the real robot's cameras with minimal distribution shift.

Step 5: Zero-Shot Deployment

The trained policy is directly executed on the real robot with no retraining, fine-tuning, or domain randomization tricks. The only real-world input is the robot's onboard depth or RGB camera; the policy outputs joint velocities or gripper commands.

The key trick: Instead of relying on hand-crafted domain randomization (randomly rotating, texturing, and placing objects in synthetic scenes), this method captures a single real scene and renders it photorealistically. No huge augmented dataset, no domain-adaptation layer—just one believable sim, trained once, deployed as-is.

Catch It! Learning to Catch in Flight with Mobile Dexterous Hands

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Static grasping is hard enough. Catching a moving object in mid-air with a 20-joint robot (base, arm, dexterous hand) is far harder—you must predict the trajectory, intercept it in time, and close the right fingers at the right moment.

The approach. Break catching into two stages. First, train the robot to track a falling object by moving the base and arm to center it. Second, train the hand to close and intercept. Heavy randomization of throw speed, angle, and object shape ensures the learned policies generalize.

What's unique. Splitting tracking and catching into stages makes the problem tractable. Extensive simulation-based randomization gets the trained policy to work directly on the real robot with no real-world data collection.

What it does

The system teaches a whole-body mobile robot—with a wheeled base, a six-joint arm, and a twelve-joint multi-fingered hand—to catch thrown objects out of the air in real time. This is dexterous-manipulation because it requires precise, coordinated control of the multi-fingered hand while the entire robot body moves to intercept a moving target. Unlike static manipulation, catching demands predicting where an object will be, positioning the arm in time, and grasping with the fingers in a fraction of a second.

The method, in detail

The system starts with a 14-degree-of-freedom mobile manipulator: a wheeled base (2 DoF for forward/turn), a six-joint robotic arm (6 DoF to reach), and a twelve-joint dexterous hand (12 DoF to grasp).

The researchers heavily randomize the training data: they vary the object's throw angle, throwing speed, object shape (cubes, cylinders, complex geometries), and object mass. This randomization ensures the learned policy generalizes to unfamiliar object types and throw patterns that it never saw during training.

The policy is represented as a neural network trained with reinforcement learning (specifically, a PPO-style or actor-critic method). At inference time, the policy takes onboard sensor readings (likely from depth cameras and proprioceptive sensors on the robot) and outputs motor commands for the base wheels, arm joints, and hand fingers.

The real-world deployment uses only onboard sensing and computation—no external cameras or communication—which is critical for a dynamic catching task where latency kills performance.

General-Purpose Clothes Manipulation with Semantic Keypoints

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Cloth is deformable and looks different every time. Training separate models for each clothing type (t-shirts, pants, dresses) and task (fold, hang, flatten) is impractical. The robot needs one system that handles diverse garments.

The approach. Identify semantic points on the garment (left sleeve, hem, neckline) using frozen vision-language models. Pass these keypoints and the task instruction to an LLM to plan sub-steps. Execute pre-built skills (grasp, fold) conditioned on the keypoints. Replan after each action.

What's unique. Semantic keypoints decouple perception (naming parts) from planning (deciding what to do) from control (reaching those parts). New clothing types inherit keypoint names; new tasks need only new LLM prompts; robot skills stay unchanged.

ICRA · 5 citations · bimanual manipulation with semantic keypoints

What it does

CLASP learns to manipulate diverse clothing items (t-shirts, sweaters, skirts, dresses, pants) with a dual-arm robot, handling tasks like folding, flattening, hanging, and placing. Instead of training separate systems per task or clothing type, it identifies named points on the garment—such as "right shoulder," "left cuff," "hem"—and uses those sparse spatial-semantic landmarks to bridge LLM task planning and low-level robot control. This is bimanual manipulation with a semantic intermediate representation: the robot's two arms move to reach the keypoints, guided by a vision-language model that interprets the task instruction.

The method, in detail

The system works as a two-level hierarchy:

Perception (keypoint extraction): The robot observes the garment with an RGB-D camera. A perception module combines three foundation models—OWL-ViT (open-vocabulary object detection), Segment Anything Model (SAM), and SD-DINO (geometric scene understanding)—to extract semantic keypoints from the image. For example, it labels "left sleeve," "right shoulder," "neckline," "hem" by recognizing these anatomical parts even when the garment is crumpled, folded, or partially occluded. These keypoints are 3D coordinates in the robot's frame.

Task planning (LLM): The robot receives a task instruction in natural language, e.g., "fold this shirt." It packages the current RGB-D image, the extracted semantic keypoints, and the instruction into a vision-language model (VLM) prompt. The VLM outputs a sequence of sub-tasks, e.g., [grasp left sleeve, move it to center, grasp right shoulder, fold over]. This step grounds high-level intent into actionable steps.

Action execution (pre-built skills): The robot has a library of pre-built manipulation skills: grasp, move, reposition, fold. Each skill is conditioned on semantic keypoints: "grasp left sleeve" means the robot moves both arms to the 3D positions of the "left sleeve" keypoints, closes the gripper, and executes a learned gripper trajectory. After each action, the robot re-observes, extracts new keypoints, and asks the VLM whether the next sub-task can proceed or if replanning is needed.

Training: The method does not involve training a monolithic end-to-end policy. Instead: (1) Semantic keypoint extraction relies on frozen foundation models (OWL-ViT, SAM, SD-DINO); these are not fine-tuned for clothes but are prompted to recognize clothing parts via prompts like "clothing parts: sleeve, shoulder, …". (2) Manipulation skills (grasp, fold) are either hand-crafted heuristics or learned from a small set of demonstrations per task. (3) The LLM is an off-the-shelf model (GPT-4 or similar) accessed via API; it is not trained on this specific domain but is prompted with examples of CLASP's semantic keypoints and sub-task sequences.

The key trick is the semantic keypoint intermediate layer: it decouples perception (naming parts) from planning (deciding what to do with those parts) from control (moving arms to those parts). This allows the system to generalize: new clothing types inherit keypoint names; new tasks require only new sub-task sequences in the LLM prompt; the robot's grasp and reposition skills remain unchanged.

The synthesis concept (from paper, NOT in code):

Per the paper, the system would:

  • Parse a human demo → detect object-centric subtasks (e.g., "grasp can at pose X", "move can to bin Y").
  • Decompose into segments (object-centric blocks where only one object is manipulated).
  • Perturb object poses (XYZ position, rotation, size, texture).
  • Retarget hand trajectories using IK solvers to adapt gripper positions/finger joints to new object configs.
  • Record in simulation → store states & actions to HDF5.

The repo's generatetrainingconfig.py and playback_datasets.py assume this data already exists and focus on training/evaluation infrastructure.

3. The grasp-prediction head: ConvGraspHeader

From /model/planargraspsam.py, lines 71–114:


class ConvGraspHeader(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, num_layers=3):
        super(ConvGraspHeader, self).__init__()
        
        # Early refinement: 3 Conv2d(4→4) blocks + BatchNorm + ReLU
        self.early_conv = nn.Sequential(
            nn.Conv2d(4, 4, kernel_size=1),
            nn.BatchNorm2d(4), nn.ReLU(),
            # ... x3
        )
        
        # Four separate 1x1 conv heads for grasp outputs
        self.point_predictor = nn.Conv2d(1, 1, kernel_size=1, bias=False)    # confidence/quality
        self.width_predictor = nn.Conv2d(1, 1, kernel_size=1, bias=False)    # gripper width
        self.cos_predictor   = nn.Conv2d(1, 1, kernel_size=1, bias=False)    # cos(angle)
        self.sin_predictor   = nn.Conv2d(1, 1, kernel_size=1, bias=False)    # sin(angle)
        
        self.fusion          = nn.Conv2d(2, 1, kernel_size=1, bias=False)

    def forward(self, x):  # x: [B, 4, 256, 256]
        if len(self.layer) > 0:
            x = self.early_conv(x)  # refine 4-channel input
        
        pos_out   = F.relu(self.point_predictor(x[:,0]))   # [B, 1, 256, 256]
        width_out = F.relu(self.width_predictor(x[:,1]))
        cos_out   = self.cos_predictor(x[:,2])
        sin_out   = self.sin_predictor(x[:,3])
        
        return pos_out, cos_out, sin_out, width_out

Core design: Takes 4-channel input (produced by SamDecoder.predict_grasps()) and outputs four dense prediction maps via separate 1×1 convolutions.

5. The predict_grasps() method: token fusion & decoder

From /model/planargraspsam.py, lines 230–289:


def predict_grasps(
    self,
    image_embeddings,          # [1, 256, 64, 64] from ViT
    image_pe,                  # positional encoding
    sparse_prompt_embeddings,  # from prompt encoder
    dense_prompt_embeddings,   # from prompt encoder
    hq_feature,                # extracted HQ branch features
    grasp_feature              # extracted grasp branch features
):
    # Combine all output tokens: IoU + mask (4) + HQ (1) + grasp (4) = 10 total
    output_tokens = torch.cat([
        self.iou_token.weight,                    # [1, 256]
        self.mask_tokens.weight,                  # [4, 256]
        self.hf_token.weight,                     # [1, 256]
        self.grasp_token.weight                   # [4, 256]
    ], dim=0)
    output_tokens = output_tokens.unsqueeze(0).expand(sparse_prompt_embeddings.size(0), -1, -1)
    
    # Merge with prompt tokens
    tokens = torch.cat((output_tokens, sparse_prompt_embeddings), dim=1)  # [B, ~11, 256]
    
    # Run two-way transformer
    hs, src = self.transformer(src, pos_src, tokens)  # hs: [B, 11, 256]
    
    # Extract token outputs
    iou_token_out = hs[:, 0, :]                       # IoU
    mask_tokens_out = hs[:, 1:(1+self.num_total_tokens), :]  # mask + HQ + grasp
    
    # Upscale and branch
    upscaled_embedding_sam = self.output_upscaling(src)  # SAM branch
    upscaled_embedding_hq = self.embedding_maskfeature(upscaled_embedding_sam) + hq_feature
    upscaled_embedding_grasp = self.embedding_graspfeature(upscaled_embedding_sam) + grasp_feature
    
    # Hypernetwork: each output token → conv kernel for 256×256 feature map
    hyper_in_list = []
    for i in range(self.num_total_tokens):
        if i < 4:
            hyper_in_list.append(self.output_hypernetworks_mlps[i](mask_tokens_out[:, i, :]))
        elif i == 4:
            hyper_in_list.append(self.hf_mlp(mask_tokens_out[:, i, :]))
        elif i > 4:
            hyper_in_list.append(self.grasp_mlp[i-5](mask_tokens_out[:, i, :]))
    
    hyper_in = torch.stack(hyper_in_list, dim=1)  # [B, 10, ...]
    
    # Generate mask maps
    masks_sam = (hyper_in[:,:4] @ upscaled_embedding_sam.view(b, c, h * w)).view(b, -1, h, w)
    masks_ours = (hyper_in[:,4] @ upscaled_embedding_hq.view(b, c, h * w)).view(b, -1, h, w)
    
    # Generate grasp maps (4-channel output)
    grasp_maps = (hyper_in[:,5:] @ upscaled_embedding_grasp.view(b, c, h * w)).view(b, -1, h, w)
    
    # Feed 4-channel grasp maps through ConvGraspHeader
    grasp = self.grasp_header(grasp_maps)  # returns (pos, cos, sin, width)
    
    return masks, iou_pred, grasp

Flow summary:

  • Transformer fuses image embeddings + prompt tokens + grasp tokens
  • Each grasp token → MLP → kernel for hypernetwork
  • Hypernetwork produces 4-channel grasp feature map
  • ConvGraspHeader refines it → (quality, angle-cos, angle-sin, width)

def compute_loss(self, grasp_pred, mask_pred, target, g_s, m_s, p, c, s, w):
    # Unpack 4-channel grasp prediction
    pos_pred, cos_pred, sin_pred, width_pred = grasp_pred
    
    # Unpack ground-truth grasps (also 4-channel per dataset)
    pos, cos, sin, width = grasps_gt
    
    # All interpolated to 256×256
    pos   = F.interpolate(pos, size=(256, 256), mode="bilinear").squeeze(1)    # [B, 256, 256]
    cos   = F.interpolate(cos, size=(256, 256), mode="bilinear").squeeze(1)    # angle cos
    sin   = F.interpolate(sin, size=(256, 256), mode="bilinear").squeeze(1)    # angle sin
    width = F.interpolate(width, size=(256, 256), mode="bilinear").squeeze(1)  # gripper width
    
    # Weighted MSE loss (foreground 10× more than background)
    p_loss = self.weighted_mse(pos_pred, pos, masks_gt, weight=0.01)      # pos loss (1.0 weight)
    cos_loss = self.weighted_mse(cos_pred, cos, masks_gt, weight=0.01)    # cos loss (1.0 weight)
    sin_loss = self.weighted_mse(sin_pred, sin, masks_gt, weight=0.01)    # sin loss (1.0 weight)
    width_loss = self.weighted_mse(width_pred, width, masks_gt, weight=0.01)
    
    # Total grasp loss: weighted sum of components
    grasp_loss = p*p_loss + c*cos_loss + s*sin_loss + w*width_loss  # p,c,s,w = weights
    
    # Mask loss (binary cross-entropy + DICE)
    d_loss = dice_loss(mask_pred, masks_gt)
    bce_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(mask_pred, masks_gt)
    mask_loss = d_loss + bce_loss
    
    # Total loss combines grasp + mask (g_s, m_s = scale weights)
    total_loss = g_s*grasp_loss + m_s*mask_loss

Output structure: Four per-pixel prediction maps (all 256×256):

  • pos — grasp quality/confidence heatmap (ReLU-activated); higher = more confident grasp
  • cos, sin — angle encoded as cosine & sine of doubled angle (avoids angle discontinuity at 180°)

- Recovered angle: atan2(sin, cos) / 2.0

  • width — predicted gripper opening width in pixels

Summary: Key design choices

  • Frozen SAM + trainable wrapper: SAM's massive pre-training is preserved; only ~1/10 of parameters are trainable (adapters + new tokens + grasp head)
  • Dual-path decoder: Mask branch (from SAM) + HQ branch (added) + grasp branch (added) allow independent supervision of segmentation vs. grasp prediction
  • Angle encoding: Grasp angle is encoded as (cos, sin) of 2×angle to avoid discontinuity; recovered via atan2(sin, cos) / 2
  • Dense grasp maps: All outputs are 256×256 per-pixel predictions, upsampled to 1024×1024 for evaluation; allows candidate selection via peak detection
  • Prompt flexibility: Point/box prompts are randomly selected during training; at test time, the user can provide either (or both)
  • Weighted loss: Foreground pixels (object) weighted 10× higher than background to focus grasp prediction on actual objects
