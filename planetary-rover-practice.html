Planetary rovers — the real problem

What driving on Mars actually demands, stage by stage — the missions that lived it, the rovers lost to it, how JPL solves it, and what our clean demo leaves out.

← the planetary rover demo · the build lab · home
Each stage below pairs our measured demo with the real practical problem behind it: who actually hits it (the industry or system), what it costs when it fails (real failure modes and incidents), how it is solved in production today, and the honest gap between our clean sim and the real thing. The demo captures the essence; this is the world it lives in.
1

Trust the ground, not the wheels — visual odometry

Our demo: at 30% slip, wheel odometry over-reports 143 m on a true 100 m traverse; visual odometry reads the truth.

Who actually hits it

NASA's Mars Exploration Rovers (Spirit and Opportunity) pioneered onboard visual odometry; Curiosity, Perseverance and China's Zhurong all rely on it.

What it costs when it fails

On slopes Spirit measured wheel slip up to ~100% — wheels turning, rover not moving. Wheel-only odometry would believe it had driven metres it never did, losing localisation and driving blind toward hazards.

How it's solved in production

Visual odometry: tracking terrain features between stereo image pairs to measure true motion, plus slip checks that abort a drive when slip exceeds a threshold.

Toy demo → real system

VO is compute-heavy — the MER rovers could only afford it in short steps ('blind drive, then VO'). Regolith texture varies, and featureless terrain defeats it. Ours is a fixed slip ratio.

2

Drive without a joystick — onboard autonomy

Our demo: autonomy covers ~24× the distance per sol that pure teleoperation does (36 → 864 m).

Who actually hits it

MER's GESTALT/AutoNav; Curiosity and Perseverance's AutoNav — Perseverance can even navigate while driving ('thinking while driving').

What it costs when it fails

Mars is 4–24 minutes away one-way at light speed, so a joystick is impossible. Human-sequenced 'directed drives' cover far less ground per sol; every metre of autonomy buys more science before the mission ends.

How it's solved in production

Onboard stereo terrain assessment and local path planning, with a full sol's plan uplinked once per day and executed autonomously.

Toy demo → real system

Real autonomy is bounded by a ~200 MHz radiation-hard CPU, the power budget, and deep risk-aversion — a stuck rover is unrecoverable. Ours is a distance-per-sol ratio.

3

Read the terrain — traversability

Our demo: a traversability-aware path takes 0 hazards where a straight line crosses 2, for modest extra distance.

Who actually hits it

Every Mars rover grades terrain by slope, roughness and step height from stereo (GESTALT/AutoNav).

What it costs when it fails

Sharp embedded rocks punctured Curiosity's aluminium wheels in 2013–14, forcing new routes and a software fix; an untraversable slope can flip or strand a rover for good.

How it's solved in production

Traversability maps from stereo, keep-out zones, and — on Curiosity — a traction-control algorithm that modulates individual wheel speeds to cut the load sharp rocks put on the wheels.

Toy demo → real system

Real hazards include benign-looking ripples that trap a rover (Spirit) and rocks below stereo resolution. Ours is a grid with labelled cells.

4

Budget the sun — energy management

Our demo: a naive continuous drive strands (battery → 0); a power-aware schedule survives and travels far further.

Who actually hits it

Solar rovers and landers live and die by the energy budget — Sojourner, the MERs, Zhurong, InSight. Opportunity was killed by a 2018 global dust storm; Spirit could not reach a Sun-facing winter slope and froze.

What it costs when it fails

Run the battery flat in shadow or overnight and the heaters stop — the electronics freeze and the mission ends. Energy, not distance, is often the true limiting resource.

How it's solved in production

Strict per-sol energy budgeting, driving only within the solar-plus-battery envelope, and parking tilted toward the Sun for winter survival; Curiosity and Perseverance use RTGs to remove the constraint entirely.

Toy demo → real system

Real budgets fold in dust accumulation on the panels, seasonal Sun angle, heater loads and instrument power. Ours is a simple drain-versus-solar sum.

5

Don't get stuck — sinkage and embedding

Our demo: an aggressive drive embeds at ~88% slip; a slip-monitored drive stays mobile and backs out.

Who actually hits it

Spirit famously embedded in soft, sulfate-rich soil at 'Troy' in 2009 and never escaped, ending the mission in 2010; Opportunity's 'Purgatory Dune' (2005) took weeks to climb out of.

What it costs when it fails

On Mars, embedding is usually terminal — there is no tow truck. It is the failure mode that actually ended a flagship mission.

How it's solved in production

Onboard slip and sinkage checks that halt a drive the moment slip spikes, conservative avoidance of loose sand, and visual odometry to detect that the rover is making no progress.

Toy demo → real system

Soil bearing strength is invisible until the wheels are already in it — the core reason Spirit was lost. Ours is a slip threshold on a modelled sand trap.
