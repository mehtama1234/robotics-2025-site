01 · the master recipe

Almost everything is minimizing a loss

Every method in this field starts with the same question: what is wrong right now, and which direction would make it less wrong? The answer is a loss function — a number that goes to zero when the model does exactly what you want. The loss is your whole specification of success, which is why choosing it is the hardest design decision in the room.

Once you have a loss, the path forward is the negative gradient: compute which way each parameter should shift to reduce the loss, then take a small step there. Backpropagation makes this tractable by working the chain rule backwards through the computation, so every parameter in a deep network gets its share of the blame for the error in a single pass. Local minima are real — gradient descent has no guarantee of finding the global best — but in practice, high-dimensional problems have so many escape routes that it works far more reliably than theory suggests.

the actual math — one level deeper

θ ← θ − η∇L(θ) — gradient descent; η is the learning rate.

Chain rule: ∂L/∂θ = (∂L/∂z) · (∂z/∂θ) — backpropagation applies this across every layer in the computation graph.

02 · under uncertainty

Update belief with evidence

Most interesting questions in robotics are not deterministic: a sensor is noisy, the world state is partly hidden, and two explanations for the same reading might both be plausible. The right answer is not to pick one guess and commit; it is to hold a distribution over possibilities and update it as evidence arrives. That update rule is Bayes'.

The prior encodes what you believed before the measurement. The likelihood says how probable the measurement would be under each possible state. Multiply them and you get the posterior — a distribution that is now sharper, more confident, wherever the evidence points. The constant you need to normalize the result is often the hardest part, which is why so much of the field is really about tractable approximations: Kalman filters for linear Gaussians, particle filters for anything else, variational inference for neural models.

the actual math — one level deeper

p(x|z) ∝ p(z|x) · p(x) — posterior proportional to likelihood times prior.

p(x|z) = p(z|x) p(x) / p(z); p(z) = ∫ p(z|x) p(x) dx is the normalizing constant (the evidence).

03 · the language of space

Vectors, transforms, and their axes

Space is where robotics lives, and the language for space is linear algebra. A 3D point is a vector; a rigid body motion — the move of a camera, the turn of a joint — is a matrix multiplication. Two cameras looking at the same scene are connected by a rotation R and a translation t: one coordinate frame expressed in terms of another. This small vocabulary — vectors, matrices, inner products — is what almost every perception algorithm is doing underneath.

When a system of equations is overdetermined or noisy, the singular value decomposition (SVD) answers 'what is the closest solution?' by breaking a matrix into UΣV⊃ₛ — two rotation matrices and a diagonal of scales. The singular values along the diagonal reveal how much information the data carries along each axis, which is also the foundation of PCA: project onto the axes that carry the most variance and you have compressed without losing what matters.

the actual math — one level deeper

Rigid transform: x' = Rx + t, R⊃ₛR = I (rotation), t ∈ ℝ³ (translation).

SVD: A = UΣV⊃ₛ; columns of U are output directions, V are input directions, Σ diag entries are the singular values (scaling factors).

04 · mixing by relevance

Similarity becomes a weighted average

The core operation in modern perception and language models is deceptively simple: let each element ask 'who should I look at?' via a query, let every candidate answer 'here is what I offer' via a key, and score the match with a dot product. Run those scores through a softmax so they form a probability distribution, then use the distribution to take a weighted average of the values. The result is a new representation of each element that has mixed in exactly as much of every other element as the similarity dictated.

The √d scaling factor in the denominator prevents the dot products from becoming so large that the softmax saturates and pushes all weight onto one element. What makes attention powerful is that it learns the queries, keys, and values simultaneously from data, so the 'relevance' it computes is task-specific: in a vision model, spatially nearby patches may not be the most relevant; in a manipulation policy, the hand and the object are.

the actual math — one level deeper

Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QK⊃ₛ/√dᵏ)V — dᵏ is the key dimension; scaling prevents saturation.

Multi-head: concatenate h independent heads, each with its own Q,K,V projections, then project back — h times the expressivity.

05 · sampling the unwritable

Follow the gradient of likelihood

A diffusion model never writes down the probability of an image. Instead it learns the score: the gradient of the log-probability at any given point. If you know which direction is 'uphill' in probability space, you can start anywhere — even pure noise — and follow those uphill steps. That is the reverse diffusion process: a sequence of small denoising steps, each using the estimated score to move toward more probable territory.

Score-based methods are powerful because the score is tractable to estimate via a denoising objective even when the distribution itself is not. Flow matching is the straight-line cousin: instead of the curved paths of a diffusion SDE, it learns a vector field that transports noise directly to data along straight trajectories, which makes training simpler and sampling faster. Both share the insight that you do not need to write down p(x) — you only need to know its gradient.

the actual math — one level deeper

Score: sθ(x,t) ≈ ∇ᵒ log pᵗ(x) — estimated by training a network to denoise x corrupted with Gaussian noise.

DDPM objective: E[‖ε − εθ(√αᵗ x₀ + √(1−αᵗ)ε, t)‖²] — predict the noise added at step t.

01

Gradient descent & backpropagation

both
the problem

You have a network with millions of parameters and a dataset of examples. The network is wrong on almost all of them. You need a way to move every parameter, simultaneously, in the direction that reduces the error — and you need to do it efficiently, not one parameter at a time.

the approaches

The key insight is that the error is a function of all the parameters, and a function has a gradient. Follow the negative gradient — decrease the loss — and the parameters improve. The chain rule lets you compute that gradient for every parameter in one backward pass through the computation graph, regardless of depth.

Modern variants (Adam, AdaGrad) keep a running estimate of how the gradient has been changing, adapting the step size per parameter. The practical consequence is that almost any differentiable computation — a robot policy, a 3D splat, a generative model — can be trained end-to-end without any hand-derived derivatives.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Model: two-layer network, 4 inputs → 8 hidden → 1 output; 41 parameters total.
  2. Forward pass: h = ReLU(W₁x + b₁); ŷ = W₂h + b₂; L = (ŷ − y)² / 2.
  3. Backward pass: ∂L/∂W₂ = (ŷ − y) · h⊃ₛ — one outer product, computed in microseconds.
  4. Chain: ∂L/∂W₁ = [(ŷ − y) W₂] ⊙ 1[h > 0] · x⊃ₛ — the ReLU gate zeros out half the gradients at each step.
  5. Step: with η = 0.01, W₁ ← W₁ − 0.01 · ∂L/∂W₁; loss drops from 2.34 to 2.17 in one step.
  6. After 1,000 steps: loss reaches 0.04; the same loop, repeated, is how every network in this field is trained.

    Diffusion policies generate robot action sequences by denoising, but action generation depends on multimodal conditioning: images, proprioceptive state, task descriptions. How do you fuse these into the diffusion process?     Second-Order Stein Variational Dynamic Optimization — uses second-order gradient information inside a particle-based trajectory optimizer

    Trajectory optimization with non-convex safety constraints (like a CBF requiring h(x) ≥ 0 along the trajectory) is hard: first-order gradients get stuck in local minima, and the non-smooth constraint (the max function in CBFs) breaks differentiability. Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD) uses particle methods to solve this: represent the trajectory as a particle, apply a second-order gradient update that repels particles away from bad trajectories, and the particles discover good solutions collectively.

    how it actually works

    Particle representation: Instead of optimizing a single trajectory τ, optimize an ensemble of particles {τ_1, ..., τ_N}, where each particle is a trajectory [x_0, u_0, x_1, u_1, ...].

    SVGD update: Each particle moves according to ∇ log p(τ) + ∇·K(τ, ·), where the first term is the gradient of the objective (tracking error, constraints), and the second is a kernel-based repulsion that keeps particles diverse.

    Second-order variant: Use the Hessian of the objective, not just the gradient, so particles respond to curvature. Regions of steep cost get stronger gradients; regions of gentle cost get weaker updates. This accelerates convergence.

    Non-convex safety: The CBF constraint h(x_t) ≥ 0 is incorporated as a penalty: if any h(x_t) < 0, the trajectory's log-probability is killed (or heavily penalized). Particles naturally avoid infeasible regions without explicitly handling the non-smoothness.

    Result: Particles converge to good feasible trajectories; the ensemble explores multiple solutions, and the mean or best particle is executed.

Least squares & bundle adjustment

robotics
the problem

You have hundreds of noisy measurements — landmark positions from a camera, range readings from a LiDAR — and you want the single set of parameters that best explains all of them at once. Any one measurement is wrong; their collective shape is not.

the approaches

Least squares is the answer when you have more equations than unknowns and noise in every one. Minimize the sum of squared residuals and you get the maximum-likelihood estimate under Gaussian noise. The Gauss-Newton method makes this tractable for nonlinear problems: linearize around the current estimate, solve the linear system, repeat.

Bundle adjustment is the full version: simultaneously optimize every camera pose and every 3D point so that reprojection errors across all views are minimized together. It is the core of every modern SLAM system and photogrammetric pipeline. Huber and Cauchy kernels downweight outlier measurements so a single bad correspondence cannot corrupt the whole solution.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Setup: 3 cameras observe 1 point P. Each camera gives a 2D observation uᵢ. We want to find P that minimizes Σ ‖uᵢ − π(RᵢP + tᵢ)‖².
  2. Linearize: at current estimate P&sup0;, compute Jacobian Jᵢ = ∂π/∂P ∈ ℝ²×³ for each camera.
  3. Form normal equations: (J⊃ₛJ) ΔP = J⊃ₛr, where r = [u₁−π(P&sup0;); u₂−π(P&sup0;); u₃−π(P&sup0;)].
  4. Solve: ΔP = (J⊃ₛJ)⊃⁻¹ J⊃ₛr in 3 unknowns; update P ← P + ΔP.
  5. Iterate: reprojection error drops from 4.2 px to 0.3 px after 5 Gauss-Newton steps.

Probability & Bayes' rule

both
the problem

A robot arm reaches for a cup, but the grasp pose is uncertain — the camera estimate has noise and the cup might have slipped. You cannot act on a single guess; you need to reason over the space of plausible states and make a decision that is good across all of them.

the approaches

Bayes' rule is the only logically consistent way to update a belief with evidence. You start with a prior over what might be true. A sensor gives you a likelihood — how probable is this reading under each possible state? Multiply and normalize: that is the posterior, which is your updated belief. Every subsequent sensor reading is the next Bayesian update.

In practice the prior and likelihood are often Gaussian, which makes the posterior Gaussian too and keeps everything closed-form. When the distributions are richer — multi-modal, heavy-tailed — you need approximations: variational inference, importance sampling, or particle filters. The recurring tension is between a prior that is too narrow (missing the truth) and one so wide it learns nothing.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Prior: object position x ~ N(0.3 m, 0.05²) — centered at the last known location, 5 cm std.
  2. Sensor: depth camera returns z = 0.28 m with noise σᵏ = 0.02 m; likelihood p(z|x) = N(z; x, 0.02²).
  3. Posterior: p(x|z) = N(x; μ*, σ*²) where 1/σ*² = 1/0.05² + 1/0.02² = 400 + 2500 = 2900.
  4. Posterior mean: μ* = σ*² (0.3/0.05² + 0.28/0.02²) = (1/2900)(120 + 700) ≈ 0.283 m.
  5. Posterior std: σ* = 1/√2900 ≈ 0.019 m — tighter than either prior or sensor alone.

Bayesian filtering (Kalman & particle)

robotics
the problem

A robot's camera gives noisy pose readings at 30 Hz, but the robot's true position evolves continuously between frames. At any moment, you need the best estimate of where the robot is now — not just what the last sensor said.

the approaches

A Bayesian filter is the recursive application of Bayes' rule in time. At each step it does two things: predict — use the motion model to advance the current belief into the future — and correct — use the new sensor reading to update that predicted belief. The Kalman filter is the closed-form version for linear systems with Gaussian noise; everything stays Gaussian, so the predict-correct cycle is just matrix arithmetic.

For nonlinear or non-Gaussian problems, the particle filter approximates the distribution with a cloud of weighted samples (particles). Prediction moves each particle forward through the motion model; correction reweights them by how well each one explains the sensor reading. Periodically resample to keep the effective particle count high. The cost is that you need many particles in high-dimensional spaces.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. State: x = [position, velocity] ∈ ℝ²; prior x&sup0; ~ N([1.0, 0.0], I).
  2. Dynamics: xᵗ₊₁ = F xᵗ + w, F = [[1, 0.1],[0, 1]], w ~ N(0, Q), Q = diag(0.01, 0.1).
  3. Predict: μ̄ = Fμᵗ; Σ̄ = FΣᵗF⊃ₛ + Q.
  4. Measurement: z = [1, 0] x + v, v ~ N(0, 0.04); H = [[1, 0]].
  5. Correct: K = Σ̄H⊃ₛ(HΣ̄H⊃ₛ + R)⊃⁻¹ (Kalman gain); μᵗ₊₁ = μ̄ + K(z − Hμ̄); Σᵗ₊₁ = (I − KH)Σ̄.

Attention & the softmax kernel

vision
the problem

Every patch in an image, or every token in a sequence, might be relevant to every other — but there is no fixed spatial structure that tells you which ones to connect. You need a way to let the data decide its own connectivity.

the approaches

Attention is a soft, learned routing mechanism: each element broadcasts a query ('what am I looking for?') and a key ('what do I provide?'). The dot product of a query with every key is a similarity score; the softmax turns scores into weights; the weighted sum of values is the output. The routing is data-dependent — different inputs produce different attention patterns — and the whole operation is differentiable.

In vision, this lets a model connect semantically related patches regardless of distance, which beats convolutions for long-range dependencies. In robot policies, it lets the model attend jointly to image features and proprioceptive state. The computational cost is quadratic in sequence length, which is why the field invests heavily in sparse and linear approximations to attention.

  3. Scores: A = QK⊃ₛ/√4 = XX⊃ₛ/2 = I/2 — orthogonal patches attend only to themselves.

the problem

the problem

the approaches

Optimal transport & matching

the problem

You have a set of predicted object positions and a set of ground-truth positions, and you need to pair them optimally — minimizing the total distance between matched pairs. No label tells you which prediction corresponds to which ground truth.

the approaches

Optimal transport asks: given two distributions (or sets of points), what is the cheapest way to move mass from one to the other? The earth-mover distance (Wasserstein distance) is the answer, and the Sinkhorn algorithm makes it differentiable and GPU-efficient by adding an entropy regularizer to the transport plan. This turns matching from a combinatorial problem into a continuous one you can differentiate through.

In detection and tracking, Hungarian matching assigns each prediction to a ground truth by minimizing total assignment cost — a discrete version of the same idea. In point-cloud registration, soft assignment via Sinkhorn finds correspondences between two scans. The recurring pattern is that you have two sets and need to align them, and optimal transport gives you a principled, learnable way to do it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Setup: 3 predicted bounding boxes P = {p₁, p₂, p₃} and 3 ground-truth boxes G = {g₁, g₂, g₃}.
  2. Cost matrix: Cᵢᵤ = 1 − IoU(pᵢ, gᵤ); C = [[0.1, 0.8, 0.9],[0.9, 0.2, 0.7],[0.8, 0.7, 0.3]].
  3. Hungarian algorithm: optimal assignment: p₁→g₁, p₂→g₂, p₃→g₃; total cost = 0.1 + 0.2 + 0.3 = 0.6.
  4. Sinkhorn: add entropy λ=0.1; iterate row and column normalization of exp(−C/λ) to get a soft assignment matrix.
  5. Why soft matters: gradients flow through the soft assignment; hard Hungarian matching is not differentiable.

MDPs, Bellman & policy gradients

both
the problem

A robot arm must pick up a block in ten steps. The reward is only given at the end. Which of the ten actions actually mattered, and how much should each one be credited for the eventual success?

the approaches

A Markov decision process (MDP) is the formal setting: states, actions, a transition model, a reward, and a discount factor. The value function V(s) answers 'how much total reward can I expect from state s, following the best policy?' The Bellman equation says V(s) = maxᵃ [r(s,a) + γ Σᵣ' P(s'|s,a) V(s')] — value now equals reward now plus discounted value next. Dynamic programming solves this exactly for small discrete problems; deep networks approximate it for the continuous case.

Policy gradient methods take the other route: parameterize the policy directly and climb the gradient of expected return. REINFORCE estimates the gradient via sampled rollouts; actor-critic methods reduce variance by using a learned value function as a baseline. GRPO, which has driven recent VLM fine-tuning, is a group-relative variant that normalizes rewards within a batch, cutting the variance that makes pure policy gradient training unstable.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. MDP: 5 states, 2 actions; discount γ = 0.9; reward r(s=5, any) = 1.0, else 0.
  2. Value iteration, step 0: V = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0].
  3. Step 1: V(4) = maxᵃ [0 + 0.9 · P(5|4,a) · 1.0] = 0.9 · 1.0 = 0.90.
  4. Step 2: V(3) = 0.9 · V(4) = 0.81; V(2) = 0.9 · 0.81 = 0.729.
  5. Converged: V = [0.590, 0.656, 0.729, 0.810, 0.900] — earlier states correctly valued lower because reward is discounted.
  6. Policy: π(s) = argmaxᵃ [r + γ Σ P V]; the optimal policy always moves toward state 5 in one step.

This lets a risk-sensitive agent choose actions that minimize downside risk (low-return tail) even if the mean return is the same — crucial for robotics, where a rare catastrophe is worse than missing an opportunity.

    how it actually works

    Standard Bellman update: V(s) = E_a[r(s,a) + γV(s')] — one number per state, the expected return.

    Distributional variant: Z(s) = the full distribution over possible returns. Decompose it as (e.g.) quantiles [q_0.1, q_0.5, q_0.9], the 10th, 50th, 90th percentile returns.

    Transformer decoder: A Transformer processes the offline trajectory history (states, actions, returns) and outputs quantile values for each state-action pair, not just the mean. The loss is the Huber loss over quantiles: each predicted quantile is trained to match the empirical quantile from the Bellman backup.

    Risk-sensitive selection: At test time, the policy can choose actions that keep the low quantile (q_0.1) high — safe actions. Standard average-reward methods would pick high-mean actions that might have high crash risk.

    Dr. Seg: Revisiting GRPO Training for Visual LLMs — group-relative policy optimization with perception-grounded reward for visual reasoning

    Visual reasoning in large language models is noisy: the model sees an image and must answer a question, but reward signals from correctness are sparse and high-variance. Group-relative policy optimization (GRPO) is a fix: instead of comparing each sample's reward to an absolute baseline, compare it to the mean reward within its batch, so the gradient signal is relative stability. Paired with perception-grounded rewards (reward that comes from grounding reasoning steps in the image, not just final answers), GRPO dramatically cuts training instability.

    how it actually works

    Standard policy gradient: Gradient = (return - baseline) × log π(a|s). Large variance makes learning slow.

    Group-relative variant: Divide the batch into groups (e.g., 8 samples per group). For each sample, compute its advantage as (return - mean_return_in_group). Use that relative advantage instead of the absolute baseline.

    Why it works: High-variance rewards are still high-variance, but the relative signal is lower-variance. If all 8 samples in a group got equally bad rewards due to bad luck, their relative advantages are all near zero, and you don't update. If one is good and 7 are bad, the good one's advantage stands out.

    Perception grounding: Intermediate steps in the reasoning ('the object is a mug') are graded against image features, not just the final answer. This gives dense, low-noise supervision throughout the trajectory, stabilizing learning.

    Multi-Agent Inverse Q-Learning from Demonstrations — recovers individual reward functions from joint multi-agent demonstrations via Q-learning

    In multi-agent imitation learning, inferring individual agent rewards from joint demonstrations is ambiguous: a shared action might come from agent A's preference or agent B's, or a coordination between them. Inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) alone cannot disentangle this. The solution is to frame it as recovering individual Q-functions from the joint observed policy — each agent has its own value function, but they act together. Solving multiple Q-learners jointly (not independently) captures coordination.

    how it actually works

    Setup: Observe a multi-agent trajectory (state, joint action, next state, reward). Each agent has a reward function r_i and Q-function Q_i(s, a_i).

    Joint Bellman: For agent i, Q_i(s, a_i) = E[r_i(s, a_i) + γ Q_i(s', argmax_a_i' Q_i(s', a_i'))]. But the argmax is over agent i's actions alone, while the environment transition depends on all agents' actions.

    Inverse step: Given the demonstrations, infer Q-functions by solving a convex program: minimize ||Q_observed - Q_Bellman||^2 subject to constraints that the joint observed actions look like they came from agents maximizing their own Q-values (not conflicting objectives).

    Separation: The convex program implicitly decomposes the joint reward into individual rewards, so each agent's learned reward reflects only its own preferences, not the coordination.

    Joint learning: The value functions are trained on multi-agent rollouts where the full coverage is known (ground truth), but each agent only sees its own FOV during execution. This trains them to infer coverage via communication.

Stability & safety: Lyapunov, MPC, CBFs

robotics
the problem

A learned locomotion policy passes all offline tests but occasionally falls on the real robot — sometimes in ways that could cause damage. You need a guarantee, not just statistics: the system must stay in a safe region at every step, not just on average.

the approaches

A Lyapunov function V(x) is an 'energy' that is always positive and always decreasing along the system's trajectories — if such a function exists, you know the system is stable and will converge. Control barrier functions (CBFs) extend this: define a set where the robot is safe, and require that the control keeps the trajectory inside it at every step. The CBF constraint is added to an optimization problem (a quadratic program) that modifies the desired control as little as possible while guaranteeing the boundary is never crossed.

Model predictive control (MPC) takes the receding-horizon view: at each time step, solve an optimization over the next N steps, apply the first action, then re-solve. Safety constraints can be added directly to the optimization. The challenge is that both CBF-based and MPC-based safety guarantees assume you know the dynamics; model uncertainty — which is the rule, not the exception — requires either uncertainty-aware constraint tightening or conservative formulations that widen the safe set by the estimated error.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. State: x = [position, velocity]; safe set: S = {x : h(x) = 1.5 − |position| ≥ 0} — stay within 1.5 m of origin.
  2. CBF condition: dh/dt + αh(x) ≥ 0 must hold; α = 1.0.
  3. Desired control: ŭ = −2.0 (move left) — would push the robot toward the boundary at x = −1.2.
  4. QP: min ‖u − ŭ‖² s.t. (dh/dx) f(x,u) + h(x) ≥ 0.
  5. Solution: u* = −0.3; the QP nudges the control to maintain h(x) ≥ 0 without stopping the robot entirely.
  6. Result: over 500 trials with random disturbances, zero safety violations vs 14% with the unconstrained policy.

    A Control Barrier Function for Safe Navigation with Online Gaussian Splatting Maps — real-time CBF-based safety filter built on a live Gaussian Splatting map

    Real-time safety in learned navigation is hard: the policy network is fast but opaque, and the environment is dynamic (obstacles appear, move). A control barrier function (CBF) is a safety filter: a small quadratic program modifies the policy's action to keep a safety constraint satisfied at every step. Paired with an online Gaussian Splatting map (which represents the environment in real time), the safety set is updated live, and the robot never enters unsafe regions.

    how it actually works

    Safety set definition: h(x) ≥ 0 defines the safe region (e.g., distance to nearest obstacle ≥ collision_radius). h(x) = distance - collision_radius.

    CBF constraint: dh/dt + αh(x) ≥ 0 must hold for all time. This is the barrier: if h(x) is positive (safe), the constraint ensures it stays positive (dh/dt must be at least -αh(x), a slow decay at worst).

    QP formulation: Given desired action u_desired from the policy, solve min ||u - u_desired||^2 subject to (∇h)^T f(x,u) + αh(x) ≥ 0. This minimally modifies the action to satisfy the barrier.

    Online map: A Gaussian Splatting representation of the scene is updated with each new sensor reading. The splats encode occupied space; the h(x) function queries the splats to compute distance. As the map changes, the safe set shrinks or expands, and the QP adapts on the fly.

    Real-Time Whole-Body Control of Legged Robots with MPPI — model predictive path integral control for whole-body legged locomotion at real-time rates

    Whole-body locomotion control requires coordinating joint angles, body orientation, and ground contact. Model predictive control (MPC) is the standard solution, but solving an optimization over a long horizon is slow. Model predictive path integral (MPPI) control sidesteps the optimization: instead of gradient-based optimization, sample many trajectories, weight them by how good they are, and average. This is fast enough for real-time control of legged robots.

    how it actually works

    Problem: Minimize J = E_τ[∫ c(x_t, u_t) dt] where x is state (joint angles, body pose), u is actions, c is cost (tracking error, energy, etc.).

    Path integral trick: Rewrite the problem as Z = ∫ exp(-J/λ) Dτ, where Z is a partition function. Sampling from the exp(-J/λ) distribution and averaging the samples solves the control problem.

    Algorithm: For each step, sample N trajectory rollouts (u_1, ..., u_N) from the learned policy. Simulate each forward. Compute cost J_i for each. Weight each by exp(-J_i/λ). Average the weighted trajectories to get the best action: u* = Σ w_i u_i where w_i ∝ exp(-J_i/λ).

    Real-time feasible: This is embarrassingly parallel — simulate rollouts on GPU in parallel, compute costs, weight and average. No gradient computation, no matrix inversion. Typical: 64-256 parallel rollouts, 10 ms horizon, runs at 100 Hz.

    Uncertainty-Aware Input-Constrained Control Barrier Functions — tightens CBF constraints proportional to model uncertainty for provably safe operation

    A learned or poorly-identified system model has uncertainty: the true dynamics might deviate from your estimate. Naïve CBF constraints based on a point estimate of the model can fail — the real system might violate the barrier. The solution is to tighten the safety constraint by the estimated model uncertainty, so even in the worst case (the model is maximally wrong), the constraint holds.

    how it actually works

    Model: dx/dt = f(x,u) ± δf, where f is the nominal model and δf is bounded uncertainty (e.g., ||δf|| ≤ ε(x)).

    Worst-case barrier: Instead of h(x) ≥ 0, require h(x) ≥ ε(x) — move the safety boundary inward by the uncertainty magnitude.

    Barrier time derivative: dh/dt = (∇h)^T f(x,u) + (∇h)^T δf. In the worst case (δf points to minimize dh/dt), compute dh/dt_min and require dh/dt_min + αh ≥ 0.

    QP with uncertainty: Solve min ||u - u_desired||^2 subject to min_{δf: ||δf||≤ε} [(∇h)^T (f + δf)] + α h(x) ≥ 0. The inner minimization is a constraint on the nominal control: the control must work even if the model is maximally wrong.

    Result: Tighter (more conservative) constraints, but provably safe under model uncertainty.

    Second-Order Stein Variational Dynamic Optimization — particle-based trajectory optimizer with second-order updates that handles non-convex safety constraints

    Trajectory optimization with non-convex safety constraints (like a CBF requiring h(x) ≥ 0 along the trajectory) is hard: first-order gradients get stuck in local minima, and the non-smooth constraint (the max function in CBFs) breaks differentiability. Stein variational gradient descent (SVGD) uses particle methods to solve this: represent the trajectory as a particle, apply a second-order gradient update that repels particles away from bad trajectories, and the particles discover good solutions collectively.

    how it actually works

    Particle representation: Instead of optimizing a single trajectory τ, optimize an ensemble of particles {τ_1, ..., τ_N}, where each particle is a trajectory [x_0, u_0, x_1, u_1, ...].

    SVGD update: Each particle moves according to ∇ log p(τ) + ∇·K(τ, ·), where the first term is the gradient of the objective (tracking error, constraints), and the second is a kernel-based repulsion that keeps particles diverse.

    Second-order variant: Use the Hessian of the objective, not just the gradient, so particles respond to curvature. Regions of steep cost get stronger gradients; regions of gentle cost get weaker updates. This accelerates convergence.

    Non-convex safety: The CBF constraint h(x_t) ≥ 0 is incorporated as a penalty: if any h(x_t) < 0, the trajectory's log-probability is killed (or heavily penalized). Particles naturally avoid infeasible regions without explicitly handling the non-smoothness.

    Result: Particles converge to good feasible trajectories; the ensemble explores multiple solutions, and the mean or best particle is executed.

Contact & complementarity

robotics
the problem

A gripper closes on an object. When contact forms, forces appear instantly; before contact, they are exactly zero. This on-off switch is not differentiable — gradients cannot flow through it, and standard physics simulators stall here.

the approaches

Contact physics is governed by complementarity conditions: the contact force is non-negative, the gap between surfaces is non-negative, and their product is zero — either there is contact (zero gap) or there is no force, but never both non-zero at once. This is a linear complementarity problem (LCP). Soft contact models relax the complementarity constraint, allowing a small penetration in exchange for smooth, differentiable forces — which is the approach most learned-dynamics and differentiable-simulation work takes.

Convex relaxations of the non-smooth dynamics enable optimization-based planning through contact sequences: the complementarity constraints become convex set constraints, and a convex program can find contact modes and forces together. The challenge is that the relaxation is not tight — the actual contact dynamics may not be achievable by the relaxed solution, and the gap grows with the complexity of the contact sequence.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Setup: a finger pushes a block; gap = d(finger, block); normal force = f ≥ 0.
  2. Complementarity: 0 ≤ d ⊥ f ≥ 0 — either d=0 (contact, f can be nonzero) or f=0 (no contact, d can be nonzero).
  3. LCP: find (d, f) s.t. d = Ad + b + Mf, 0 ≤ d, 0 ≤ f, d·f = 0.
  4. Soft relaxation: replace d·f = 0 with d·f ≤ ε; allows gradient-based optimization through approximate contact.
  5. Planning: optimize over contact forces and poses simultaneously; convex relaxation finds grasp forces consistent with friction cone in 12 ms vs 340 ms for a combinatorial search over contact modes.

    From Compliant to Rigid Contact Simulation: a Unified Approach — unifies soft and hard contact in one simulation system that recovers rigid contact as a limit

    Contact dynamics fall into two camps: soft (springs and dampers, smooth) and hard (instantaneous impulses, discontinuous). Each has trade-offs: soft contact is differentiable but adds artificial compliance; hard contact is accurate but introduces numerical stiffness and non-differentiability. The solution is a unified system that recovers rigid contact as a limit of soft contact — as the spring stiffness goes to infinity, the soft solution approaches the hard solution, so both are the same under one model.

    how it actually works

    Soft contact: Model contact as a spring-damper: f_contact = k(d_penetration) + b(v_relative), where k is stiffness, b is damping. With large k (soft-to-rigid transition), contact forces rise sharply on penetration.

    Complementarity condition: In hard contact, either d_gap = 0 (contact) or f_normal = 0 (no contact), but not both nonzero. Model this as: d_gap · f_normal = 0; d_gap ≥ 0; f_normal ≥ 0.

    Limit:** In the limit k → ∞, the soft model's solution approaches the hard contact solution. Prove this via perturbation analysis or direct construction.

    Implementation:** Implement one solver that handles both regimes: for user-specified stiffness k, solve the soft problem. If k ≥ threshold, add explicit complementarity enforcer (clamping negative gaps to zero, clamping normal forces to nonnegative). The user can dial between soft and hard contact smoothly.

    Benefit for learning: Backpropagation works through soft contact; the limit to hard contact is well-defined so neural networks can learn contact-rich manipulation.

    Towards Tight Convex Relaxations for Contact-Rich Manipulation — tightens the convex relaxation of contact complementarity for better trajectory optimization

    Contact mode switching (grasp, slide, roll) creates a combinatorial explosion: 10 contacts × 3 modes each = 3^10 ≈ 60,000 possibilities. Mixed-integer programming can solve this exactly, but is slow. Convex relaxations remove the integer constraints, making the problem polynomial-time solvable — but the relaxation is loose, so the relaxed solution might not be realizable as a true contact sequence. The goal is to tighten the relaxation without losing polynomial time.

    how it actually works

    Setup: Contact forces f, slipping velocities v_slide, and mode indicators m_ij (1 if contact i is in mode j, 0 otherwise).

    Hard constraints (MIP): If m_ij = 1, then f_i ∈ friction_cone_i. If m_ij = 0, then f_i = 0. These are nonconvex (mode determines force).

    Tightening strategies: Add valid inequalities (constraints that are implied by the hard constraints but cut off infeasible relaxed solutions). E.g., if two forces are in the same cone, add a constraint that they cannot be orthogonal (valid for physics, cuts off relaxed solutions). Use cutting planes: solve relaxed problem, check if solution is realizable, add constraints.

    Practicality: Tightened relaxations solve 10× faster than MIP on grasp problems, and the solutions are close to optimal.

Part IV

Where it stands

The math works well enough to build the systems in this corpus — and it has deep open problems that no current method solves:

Non-convexity. Gradient descent finds a local minimum, not necessarily the global one. For most deep networks this works in practice, but there is no theory predicting when it will fail and when it will not.
The curse of dimensionality. Bayesian inference becomes intractable in high dimensions; particle filters need exponentially many particles; even coverage of the state space becomes impossible. Approximations are necessary but all introduce bias.
Uncertainty calibration. A model that says it is 90% confident should be correct 90% of the time. In practice, neural networks are systematically overconfident, especially on inputs far from the training distribution.
Discrete and non-smooth math resists gradients. Contact, combinatorial assignment, and symbolic logic do not have gradients in the usual sense. Relaxations exist but lose the exact structure, and the gap between relaxation and reality is the hardest engineering problem in the field.
Guarantees versus scale. Lyapunov and CBF guarantees require knowing the dynamics. Learned dynamics have uncertainty. Making formal safety guarantees hold in the presence of learned components is an open theoretical problem.
The gap between what we optimize and what we want. Loss functions are proxies for what we actually care about. A model that minimizes the loss perfectly can still produce outputs that are wrong in the ways that matter most — the specification problem has no general solution.
