Part I

The core idea, from first principles

Five ideas, each drawn as a live diagram you can watch run, then said plainly. Together they are the whole method — the primitive, how it renders, how it learns, how it stays photographic, and why it runs in real time.

01 · the primitive

What a scene is made of: soft colored blobs

Start with the thing itself. A Gaussian-splatting scene is not a mesh of triangles and not a grid of voxels. It is a cloud of little soft blobs floating in space. Each blob is a 3D Gaussian — a smooth bell-curve of color that is dense and bright at its center and fades to nothing at its edge.

Every blob carries exactly four things: a position (where its center is in 3D), a covariance (how big it is and which way it is stretched — a squashed, tilted ellipsoid, not just a sphere), an opacity (how solid versus see-through it is), and a color. That is the whole vocabulary. A blurry wall is a few fat blobs; a crisp edge is many thin ones lined up.

Why blobs and not triangles? Because a triangle either covers a pixel or it does not — a hard yes/no you cannot nudge smoothly. A soft blob has a gentle falloff you can nudge, a hair bigger, a shade redder, a touch more solid. That smoothness is the entire trick, and the next three sections are just consequences of it.

02 · rendering

Turning blobs into a picture: splat, sort, blend

Now make a picture from the cloud. Pick a camera. Every 3D blob casts a 2D shadow on the image — a little elliptical smudge. That projection step is the splat: the blob is splatted flat onto the screen, keeping its soft edge.

For any single pixel, several blobs might overlap it, at different depths. So the blobs are sorted from near to far, and their colors are blended front-to-back. A blob near the camera paints its color and uses up some of the light; whatever is behind only shows through the fraction still left. That bookkeeping is one line — C = Σ ci αi Πj<i(1−αj) — color equals each blob's color times its opacity times how much light survived everything nearer.

The reason this matters: projecting and blending are both smooth, differentiable, and embarrassingly parallel. A GPU can splat millions of blobs for a full frame in a few milliseconds — and because every step is smooth, you can measure exactly how a tiny change to any blob would change the final picture. Hold onto that; it is what makes learning possible.

03 · learning

How the blobs learn the scene: photos in, gradients out

You never place the blobs by hand. You give the system a few dozen ordinary photos of a scene, each with its camera position, and it fits the cloud to them. Render the blobs from photo #1's viewpoint, subtract from the real photo #1, and you have an error image. Because rendering was differentiable, that error tells you which direction to push every blob's position, size, opacity, and color to make the render look more like the photo. Repeat over all the photos, thousands of times. This is ordinary gradient descent — the same engine that trains neural nets — but here the thing being trained is the blob cloud itself.

Gradient descent alone can only adjust the blobs it already has; it cannot decide the cloud needs more blobs here and fewer there. So splatting adds a second move called adaptive density control. Where a region stays blurry and the gradients are large, a blob is cloned or split to add detail. Where a blob has drifted nearly transparent and is doing no work, it is pruned away. The cloud grows dense exactly where the scene is complicated and stays sparse on flat walls.

Put together, a scene of 10 to 50 casual photos becomes a few million blobs in minutes, and the result renders in real time forever after. No network is queried at render time — the scene simply is the blobs.

04 · view-dependence

Color that changes as you move: spherical harmonics

A matte wall looks the same from anywhere, but almost nothing else does. Polished paint, a wet floor, a metal rim — their brightness and even hue shift with where you stand. If each blob had a single fixed color, a moving camera would look flat and dead.

So a blob's color is stored as a tiny function of direction instead of a constant. The tool for that is spherical harmonics — think of them as a compact set of dials that describe “from this angle the blob is a bit brighter and warmer, from that angle a bit darker.” The lowest dial is just the flat base color a matte surface needs; the higher dials add the gloss and the moving highlight a shiny surface needs.

This is why a Gaussian-splat scene feels photographic rather than cardboard as you fly through it: every blob is quietly re-tinting itself for your current viewpoint.

05 · why it won

Why it runs in real time — and NeRF didn’t

The technique splatting dethroned was NeRF. NeRF represents a scene as one neural network: to color a pixel you shoot a ray into the scene and ask the network “what is here?” at dozens or hundreds of points along that ray, then add them up. It is beautiful and it works — but every pixel of every frame costs hundreds of network evaluations, so rendering is slow and training takes hours.

Gaussian splatting makes the opposite bet. Instead of hiding the scene inside a network you must interrogate point by point, it writes the scene down explicitly as the blobs and simply projects them. There is no per-point network call at render time — just sort and blend. The same realism NeRF took seconds per frame to produce, splatting produces at 100-plus frames per second, and trains in minutes rather than hours.

That single swap — implicit-and-queried for explicit-and-rasterized — is the whole reason the field pivoted. And it buys a bonus that turns out to matter enormously for robotics: because the scene is now explicit geometry you can touch, you can edit it, attach meaning to individual blobs, hand pieces to a physics engine, or drop new objects in. Everything in Part III grows from that.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every application below is chasing one or more of these five payoffs — the recurring reasons the field switched to splatting, in the words the papers keep using.

Real-time rendering

No per-point network to query — sort and blend the explicit blobs. Robotics papers cite 100+ fps and 20–50× faster than NeRF-based methods, fast enough to track a live camera against the map.

Explicit, editable geometry

The scene is the primitives, so you can move, delete, segment, or add blobs. This is what lets manipulation work edit a scene, sample grasps on it, or hand it to a physics solver.

Photographic accuracy

Trained directly against photos with a smooth loss, splats hit 40+ dB reconstruction quality and sub-2 mm depth in surgical scenes — realistic enough that policies trained on renders transfer to real hardware.

Minutes, not hours

Differentiable rasterization plus adaptive density control converges a scene from a few dozen photos in minutes, and some feed-forward variants now predict the blobs in a single pass.

Shrinks to the edge

Voxel-merging, quantization and pruning cut millions of blobs down to fit a drone or a phone without visibly hurting the render — the reason splatting reaches real robots at all.

Part III

Every way the field is bending it

The explicit-blob representation turned out to be a substrate you can twist in dozens of directions. Sixteen families, each with the problem it solves, the specific splat twist, and real papers — robotics (ICRA / IROS / CoRL / RSS) and computer vision (CVPR 2026).

01

Live SLAM & dense mapping

the problem

A robot moving through a place must build a map and stay located in it, right now, from a live camera.

the splat twist

The map itself is a blob cloud, optimized frame-by-frame from the incoming video; because it renders in real time, the robot can localize by matching its camera against the map it is still building.

  • ROBOTICSGaussian-LIC: real-time photo-realistic SLAM with LiDAR-inertial-camera fusion
  • ROBOTICSMemGS: memory-efficient Gaussian splatting for real-time SLAM
  • ROBOTICSMGSO: monocular real-time photometric SLAM
  • CVPRODGS-SLAM: omnidirectional Gaussian splatting SLAM
  • ROBOTICSCGS-SLAM: compact 3D Gaussian splatting for dense visual SLAM
02

Self-driving & street-scene reconstruction

the problem

Rebuild a whole moving street from a car’s cameras and LiDAR so you can replay it, test in it, or predict what happens next.

the splat twist

Static background blobs are constrained by road/sky geometry and LiDAR depth, while moving vehicles get their own time-varying blobs — a replayable, editable digital twin of the drive.

  • ROBOTICSAutoSplat: constrained Gaussian splatting for autonomous driving scenes
  • ROBOTICSGS-SDF: LiDAR-augmented Gaussian splatting + neural SDF
  • CVPRGaussianDWM: 3D Gaussian driving world model
  • CVPRRaGS: 3DGS from 4D radar and a monocular camera
  • CVPRGau-Occ: geometry-completed Gaussians for 3D occupancy
03

Manipulation & world models

the problem

To grasp and rearrange objects, a robot needs a 3D model of the scene it can actually reason over and act on.

the splat twist

Distill vision-model features into the blobs so the scene is editable and queryable, sample grasps on it, or couple the blobs to physics particles so the render doubles as a dynamics model.

Photoreal simulators & sim-to-real

Learned policies need endless photorealistic training views, but collecting them on real robots is slow and expensive.

the splat twist

Capture the world once as a splat, render unlimited novel viewpoints and poses to train in simulation, then deploy on hardware — the render is realistic enough that the policy transfers.

Dynamic & 4D scenes

the problem

The world moves — a static blob cloud can’t capture a waving hand, a driving car, or a beating heart.

the splat twist

Give each blob motion: a deformation over time, a velocity, or a learnable “lifespan”, turning the cloud into a 4D reconstruction you can scrub through.

  • ROBOTICSEmbracing Dynamics: dynamics-aware 4D Gaussian splatting SLAM
  • CVPRReFlow: self-correcting motion learning for dynamic scenes
  • CVPRSharpTimeGS: dynamic splatting via lifespan modulation
  • CVPRMoRGS: per-Gaussian motion for streamable dynamic scenes

Human avatars, faces & bodies

the problem

Build a photorealistic person you can animate — pose, expression, speech — from just a few views or one video.

the splat twist

Bind the blobs to a skeleton and a face model (linear blend skinning + spacetime Gaussians) so driving the rig moves the cloud, keeping detail while staying animatable.

  • CVPRHyperGaussians: high-dimensional conditioning for facial detail

the problem

the splat twist

Surfaces & meshes out of blobs

the problem

Blobs look great but engineering, physics and collision need a clean surface or watertight mesh.

the splat twist

Flatten the 3D Gaussians into oriented 2D disks (2D Gaussian splatting) or tie them to a signed-distance field, so a real surface with correct normals falls out.

  • ROBOTICSEndo-2DTAM: 2D-Gaussian surface-normal-aware tracking + mapping
  • ROBOTICSGS-SDF: geometrically consistent rendering + reconstruction
  • ROBOTICSMesh-Learner: texturing meshes with spherical harmonics

Sparse-view, few-shot & feed-forward

the problem

Vanilla splatting needs many photos and a per-scene optimization; often you only have three or four views — or want an instant answer.

the splat twist

Learn strong priors so 3–4 views suffice, or train a network that predicts the whole blob cloud in a single forward pass with no per-scene fitting at all.

  • CVPRSR3R: super-resolution 3D reconstruction with feed-forward splatting

  • CVPRHeroGS: hierarchical guidance for robust sparse-view splatting

11

Materials, lighting & relighting

the problem

Reconstructions bake the original lighting in — you can’t move the sun or drop the object into a new scene.

the splat twist

Split each blob’s appearance into material versus illumination (inverse rendering), so the scene can be relit, edited, or path-traced afterwards.

  • CVPRGHPT: real-time relightable Gaussian splatting via hybrid path tracing
  • CVPRMatSpray: fusing 2D material knowledge onto 3D geometry
  • CVPRPhyGaP: physically-grounded Gaussians with polarization cues
  • CVPRSGS-Intrinsic: sparse-view indoor inverse rendering

the splat twist

Rewrite the observation model for another sensor’s physics — an event camera’s asynchronous spikes, a thermal camera’s radiation, radar returns, or a fingertip’s touch.

13

Compression & on-device speed

the problem

A few million blobs won’t fit — or run fast enough — on a drone, a headset, or a phone.

the splat twist

Merge blobs in voxels, quantize their attributes into codebooks, and prune the invisible ones — shrinking the cloud and speeding training without a visible quality drop.

  • CVPRFastGS: training 3D Gaussian splatting in 100 seconds
  • CVPRTurbo-GS: accelerating high-resolution radiance-field fitting
  • ROBOTICSMemGS: voxel-merging for memory-efficient SLAM
  • ROBOTICSCGS-SLAM: geometry-codebook quantization

Localization, navigation & safety on GS maps

the problem

Given a splat map, a robot still has to answer: where am I, and how do I move through here without hitting anything?

the splat twist

Localize by rendering candidate views and matching, plan through a differentiable collision cost built from blob overlap, and filter motions with a safety barrier — all on the live map.

  • ROBOTICSGSplatLoc: grounding keypoint descriptors into 3DGS for localization
  • ROBOTICSLoGS: visual localization via Gaussian splatting, fewer images
  • ROBOTICSFOCI: trajectory optimization on Gaussian splats
  • ROBOTICSA control barrier function for safe navigation on online GS maps

Model the scope’s co-located light and the tissue’s reflectance physically, use 2D-Gaussian surfaces for accurate depth, and add deformation fields for non-rigid anatomy.

Part IV

Where it stands

Splatting is settled as the default way to turn photos into an explorable 3D scene. What is still hard:

Truly one-shot capture. Single-image and ultra-sparse reconstruction still lean on hallucinated priors that can invent geometry that isn’t there.
Watertight, simulation-ready meshes. Surfaces come out fast but not always closed and physical enough to drop straight into a simulator.
City scale. Kilometer-scale scenes need careful hierarchical, streaming engineering — memory is the wall.
Hard materials. Caustics, glass, subsurface scattering and true reflections still break the simple blob model.
Long, live video. Continuous online reconstruction over long horizons runs into a growing-memory problem.
Theory. There is still no clean account of why Gaussians work this well, or how to predict the right number of them up front.
