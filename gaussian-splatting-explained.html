01 · the primitive

What a scene is made of: soft colored blobs

Start with the thing itself. A Gaussian-splatting scene is not a mesh of triangles and not a grid of voxels. It is a cloud of little soft blobs floating in space. Each blob is a 3D Gaussian — a smooth bell-curve of color that is dense and bright at its center and fades to nothing at its edge.

Every blob carries exactly four things: a position (where its center is in 3D), a covariance (how big it is and which way it is stretched — a squashed, tilted ellipsoid, not just a sphere), an opacity (how solid versus see-through it is), and a color. That is the whole vocabulary. A blurry wall is a few fat blobs; a crisp edge is many thin ones lined up.

Why blobs and not triangles? Because a triangle either covers a pixel or it does not — a hard yes/no you cannot nudge smoothly. A soft blob has a gentle falloff you can nudge, a hair bigger, a shade redder, a touch more solid. That smoothness is the entire trick, and the next three sections are just consequences of it.

the actual math — one level deeper

A 3D Gaussian is G(x) = exp( −½ (x−μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x−μ) ).

μ = position (3 numbers). Σ = the 3×3 covariance = the ellipsoid’s size + orientation.

Σ is kept a valid ellipsoid by building it from a rotation and a scale: Σ = R S Sᵀ Rᵀ — so you optimize a quaternion R and three scales S, never a raw matrix.

Plus one opacity α and the color (below). ~59 learnable numbers per blob.

02 · rendering

Turning blobs into a picture: splat, sort, blend

Now make a picture from the cloud. Pick a camera. Every 3D blob casts a 2D shadow on the image — a little elliptical smudge. That projection step is the splat: the blob is splatted flat onto the screen, keeping its soft edge.

For any single pixel, several blobs might overlap it, at different depths. So the blobs are sorted from near to far, and their colors are blended front-to-back. A blob near the camera paints its color and uses up some of the light; whatever is behind only shows through the fraction still left. That bookkeeping is one line — C = Σ ci αi Πj<i(1−αj) — color equals each blob's color times its opacity times how much light survived everything nearer.

The reason this matters: projecting and blending are both smooth, differentiable, and embarrassingly parallel. A GPU can splat millions of blobs for a full frame in a few milliseconds — and because every step is smooth, you can measure exactly how a tiny change to any blob would change the final picture. Hold onto that; it is what makes learning possible.

the actual math — one level deeper

Project each 3D Gaussian to a 2D one on the image: Σ′ = J W Σ Wᵀ Jᵀ, where W is the world→camera transform and J is the Jacobian (local linear approximation) of the projection.

Splat i’s weight at a pixel: αᵢ = opacityᵢ · exp(−½ dᵀ Σ′⁻¹ d), with d = pixel − center.

Composite front→back: C = Σᵢ cᵢ αᵢ ∏₃₋ᵢ(1−αᵡ). Transmittance T = ∏(1−αᵡ).

03 · learning

How the blobs learn the scene: photos in, gradients out

You never place the blobs by hand. You give the system a few dozen ordinary photos of a scene, each with its camera position, and it fits the cloud to them. Render the blobs from photo #1's viewpoint, subtract from the real photo #1, and you have an error image. Because rendering was differentiable, that error tells you which direction to push every blob's position, size, opacity, and color to make the render look more like the photo. Repeat over all the photos, thousands of times. This is ordinary gradient descent — the same engine that trains neural nets — but here the thing being trained is the blob cloud itself.

Gradient descent alone can only adjust the blobs it already has; it cannot decide the cloud needs more blobs here and fewer there. So splatting adds a second move called adaptive density control. Where a region stays blurry and the gradients are large, a blob is cloned or split to add detail. Where a blob has drifted nearly transparent and is doing no work, it is pruned away. The cloud grows dense exactly where the scene is complicated and stays sparse on flat walls.

Put together, a scene of 10 to 50 casual photos becomes a few million blobs in minutes, and the result renders in real time forever after. No network is queried at render time — the scene simply is the blobs.

the actual math — one level deeper

Loss against the photo: L = (1−λ)·L₁ + λ·L_D-SSIM, with λ ≈ 0.2.

Every parameter (μ, R, S, α, the SH color) gets a gradient from L and steps with Adam.

Densify where the view-space positional gradient ‖∇μ‖ exceeds a threshold τ: clone a small under-reconstructed blob, split a too-large one. Prune where α < ε.

04 · view-dependence

Color that changes as you move: spherical harmonics

A matte wall looks the same from anywhere, but almost nothing else does. Polished paint, a wet floor, a metal rim — their brightness and even hue shift with where you stand. If each blob had a single fixed color, a moving camera would look flat and dead.

So a blob's color is stored as a tiny function of direction instead of a constant. The tool for that is spherical harmonics — think of them as a compact set of dials that describe “from this angle the blob is a bit brighter and warmer, from that angle a bit darker.” The lowest dial is just the flat base color a matte surface needs; the higher dials add the gloss and the moving highlight a shiny surface needs.

This is why a Gaussian-splat scene feels photographic rather than cardboard as you fly through it: every blob is quietly re-tinting itself for your current viewpoint.

the actual math — one level deeper

Color as a function of view direction d: c(d) = Σₗ Σᵤ kₗᵤ Yₗᵤ(d).

Yₗᵤ are the spherical-harmonic basis functions; k are the learned coefficients (one triple per band, per channel).

Degree L=3 → 16 coefficients × 3 channels = 48 numbers. Band l=0 is the flat base color; higher bands carry the view-dependent shine.

05 · why it won

Why it runs in real time — and NeRF didn’t

The technique splatting dethroned was NeRF. NeRF represents a scene as one neural network: to color a pixel you shoot a ray into the scene and ask the network “what is here?” at dozens or hundreds of points along that ray, then add them up. It is beautiful and it works — but every pixel of every frame costs hundreds of network evaluations, so rendering is slow and training takes hours.

Gaussian splatting makes the opposite bet. Instead of hiding the scene inside a network you must interrogate point by point, it writes the scene down explicitly as the blobs and simply projects them. There is no per-point network call at render time — just sort and blend. The same realism NeRF took seconds per frame to produce, splatting produces at 100-plus frames per second, and trains in minutes rather than hours.

That single swap — implicit-and-queried for explicit-and-rasterized — is the whole reason the field pivoted. And it buys a bonus that turns out to matter enormously for robotics: because the scene is now explicit geometry you can touch, you can edit it, attach meaning to individual blobs, hand pieces to a physics engine, or drop new objects in. Everything in Part III grows from that.

the actual math — one level deeper

NeRF renders a ray by integration: C = ∫ T(t) σ(t) c(t) dt,  T(t)=exp(−∫₀ᵗ σ ds) — sampled at ~64–192 points, each one an MLP query.

3DGS renders by discrete sorted compositing: C = Σ cᵢ αᵢ ∏(1−αᵡ) — no per-point network, one rasterization pass.

Same volumetric-blend math at heart; the win is precomputing the answer into explicit primitives instead of querying a network hundreds of times per ray.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every application below is chasing one or more of these five payoffs — the recurring reasons the field switched to splatting, in the words the papers keep using.

Real-time rendering

No per-point network to query — sort and blend the explicit blobs. Robotics papers cite 100+ fps and 20–50× faster than NeRF-based methods, fast enough to track a live camera against the map.

Explicit, editable geometry

The scene is the primitives, so you can move, delete, segment, or add blobs. This is what lets manipulation work edit a scene, sample grasps on it, or hand it to a physics solver.

Photographic accuracy

Trained directly against photos with a smooth loss, splats hit 40+ dB reconstruction quality and sub-2 mm depth in surgical scenes — realistic enough that policies trained on renders transfer to real hardware.

Minutes, not hours

Differentiable rasterization plus adaptive density control converges a scene from a few dozen photos in minutes, and some feed-forward variants now predict the blobs in a single pass.

Shrinks to the edge

Voxel-merging, quantization and pruning cut millions of blobs down to fit a drone or a phone without visibly hurting the render — the reason splatting reaches real robots at all.

Part III

Every way the field is bending it

The explicit-blob representation turned out to be a substrate you can twist in dozens of directions — but under the variety there are really just two moves.

move A — add an attribute. Bolt an extra channel onto every Gaussian beyond color and opacity: a semantic feature (so you can query the scene in words), a motion (so it can move over time), a material (so it can be relit). The cloud stays a cloud; each blob just carries more.

move B — constrain the cloud. Leave the blobs alone but pin or reshape the whole cloud — onto a surface or SDF, to a sensor's physics, to a body rig, to a physics engine, or to known geometry like a road. You change what the cloud is allowed to be.

01

Live SLAM & dense mapping

constrain the cloud
the problem

A robot walking into an unknown building has to do two things at once, and each one needs the other. It has to figure out where its camera is — but it can only do that against a map. And it has to build that map — but it can only place things correctly if it knows where the camera is. It starts with neither.

the approaches

Vanilla splatting assumes you already know every camera pose and have all the photos up front. A robot exploring a building has neither: frames arrive one at a time and the camera's position is exactly what you're trying to find. The move is to run two optimizations against the same blob cloud, alternating. Track: freeze the map and solve for the new frame's pose by sliding the virtual camera until its rendered view matches the incoming photo — the photometric error is the tracking signal. Map: freeze the pose and grow/adjust blobs to fold the new frame in.

Because the render is real-time, this tracking-by-rendering closes the loop fast enough to keep up with a moving robot — the thing NeRF-based SLAM could never do. The two hard parts are both about the explicit cloud: it grows without bound (a memory problem) and small pose errors accumulate into drift, so much of the work here is merging, pruning, and bundle-adjusting the blobs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Frame 47 arrives. Best first guess for where the camera is: wherever frame 46 ended up.
  2. Render the half-built blob map from that guessed viewpoint. It comes out shifted about 12 pixels off from the real photo.
  3. Slide the guessed pose a little at a time until that shift shrinks to almost nothing. Where the render finally lines up with the photo is the camera’s true position — you found it by matching, not by any external sensor.
  4. Now trust that pose and fold the frame in: the far wall is still blurry in the map, so clone blobs there and tune their color until the render matches.
  5. Frame 48 arrives; repeat. Every few seconds, merge duplicate blobs so the map’s memory doesn’t grow without end.

    Gaussian-LIC — seeds blobs from LiDAR + triangulated points, adds a sky model + exposure comp; C++/CUDA — beats methods that use ground-truth poses

    A robot stepping into an unknown building must track its own position and build a map simultaneously—each depends on the other. Rather than waiting for dozens of frames or needing external pose sensors, this paper seeds a Gaussian blob cloud from LiDAR returns and structure-from-motion points, then adds a physical sky model and exposure compensation so the cloud can lock onto incoming frames in real time. The trick: explicit geometric anchors from a laser scanner eliminate the guessing about where surfaces sit.

    how it actually works

    The machinery:

    1. LiDAR and triangulated SfM points are mapped directly to initial Gaussian positions and covariances—each 3D point becomes a seed blob.
    2. As frames arrive, the camera pose is refined by rendering the blob cloud and sliding it photometrically until it matches the live image.
    3. Simultaneously, the blobs adjust their position, color, and size to explain the new frame.
    4. A separate sky model (typically a distant sphere) prevents blobs from incorrectly filling the empty space above the scene, and exposure compensation handles changing lighting between frames.
    5. The LiDAR anchor is decisive: blobs cannot drift far from where the laser said geometry sits, so the depth stays honest even when photometric signals are ambiguous.
  • IROS
    MemGS — merges redundant blobs in voxel space by geometric similarity + patch-grid init → cuts GPU memory without losing quality (memory is the SLAM wall)

    A SLAM robot rebuilding a dense map as it explores produces a splat that grows without bound — millions of redundant blobs covering the same wall from slightly different angles. That redundancy devours GPU memory, the resource robots have least of. MemGS treats the blob cloud as a voxel grid and merges Gaussians that occupy the same voxel (same position, similar color and covariance). It does this periodically during mapping to keep the cloud lean. The result is a SLAM system that never blows out memory, rebuilds maps that are geometrically tight, and gains the speed of a smaller cloud for free.

    how it actually works

    The problem: As a SLAM robot explores, each new frame adds new blobs to fill in detail. Over hours or in a large space, redundancy accumulates — many blobs sitting inches apart, saying the same thing about the wall they cover.

    The mechanism: (1) Divide the blob cloud into a voxel grid (coarse 3D bins). (2) Within each bin, cluster the blobs by position and color similarity. (3) Merge each cluster into a single representative Gaussian, weighted by the component blobs' opacity. (4) Run this periodically (every 100–200 frames) to keep memory bounded. (5) Optionally, use the voxel structure as an init seed for new scenes (fast restart from a coarse prior).

    Why it works: Merging does not lose information because the Gaussians being merged already cover nearly identical space and color. The merged Gaussian's covariance naturally widens to match the spread of its components, and opacity is re-weighted. The voxel-space geometry quantization is a compact representation that SLAM exploits to make memory the non-binding constraint.

  • ICRA
    MGSO — a photometric SLAM front-end seeds a dense point cloud to initialize the blobs → far fewer Gaussians, runs on a laptop

    Fitting a blob cloud from frames is fast, but it requires enough overlap in the photos and enough GPU power. This paper flips the usual order: run a traditional photometric SLAM front-end first to recover the camera trajectory, then use that known pose to seed a dense point cloud via triangulation. That point cloud becomes the initializer for the Gaussian cloud, so far fewer blobs are needed, and the whole system runs on a laptop. Dense geometric initialization trades upfront pose estimation for downstream blob efficiency.

    how it actually works

    The machinery:

    1. Incoming frames are tracked photometrically using a lightweight SLAM frontend (e.g., dense photometric warping or semi-direct methods).
    2. Once the camera poses are known, standard triangulation from multiple views produces a dense 3D point cloud.
    3. That point cloud is fed directly as initial Gaussian positions and covariances, skipping the usual sparse SfM step.
    4. Since the cloud starts dense and well-posed (geometry is already triangulated), the optimization loop needs fewer iterations and fewer blobs to converge.
    5. On modest hardware (single GPU or CPU), the training time and memory remain tractable while reconstruction quality stays high.
  • IROS
    CGS-SLAM — sliding-window masking prunes ellipsoids + a geometry codebook quantizes their attributes → compact map, local-to-global bundle adjustment

    A live SLAM system has to make three hard choices simultaneously: which blobs to keep (memory), how to slide poses without drift (optimization), and how to make the result useful later (compression). CGS-SLAM addresses all three by building a geometry codebook — a small set of learned covariance patterns that almost every blob in the map can be quantized to. Sliding-window pose graphs keep the number of simultaneous variables bounded. The result: a SLAM system that fits on a laptop, runs at video rate, and produces a map 50–100× smaller without visibly hurting quality.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Each Gaussian's 3×3 covariance is 6 unique numbers (symmetric matrix); millions of blobs mean millions of these matrices. Pose drift over long sequences breaks global consistency. Memory is the wall.

    The mechanism: (1) Learn a codebook of ~100 representative covariance matrices from a sample of scenes. (2) Quantize each blob's covariance to the nearest codebook entry (one 8-bit index instead of 6 floats). (3) Maintain a sliding window of recent keyframes and poses; optimize poses only within the window, then fix and marginalize old poses. (4) Periodically run bundle adjustment on fixed windowed poses to absorb any accumulated error.

    Why it works: Most of the scene (walls, ground, sky) use a small palette of shapes — flat, thin, or sphere-like. A codebook of 100 entries covers ~95% of the variance. The quantization saves 6–8 bits per blob with no perceptual loss. The sliding window keeps the optimization convex and fast, while periodic bundle adjustment prevents global drift.

  • CVPR
    ODGS-SLAM — first omnidirectional (360°) splatting SLAM

    Standard SLAM works on a pinhole camera model: you point the camera forward and measure depth linearly from the optical axis. A 360-degree fish-eye or omnidirectional camera sees the entire sphere around it at once, but the pinhole mathematics falls apart. ODGS-SLAM extends Gaussian splatting SLAM to spherical projection: rays are cast from the camera center in all directions, and blobs are projected using the spherical geometry. The result: first omnidirectional Gaussian-SLAM, capable of mapping an entire room (walls, floor, ceiling) without rotating the camera body, useful for drones and rotating head-mount cameras.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Pinhole projection assumes rays emanate from a point and intersect a flat image plane at right angles to the optical axis. Omnidirectional cameras violate this: rays go in all directions and wrap around. The standard projected-Gaussian math produces nonsense.

    The mechanism: (1) Instead of projecting a 3D Gaussian to 2D via the Jacobian of pinhole projection, use the Jacobian of spherical projection: a 3D point maps to (θ, φ) angles. (2) The projected Gaussian on the sphere is still a Gaussian but in angle-space. (3) Splatting and blending proceed as usual, but the pixel grid is understood as a latitude–longitude map. (4) Tracking and densification both work on this spherical canvas.

    Why it works: The math generalizes: the rendering equation (splat, sort, blend) is independent of the projection model. Spherical projection is just a different Jacobian. The payoff is that a single forward-looking camera on a drone or robot can now capture an entire 360° field in one pass, halving capture time for full-room mapping.

02

Self-driving & street-scene reconstruction

constrain the cloud
the problem

You want to rebuild a whole street from a car’s cameras so you can replay the drive, test a self-driving stack in it, or edit it. Two things make a street different from a room: it is full of other cars that move, and you only ever saw each surface from the single lane you happened to drive in — never the far side of anything.

the approaches

A street is not one static scene: it is a rigid world plus cars that move, filmed from a car that only ever sees each surface from one side. Fit one blob cloud naively and the moving cars smear into streaks while the unseen backs of objects fill with garbage. So the cloud is split — static background versus dynamic actors — and each half is constrained differently.

The static blobs get pinned to physical structure so they don't float: road and sky are forced flat and consistent across views, and LiDAR depth anchors where blobs are allowed to sit. Each moving vehicle gets its own pose track over time plus a color that is allowed to change frame-to-frame. The payoff is a replayable, editable digital twin of a drive — you can pull a car out, add one, or re-render the whole street from a lane that never existed.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Sort every blob into two piles: the rigid world (road, buildings, sky) and the movers (other vehicles).
  2. Pin the world blobs to a flat road plane and a distant sky so they cannot float or warp as the viewpoint changes.
  3. Anchor their depth with the LiDAR: a wall sits exactly where the laser says it does, not where color alone guesses.
  4. Give the car ahead its own blobs with a pose that slides frame to frame — so it drives cleanly across the scene instead of smearing into a streak.
  5. You never saw that car’s left side. Mirror its right side onto a template to fill the unseen half plausibly.
  6. Now the drive is editable: delete a car, add one, or re-render the whole street from a lane you never actually drove.

    AutoSplat — geometry constraints on road/sky blobs; a “reflected Gaussian consistency” trick supervises a car's unseen far side via a mirrored 3D template; per-object residual spherical harmonics model changing appearance

    A street is not a single static scene—cars move, buildings stay put, and you only ever saw each building's back from the lane you drove in. This paper splits the Gaussian cloud into two groups: static background (road, sky, buildings) and dynamic actors (cars). Static blobs are pinned to a flat road plane and anchored by LiDAR depth so they do not float or warp with viewpoint changes. Dynamic vehicles get their own pose tracks. For the unseen back of a car, a reflected Gaussian consistency trick mirrors the visible side onto a 3D template, filling in plausible geometry the camera never captured.

    how it actually works

    The machinery:

    1. Blobs are classified as static or dynamic via a learned per-blob logit that is optimized during training.
    2. Static blobs are constrained: (a) road blobs are projected onto a ground plane and their depth is supervised by LiDAR, (b) sky blobs sit on a distant sphere to stay consistent across viewpoint changes.
    3. Dynamic blobs (cars) are given an explicit pose trajectory over time; their opacity and color can change per-frame to handle appearance shifts.
    4. For a car's unseen side, a reflected consistency loss is applied: the 3D Gaussian cloud is mirrored across a plane, the mirrored rendering is compared to the visible rendering, and the loss penalizes asymmetry—forcing the hidden side to plausibly mirror the seen side.
    5. Per-object residual spherical harmonics handle view-dependent appearance changes (e.g., headlights turning on), modeling dynamics within the SH coefficients.
  • IROS
    GS-SDF — trains a neural signed-distance field on LiDAR as a geometry scaffold, then initializes + regularizes the blobs against it so geometry doesn't drift

    A street reconstruction from a self-driving car dataset has a geometry problem: if you fit one blob cloud naively, the blobs will drift and float because color alone cannot anchor them to the ground. You want the road to be flat, the walls to be vertical, and the geometry to agree with LiDAR measurements — but naive gradient descent on color will ignore all that.

    The solution is to create a signed-distance field (SDF) learned from LiDAR points: a function that, at any position in space, returns the distance to the nearest surface. Then use that SDF as a constraint during blob fitting — regularize each Gaussian's position and shape to stay close to the SDF's zero-level set (the true surface).

    The SDF acts as a manifold field that pulls stray blobs back onto the physically correct geometry, preventing the blobs from floating and ensuring consistency with the LiDAR scan. This is especially important for static structure like roads where the LiDAR is reliable.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Color gradients alone do not constrain geometry to match LiDAR; blobs drift freely and contradict the physical scan.

    The mechanism:

    1. Train a neural signed-distance field (SDF) on the LiDAR point cloud: it learns a function that outputs the distance to the nearest surface at any 3D point.
    2. Initialize Gaussians by sampling from the LiDAR point cloud, seeding them at the surface points.
    3. During optimization, add a regularization term that penalizes blobs for being far from the SDF's zero-level set: loss += λ * distance_to_sdf_surface(blob_center).
    4. The SDF acts as a geometry scaffold: it pulls blobs back toward the true surface when color alone would push them astray.

    Why it works: The SDF encodes the physical truth of where surfaces are (from LiDAR). By regularizing against it, the Gaussians stay physically grounded even when color might be ambiguous, solving the geometry drift problem that plagues street reconstruction.

  • CVPR
    GaussianDWM — a driving “world model” — the Gaussian scene encodes space + language and predicts what happens next

    A traditional driving SLAM map is a static, inert geometry. But self-driving systems need to predict what will happen next—where other cars will move, whether a pedestrian will step into the road. This paper treats the Gaussian scene as a world model: it encodes both spatial geometry and language understanding, then adds a prediction head that forecasts the future state of the scene. The same blob cloud that renders the street also predicts its next frame, enabling planning systems to imagine consequences before acting.

    how it actually works

    The machinery:

    1. The Gaussian cloud is augmented with per-blob feature vectors—learned embeddings that capture object identity and semantic class.
    2. A language encoder embeds free-text queries or instructions (e.g., car turning left) into the same feature space.
    3. A temporal transformer predicts the next frame's Gaussian attributes (positions, opacities, colors) given the current frame and language context—actors move according to learned patterns.
    4. The rendering engine then converts predicted Gaussians into an image, which is compared to ground-truth future frames to supervise the prediction network.
    5. At inference, a planner can sample predicted futures under different actions (e.g., if I brake), visualizing the consequences before committing.
  • CVPR
    RaGS — fuses 4D radar + a single camera into blobs for 3D detection when vision alone fails

    A camera alone struggles in rain, glare, or when looking through windows. But automotive radar penetrates weather and glass, returning raw detections (range, azimuth, elevation, velocity) without images. This paper fuses 4D radar (range plus 3 velocity) with a single camera: the radar provides sparse 3D detection anchors, and the camera provides appearance. The Gaussian cloud is fit jointly against both modalities, so geometry is validated by radar when vision ambiguity would lead to hallucination.

    how it actually works

    The machinery:

    1. Radar returns are converted to 3D detections in world coordinates using the known camera pose and radar extrinsics.
    2. A Gaussian blob cloud is initialized with blobs placed at radar-detected positions; initial size is set from the radar resolution and velocity uncertainty.
    3. The photometric loss compares the rendered blob appearance to the camera image (standard splatting).
    4. A geometric regularization loss constrains blobs to remain close to radar-detected object centroids and velocities—if a blob drifts away from a radar detection, the loss rises.
    5. The fusion is tight: radar detections in poor-visibility conditions anchor the cloud, preventing the vision-only optimizer from building false geometry.
  • CVPR
    Gau-Occ — geometry-completed Gaussians fuse camera/radar/LiDAR into 3D occupancy

    Occupancy prediction asks is there an obstacle here? at every voxel, a coarse geometric question that does not need fine photorealistic detail. This paper fuses multi-sensor observations (camera, radar, LiDAR) into a Gaussian representation, then samples the occupancy grid from the blob cloud's density. Because Gaussians are continuous and differentiable, the entire pipeline—from sensor fusion to occupancy—is end-to-end trainable, and geometry is completed where sensors have gaps (e.g., radar fills vision's blind spots).

    how it actually works

    The machinery:

    1. Sensor data is fused into a blob-cloud representation: LiDAR points seed blob positions, camera photometry refines appearance, radar velocity supervises motion.
    2. An occupancy grid is defined (e.g., 0.1 m voxels); for each voxel, the occupancy probability is read from the integrated Gaussian density—sum the opacity and size contributions of all blobs overlapping that voxel.
    3. The occupancy grid is compared to a ground-truth occupancy label (e.g., from LiDAR or manual annotation) via a binary cross-entropy loss.
    4. Gradients flow backward through the density integration, updating blob positions, sizes, and opacities to improve occupancy prediction.
    5. Fusion completeness: sensors see different aspects (LiDAR sees occlusions, radar sees moving objects, camera sees fine detail), so the joint blob cloud naturally fills gaps where one sensor is blind.
03

Manipulation & world models

add an attribute
the problem

Before a robot can pick up ‘the mug’, two things have to be true that a plain 3D scan cannot give it. It has to know which blobs are the mug. And it has to be able to picture the scene after it moves something, so it can check a plan before its arm commits to it.

the approaches

the problem

When you train a splat, the lighting of the room you filmed gets baked into every blob’s color. So you cannot move the sun, or drop that object into a new scene with different light, without it looking wrong — the old highlights are painted on for good.

the approaches

A trained splat bakes the capture lighting into each blob's color — so you cannot move the sun, or drop the object into a new scene with new light, without it looking wrong. The move is inverse rendering: split each Gaussian's appearance into material (albedo, roughness) and illumination, so lighting becomes a separate factor you can change afterward.

This is strictly harder than plain reconstruction — you're asking one set of photos to reveal both what things are made of and how they were lit — so these methods lean on extra physics (path tracing, polarization) or 2D material priors to break the ambiguity.

worked example — one concrete run

  2. To render, combine the two: material × illumination = the color you see.
  3. Now swap the illumination — move the light, change its color — and re-combine. The highlight slides to the correct new place instead of staying painted where it was.
  4. Break the ambiguity with physics: one set of photos revealing both material and light at once is hard, so these methods add path tracing or polarization cues to pull the two apart.
  5. Relight it anywhere, like an ordinary 3D asset.

  3. Prune the invisible: blobs so faint they add no visible light are deleted outright.
  4. Check the render still looks the same. It does — because you removed only redundancy, not detail.
  5. A cloud that was millions of blobs now fits on the device in front of you.

The result: localization and reconstruction from a small image set, where vanilla splatting would struggle or fail entirely.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Gaussian splatting wants many overlapping views to pin down the cloud. With only 3–8 images, underconstrained regions collapse or fill with gibberish. SfM is designed for sparse data; splatting is not.

    The mechanism: (1) Run standard SfM (COLMAP, OpenMVG) on the input images to get a sparse point cloud and camera poses. (2) Initialize each blob at an SfM point's location with a reasonable default covariance (small isotropic Gaussian). (3) Densify as usual, but start from this informed prior instead of random initialization. (4) For localization, solve PnP on the sparse SfM points to get a coarse 6D pose, then refine photometrically by rendering the Gaussian map.

    Why it works: SfM is a strong geometric prior that validates the camera model and anchor the cloud before optimization. The Gaussian then learns appearance and fine detail on top of that foundation. With few views, this structure-first-then-detail is more reliable than end-to-end raw optimization.

  • IROS
    FOCI — optimizes a trajectory directly against a differentiable Gaussian-overlap collision cost

    A robot planning a path through a mapped scene must avoid collisions. Classical planners discretize space (a grid, a roadmap). FOCI (Flow-based Collision Inference) treats the Gaussian splat as a continuous collision field: every blob has a shape and solidity, so you can compute how much a proposed trajectory overlaps the cloud and treat that overlap as a differentiable cost. Path planning becomes continuous optimization: nudge the path to minimize collision cost in real time, without ever discretizing the space. The result: smooth, reactive motion planning on the live map, at 10–50× the speed of grid-based methods.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Voxel grids or mesh collision detection requires rasterizing the splat into another representation (slow) or requires discrete checks (clunky). Splatting is already a differentiable rendering; can you make collision differentiable too?

    The mechanism: (1) For a proposed trajectory (a time-parameterized path), sample positions along it. (2) For each position, render the Gaussian map at that viewpoint. (3) The density (number of blobs, their opacity summed) at each location encodes collision. (4) Define collision cost as the summed opacity of blobs within a collision radius of the trajectory. (5) Backprop that cost through the path parameters (waypoint positions, via timing constraints) to nudge the path away from occupied space.

    Why it works: Rendering is differentiable and continuous in the pose parameters. A path that grazes a blob gets a small cost; one that plows through gets large cost. Because the cost is smooth, gradient descent can optimize the path without local minima traps that plague discrete grid search.

  • IROS
    CBF on GS maps — a control-barrier-function safety filter on the live map — 20–50× faster than NeRF-based safety

    Safety certification of robot motion requires formal guarantees: "this trajectory will not hit an obstacle." Classical approaches use convex approximations of the collision space. Control Barrier Functions (CBF) on Gaussian maps treat the blob cloud as a differentiable scalar field (distance-to-obstacle), and use CBF theory to synthesize control laws that provably keep the robot safe. The result: formally safe motion on a real learned map (not an abstract approximation), at 20–50× the speed of sampling-based verification, because the collision check is one neural network forward pass.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Formal safety verification requires a known obstacle representation. Classical methods assume polytopes or known meshes. A learned Gaussian map is implicit and continuous — hard to verify against.

    The mechanism: (1) Define a barrier function h(x) = minimum distance from state x to any blob, measured as opacity-weighted distance. (2) The Control Barrier Function theorem states: if h(x) ≥ 0 and certain derivatives of h along the system dynamics stay non-negative, the system cannot enter the unsafe set h < 0. (3) Synthesize a controller (acceleration command) that respects that constraint. (4) At each timestep, render the Gaussian map and query the barrier function, which is a differentiable operation.

    Why it works: The barrier function is a continuous signed distance approximated by the Gaussian density field. CBF provides a formal system to translate that barrier into control laws. Because rendering is cheap (milliseconds), safety filtering runs at high control frequency (100+ Hz) with negligible latency.

Medicine — surgery, endoscopy, MRI

constrain the cloud
the problem

A surgeon’s endoscope breaks every assumption plain splatting likes, all at once: the light is bolted right beside the camera (so glare moves with you), the tissue is wet and textureless, and it deforms as the patient breathes. Get it right and you can rebuild the anatomy to sub-millimeter accuracy.

the approaches

A surgeon's endoscope is a moving camera with its light source rigidly bolted alongside, staring at wet, textureless, deforming tissue. Every assumption plain splatting likes — fixed lighting, static scene, rich texture — is violated at once. The move is to put the missing physics back: model the co-located light so specular glare is explained rather than baked in, use 2D-Gaussian surfaces for honest sub-millimeter depth, and add a deformation field for the non-rigid anatomy.

The stakes make the accuracy worth it — this is the difference between a pretty reconstruction and one a surgeon could navigate by.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Model the co-located light: because the lamp is fixed to the camera, the bright specular spot is predictable — explain it physically instead of baking it into the tissue’s color.
  2. Use flat 2D-Gaussian surfaces (not fat blobs) so the depth of the tissue wall is honest.
  3. Add a deformation field so the surface can flex frame to frame as the anatomy moves.
  4. Track the scope and map the tissue together — it is SLAM, performed inside a body.
  5. Out comes a sub-2mm reconstruction a surgeon could navigate by.
  • IROS
    Endo-2DTAM — 2D-GS surfaces + normal-aware tracking → sub-2mm depth on live surgical scenes (our medical demo)

    A surgeon's endoscope is a nightmare for 3D reconstruction: the light source is bolted next to the camera so specular glare moves with you, tissue is nearly featureless and homogeneous, and it deforms as the patient breathes. Endo-2DTAM abandons fat 3D Gaussians for thin 2D Gaussian surfels — each one a tiny oriented disk sitting on the tissue surface — and adds normal-aware tracking that measures how much the tissue surface bends frame to frame. Co-located illumination is modeled physically (Lambertian + specular), not baked into color. The result: sub-2 mm depth reconstruction of live surgical tissue, accurate enough for a surgeon to navigate by.

    how it actually works

    The problem: 3D Gaussians are fat blobs with ambiguous surface normals. Endoscopic tissue is thin, deforming non-rigidly, and nearly textureless. Specular glare from the co-located light overwhelms texture and breaks traditional feature tracking.

    The mechanism: (1) Use 2D Gaussian surfels (not 3D blobs) — each one a thin disk with a well-defined normal vector. (2) Tracking: measure surface normals from Sobel gradients in the live endoscopic frame. Align the predicted normals (rendered from the current map) to observed normals via rotation, not intensity. (3) Separate illumination from geometry: model light as Lambertian + specular (Phong), and optimize the specularity separately from the tissue reflectance. (4) Allow blobs' positions and orientations to deform frame-to-frame (track non-rigid motion). (5) Prune invisible or redundant surfels to keep the map compact.

    Why it works: 2D surfels give honest surface representation with definite normals. Normal-based tracking is reliable to specular glare because normals are a geometric property. Physics-based lighting separation means the map stays stable as the endoscope moves and the light direction changes. Non-rigid deformation tracking handles anatomy motion.

  • IROS
    Reflectance-regularized endoscopy — an inverse-rendering reflectance term handles the endoscope's co-located illumination

    Endoscopic reconstruction tries to capture photorealistic tissue appearance, but the co-located light creates a moving specular hotspot that is baked into every blob's color if you are not careful. Reflectance-regularized endoscopy adds an inverse-rendering term: during training, the system learns not just blob position and color, but a reflectance model (albedo, roughness, specularity) for each blob and the light's position and intensity. A loss term penalizes unrealistic reflectance (e.g., specularity that jumps around), forcing the model to correctly separate what the tissue is made of from how the light hit it. The result: a map that transfers better to different lighting, and removes ghostly specular artifacts from the endoscopic capture.

    how it actually works

    The problem: If you optimize only for render-to-real-photo MSE, the network bakes specular glare into the blob colors. Move the light slightly, and the render looks wrong. Surgeons want a map of actual tissue reflectance, not specular glare.

    The mechanism: (1) Parameterize each blob's appearance as: (albedo, roughness) + (specular intensity, normal). (2) The light source has learnable position and intensity. (3) Render using a physically-based model: for each blob, compute Lambertian (diffuse) contribution = albedo × dot(normal, light direction), and Blinn-Phong specular = specularity × (dot(half-vec, normal))^roughness. (4) The main loss is render-vs-photo as usual. (5) Add regularization: penalize roughness that changes between frames (tissue properties are stable), penalize specular intensity above a threshold (tissue is mostly matte).

    Why it works: Inverse rendering decouples appearance from lighting. The regularizer encourages the model to explain variation as lighting changes, not tissue changes. The learned light position evolves smoothly, which acts as a check that the model is sensible.

  • IROS
    Stylized-GS endoscopy — GS-generated synthetic endoscopy enables a 5-minute sim-to-real domain transfer

    Deep-learning-based surgical navigation requires thousands of annotated endoscopic frames. Real data is scarce and expensive; synthetic data from game engines looks fake and does not transfer. Stylized-GS endoscopy generates synthetic endoscopy from real-world 3D Gaussian splat maps: record a real endoscopic video, fit a splat to it, then render that splat from different (unseen) viewpoints under different simulated lighting to create synthetic training data. Because the render is photorealistic (it learned from real photos), policies trained on it transfer to real endoscopy in five minutes of fine-tuning instead of the usual hours. The result: the first sim-to-real pipeline for surgical vision, closing the annotation gap.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Surgical datasets are small (hundreds of frames per patient). Game-engine renderers produce clearly synthetic images. Policies trained on them do not transfer to real endoscopy without expensive fine-tuning. How do you generate unlimited realistic surgical training data?

    The mechanism: (1) Capture a real endoscopic video of a surgical scene (e.g., a colon or a knee joint). (2) Fit a Gaussian splat to that video using the SLAM techniques above. (3) For data synthesis: sample random camera poses (positions and orientations the endoscope never actually visited). (4) Render the splat from each pose, using the learned appearance (albedo, specularity, normals). (5) Add synthetic variation: vary the light intensity and direction (simulated operating-room lights), add simulated blood or fluid on the lens (thin transparent Gaussians in front), and add annotation layers (segmentation, depth, tool masks).

    Why it works: The splat encodes the real photographic appearance learned from real frames. Rendering unseen viewpoints from it produces images that look real because they are built from real photos, not a game engine. The visual realism means the sim-to-real gap is small, so fine-tuning is brief.

  • CVPR
    Anisotropic GS for MRI — 2D Gaussian splatting for arbitrary-scale MRI super-resolution with anatomy guidance

    MRI produces thick slices — 5 mm thick or more — with severe undersampling perpendicular to the imaging plane. Reconstructing anatomy at true high resolution requires super-resolution: inferring what a thin-slice MRI would look like. Anisotropic Gaussian Splatting for MRI uses 2D surfels oriented along the MRI slice planes, and encodes anisotropic structure (what the anatomy looks like perpendicular to the slice) as an additional learnable field. Splatting this representation at higher resolution, guided by anatomical priors (MRI-specific regularization), produces super-resolved 3D MRI. The result: fine anatomical detail for surgical planning from a coarse MRI acquisition.

    how it actually works

    The problem: MRI slice spacing (5–10 mm) is much coarser than in-plane resolution (1 mm). Naive upsampling creates blocky or implausible anatomy. You need a model that learns what anatomy looks like between slices.

    The mechanism: (1) Initialize 2D surfels aligned with the MRI slice planes (XY-planes at each Z location). (2) For each surfel, learn not just in-plane (XY) color but also out-of-plane (Z) structure via a learned anisotropic covariance: the Z-axis is much larger than the XY axes. (3) Render at high in-plane resolution (multiple pixels per original voxel). The thick anisotropic covariance ensures inter-slice consistency. (4) Add anatomical regularization: penalize unrealistic organ shapes, enforce smoothness in certain regions. (5) Train on paired low/high resolution data (either real thin-slice MRIs or synthetic).

    Why it works: Anisotropic Gaussians encode directional bias. The fat Z-covariance naturally bridges the gap between thick slices by providing continuity. The anatomical prior prevents hallucination and grounds the super-resolution in plausible anatomy.

Creative capture & VFX / production

constrain the cloud
the problem

Film and advertising want real places and objects as 3D assets they can relight, refocus, and composite — captured in minutes, where classic photogrammetry meant weeks of shooting and cleanup. Here the new idea is not a change to the Gaussian at all; it is the pipeline around it.

the approaches

Film and advertising want real places and objects as editable, relightable 3D assets captured in minutes — where classic photogrammetry meant weeks of shooting and cleanup. Here the twist is not a new attribute on the Gaussian; it is the pipeline. A fast multi-camera capture is posed, trained into a splat, and dropped into a production renderer as one more 3D layer — with lens effects added at render time precisely because the splat is real explicit geometry the renderer can focus, defocus, and composite.

The viral VW “Think Small” Beetle scan is the clean example: five DJI Osmo Action cameras, a three-minute capture, aligned in RealityCapture, trained in Postshot, then rendered live in OctaneRender inside Cinema 4D. The depth-of-field you see is not in the scan — it is simulated in-render, and the car breaks out of the vintage ad frame because it is a second splat composited in front via Octane's AOV compositor. Every reconstruction, relighting, and feed-forward advance above is what keeps this pipeline getting faster and cleaner.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Capture fast: a small rig of five action cameras films the object for about three minutes.
  2. Align the footage into camera poses (RealityCapture) and train a splat of the object (Postshot).
  3. Load the splat into a production renderer (OctaneRender inside Cinema 4D) as one more 3D layer.
  4. Add lens effects at render time — the depth-of-field is simulated, not captured, precisely because the splat is real geometry the renderer can focus and defocus.
  5. Composite it: the VW Beetle is a second splat rendered in front of the vintage ad, breaking out of the frame — a real backyard scan turned into a finished shot.

    VW “Think Small” Beetle scan — 5× DJI Osmo Action 6 · 3-min capture → RealityCapture align → Postshot train → live in OctaneRender / Cinema 4D, depth-of-field simulated in-render, composited via AOV

    The VFX and advertising industries need real objects as editable 3D assets, but classic photogrammetry takes weeks. The unfamiliarity here is not a change to Gaussians at all; it is the end-to-end pipeline from fast multi-camera capture through aligned poses, trained blobs, and direct integration into a production renderer. In 18 minutes total — five action cameras, three-minute capture, RealityCapture alignment, Postshot training — a vintage Beetle is a usable, relightable, renderable 3D asset in Cinema 4D.

    how it actually works

    The problem: VFX pipelines need relit, refocused 3D assets fast; photogrammetry is weeks of manual cleanup.

    The mechanism: orchestrate a four-step pipeline. (1) Five DJI Osmo Action cameras record the object synchronously for three minutes from fixed standoff positions. (2) Feed the video frames to RealityCapture, which solves camera poses (structure-from-motion) and optionally produces a point-cloud scaffold. (3) Train a Gaussian splatting model on the frames with the computed poses fixed, using Postshot (feed-forward or optimization; this is Gaussian fitting). (4) Export the trained blob cloud in a format OctaneRender understands, and load it as a 3D layer inside Cinema 4D. Depth-of-field and lens effects are rendered by OctaneRender at output time, not baked into the capture.

    Why it works: because Gaussians render in real-time at high quality and are explicit geometry, they drop into a production renderer without special handling — the renderer treats them like any other 3D primitive, applying its own lens model and compositing on top. The speed gain comes from automation: one human loads a captured Gaussian into the timeline, no weeks of mesh cleanup or texture painting.

Part IV

Where it stands

Splatting is settled as the default way to turn photos into an explorable 3D scene. What is still hard:

Truly one-shot capture. Single-image and ultra-sparse reconstruction still lean on hallucinated priors that can invent geometry that isn’t there.
Watertight, simulation-ready meshes. Surfaces come out fast but not always closed and physical enough to drop straight into a simulator.
City scale. Kilometer-scale scenes need careful hierarchical, streaming engineering — memory is the wall.
Hard materials. Caustics, glass, subsurface scattering and true reflections still break the simple blob model.
Long, live video. Continuous online reconstruction over long horizons runs into a growing-memory problem.
Theory. There is still no clean account of why Gaussians work this well, or how to predict the right number of them up front.
