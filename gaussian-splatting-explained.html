The core idea: the primitive · rendering · learning · view-dependence · why it won
Part I

The core idea, from first principles

Five ideas, each drawn as a live diagram you can watch run, then said plainly. Together they are the whole method — the primitive, how it renders, how it learns, how it stays photographic, and why it runs in real time.

01 · the primitive

What a scene is made of: soft colored blobs

Start with the thing itself. A Gaussian-splatting scene is not a mesh of triangles and not a grid of voxels. It is a cloud of little soft blobs floating in space. Each blob is a 3D Gaussian — a smooth bell-curve of color that is dense and bright at its center and fades to nothing at its edge.

Every blob carries exactly four things: a position (where its center is in 3D), a covariance (how big it is and which way it is stretched — a squashed, tilted ellipsoid, not just a sphere), an opacity (how solid versus see-through it is), and a color. That is the whole vocabulary. A blurry wall is a few fat blobs; a crisp edge is many thin ones lined up.

Why blobs and not triangles? Because a triangle either covers a pixel or it does not — a hard yes/no you cannot nudge smoothly. A soft blob has a gentle falloff you can nudge, a hair bigger, a shade redder, a touch more solid. That smoothness is the entire trick, and the next three sections are just consequences of it.

02 · rendering

Turning blobs into a picture: splat, sort, blend

Now make a picture from the cloud. Pick a camera. Every 3D blob casts a 2D shadow on the image — a little elliptical smudge. That projection step is the splat: the blob is splatted flat onto the screen, keeping its soft edge.

For any single pixel, several blobs might overlap it, at different depths. So the blobs are sorted from near to far, and their colors are blended front-to-back. A blob near the camera paints its color and uses up some of the light; whatever is behind only shows through the fraction still left. That bookkeeping is one line — C = Σ ci αi Πj<i(1−αj) — color equals each blob's color times its opacity times how much light survived everything nearer.

The reason this matters: projecting and blending are both smooth, differentiable, and embarrassingly parallel. A GPU can splat millions of blobs for a full frame in a few milliseconds — and because every step is smooth, you can measure exactly how a tiny change to any blob would change the final picture. Hold onto that; it is what makes learning possible.

03 · learning

How the blobs learn the scene: photos in, gradients out

You never place the blobs by hand. You give the system a few dozen ordinary photos of a scene, each with its camera position, and it fits the cloud to them. Render the blobs from photo #1's viewpoint, subtract from the real photo #1, and you have an error image. Because rendering was differentiable, that error tells you which direction to push every blob's position, size, opacity, and color to make the render look more like the photo. Repeat over all the photos, thousands of times. This is ordinary gradient descent — the same engine that trains neural nets — but here the thing being trained is the blob cloud itself.

Gradient descent alone can only adjust the blobs it already has; it cannot decide the cloud needs more blobs here and fewer there. So splatting adds a second move called adaptive density control. Where a region stays blurry and the gradients are large, a blob is cloned or split to add detail. Where a blob has drifted nearly transparent and is doing no work, it is pruned away. The cloud grows dense exactly where the scene is complicated and stays sparse on flat walls.

Put together, a scene of 10 to 50 casual photos becomes a few million blobs in minutes, and the result renders in real time forever after. No network is queried at render time — the scene simply is the blobs.

04 · view-dependence

Color that changes as you move: spherical harmonics

A matte wall looks the same from anywhere, but almost nothing else does. Polished paint, a wet floor, a metal rim — their brightness and even hue shift with where you stand. If each blob had a single fixed color, a moving camera would look flat and dead.

So a blob's color is stored as a tiny function of direction instead of a constant. The tool for that is spherical harmonics — think of them as a compact set of dials that describe “from this angle the blob is a bit brighter and warmer, from that angle a bit darker.” The lowest dial is just the flat base color a matte surface needs; the higher dials add the gloss and the moving highlight a shiny surface needs.

This is why a Gaussian-splat scene feels photographic rather than cardboard as you fly through it: every blob is quietly re-tinting itself for your current viewpoint.

05 · why it won

Why it runs in real time — and NeRF didn’t

The technique splatting dethroned was NeRF. NeRF represents a scene as one neural network: to color a pixel you shoot a ray into the scene and ask the network “what is here?” at dozens or hundreds of points along that ray, then add them up. It is beautiful and it works — but every pixel of every frame costs hundreds of network evaluations, so rendering is slow and training takes hours.

Gaussian splatting makes the opposite bet. Instead of hiding the scene inside a network you must interrogate point by point, it writes the scene down explicitly as the blobs and simply projects them. There is no per-point network call at render time — just sort and blend. The same realism NeRF took seconds per frame to produce, splatting produces at 100-plus frames per second, and trains in minutes rather than hours.

That single swap — implicit-and-queried for explicit-and-rasterized — is the whole reason the field pivoted. And it buys a bonus that turns out to matter enormously for robotics: because the scene is now explicit geometry you can touch, you can edit it, attach meaning to individual blobs, hand pieces to a physics engine, or drop new objects in. Everything in Part III grows from that.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every application below is chasing one or more of these five payoffs — the recurring reasons the field switched to splatting, in the words the papers keep using.

Real-time rendering

No per-point network to query — sort and blend the explicit blobs. Robotics papers cite 100+ fps and 20–50× faster than NeRF-based methods, fast enough to track a live camera against the map.

Explicit, editable geometry

The scene is the primitives, so you can move, delete, segment, or add blobs. This is what lets manipulation work edit a scene, sample grasps on it, or hand it to a physics solver.

Photographic accuracy

Trained directly against photos with a smooth loss, splats hit 40+ dB reconstruction quality and sub-2 mm depth in surgical scenes — realistic enough that policies trained on renders transfer to real hardware.

Minutes, not hours

Differentiable rasterization plus adaptive density control converges a scene from a few dozen photos in minutes, and some feed-forward variants now predict the blobs in a single pass.

Shrinks to the edge

Voxel-merging, quantization and pruning cut millions of blobs down to fit a drone or a phone without visibly hurting the render — the reason splatting reaches real robots at all.

Part III

Every way the field is bending it

The explicit-blob representation turned out to be a substrate you can twist in dozens of directions — but under the variety there are really just two moves.

move A — add an attribute. Bolt an extra channel onto every Gaussian beyond color and opacity: a semantic feature (so you can query the scene in words), a motion (so it can move over time), a material (so it can be relit). The cloud stays a cloud; each blob just carries more.

move B — constrain the cloud. Leave the blobs alone but pin or reshape the whole cloud — onto a surface or SDF, to a sensor's physics, to a body rig, to a physics engine, or to known geometry like a road. You change what the cloud is allowed to be.

Sixteen families below, each with how the twist works and real papers — robotics (ICRA / IROS / CoRL / RSS) and computer vision (CVPR 2026). Each family is tagged with its dominant move.

01

Live SLAM & dense mapping

constrain the cloud
the problem

A robot walking into an unknown building has to do two things at once, and each one needs the other. It has to figure out where its camera is — but it can only do that against a map. And it has to build that map — but it can only place things correctly if it knows where the camera is. It starts with neither.

the approaches

Vanilla splatting assumes you already know every camera pose and have all the photos up front. A robot exploring a building has neither: frames arrive one at a time and the camera's position is exactly what you're trying to find. The move is to run two optimizations against the same blob cloud, alternating. Track: freeze the map and solve for the new frame's pose by sliding the virtual camera until its rendered view matches the incoming photo — the photometric error is the tracking signal. Map: freeze the pose and grow/adjust blobs to fold the new frame in.

Because the render is real-time, this tracking-by-rendering closes the loop fast enough to keep up with a moving robot — the thing NeRF-based SLAM could never do. The two hard parts are both about the explicit cloud: it grows without bound (a memory problem) and small pose errors accumulate into drift, so much of the work here is merging, pruning, and bundle-adjusting the blobs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Frame 47 arrives. Best first guess for where the camera is: wherever frame 46 ended up.
  2. Render the half-built blob map from that guessed viewpoint. It comes out shifted about 12 pixels off from the real photo.
  3. Slide the guessed pose a little at a time until that shift shrinks to almost nothing. Where the render finally lines up with the photo is the camera’s true position — you found it by matching, not by any external sensor.
  4. Now trust that pose and fold the frame in: the far wall is still blurry in the map, so clone blobs there and tune their color until the render matches.
  5. Frame 48 arrives; repeat. Every few seconds, merge duplicate blobs so the map’s memory doesn’t grow without end.
  • ICRAGaussian-LIC — seeds blobs from LiDAR + triangulated points, adds a sky model + exposure comp; C++/CUDA — beats methods that use ground-truth poses
  • IROSMemGS — merges redundant blobs in voxel space by geometric similarity + patch-grid init → cuts GPU memory without losing quality (memory is the SLAM wall)
  • ICRAMGSO — a photometric SLAM front-end seeds a dense point cloud to initialize the blobs → far fewer Gaussians, runs on a laptop
  • IROSCGS-SLAM — sliding-window masking prunes ellipsoids + a geometry codebook quantizes their attributes → compact map, local-to-global bundle adjustment
  • CVPRODGS-SLAM — first omnidirectional (360°) splatting SLAM
02

Self-driving & street-scene reconstruction

constrain the cloud
the problem

You want to rebuild a whole street from a car’s cameras so you can replay the drive, test a self-driving stack in it, or edit it. Two things make a street different from a room: it is full of other cars that move, and you only ever saw each surface from the single lane you happened to drive in — never the far side of anything.

the approaches

A street is not one static scene: it is a rigid world plus cars that move, filmed from a car that only ever sees each surface from one side. Fit one blob cloud naively and the moving cars smear into streaks while the unseen backs of objects fill with garbage. So the cloud is split — static background versus dynamic actors — and each half is constrained differently.

The static blobs get pinned to physical structure so they don't float: road and sky are forced flat and consistent across views, and LiDAR depth anchors where blobs are allowed to sit. Each moving vehicle gets its own pose track over time plus a color that is allowed to change frame-to-frame. The payoff is a replayable, editable digital twin of a drive — you can pull a car out, add one, or re-render the whole street from a lane that never existed.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Sort every blob into two piles: the rigid world (road, buildings, sky) and the movers (other vehicles).
  2. Pin the world blobs to a flat road plane and a distant sky so they cannot float or warp as the viewpoint changes.
  3. Anchor their depth with the LiDAR: a wall sits exactly where the laser says it does, not where color alone guesses.
  4. Give the car ahead its own blobs with a pose that slides frame to frame — so it drives cleanly across the scene instead of smearing into a streak.
  5. You never saw that car’s left side. Mirror its right side onto a template to fill the unseen half plausibly.
  6. Now the drive is editable: delete a car, add one, or re-render the whole street from a lane you never actually drove.
  • ICRAAutoSplat — geometry constraints on road/sky blobs; a “reflected Gaussian consistency” trick supervises a car's unseen far side via a mirrored 3D template; per-object residual spherical harmonics model changing appearance
  • IROSGS-SDF — trains a neural signed-distance field on LiDAR as a geometry scaffold, then initializes + regularizes the blobs against it so geometry doesn't drift
  • CVPRGaussianDWM — a driving “world model” — the Gaussian scene encodes space + language and predicts what happens next
  • CVPRRaGS — fuses 4D radar + a single camera into blobs for 3D detection when vision alone fails
  • CVPRGau-Occ — geometry-completed Gaussians fuse camera/radar/LiDAR into 3D occupancy
03

Manipulation & world models

add an attribute
the problem

Before a robot can pick up ‘the mug’, two things have to be true that a plain 3D scan cannot give it. It has to know which blobs are the mug. And it has to be able to picture the scene after it moves something, so it can check a plan before its arm commits to it.

the approaches

To pick something up a robot needs two things a raw blob cloud lacks: meaning (“which blobs are the mug's handle?”) and editability (“if I move this, what does the scene become?”). The first move is to hang an extra vector on every Gaussian — a distilled CLIP/feature embedding — so the scene becomes queryable in words and graspable by part. The second is to treat the explicit cloud as a scene you can rewrite, so the robot can simulate the consequence of an action before taking it.

The most striking versions fuse the render with physics: keep a twin set of particles for the dynamics and a set of Gaussians for the look, and let the mismatch between predicted and observed images push the particles around like a visual force. Now the same representation both renders and predicts how the world will move.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scan the table into blobs. Onto each blob, hang the feature vector a vision model (CLIP) produces for that spot in the image.
  2. Say ‘mug’. Turn the word into the same kind of feature vector, and light up the blobs whose feature is closest. That glowing cluster is the mug — found by meaning, not by a label you pre-defined.
  3. Read a grasp off its geometry: the blob shape tells you where the handle is and how the gripper should close.
  4. Rehearse before acting: edit the cloud — delete the mug’s blobs from the shelf, place them at the goal — and look at the result to sanity-check the plan.
  5. Stay honest (physics version): keep shadow particles under the blobs; if the render drifts from the live camera, that gap acts like a force that nudges the particles back into agreement with reality.
  • CoRLSplat-MOVER — three modules — ASK-Splat distills semantics+affordances into the cloud, SEE-Splat edits the scene live via 3D masks, Grasp-Splat samples grasps on the edited scene → true multi-stage tasks
  • CoRLGraspSplats — depth-supervised blobs; samples grasps by point-tracking (no differentiable rendering); 60s capture; beats NeRF baselines F3RM & LERF-TOGO on part manipulation
  • CoRLPhysically Embodied GS — dual particle+Gaussian — particles do physics, Gaussians render; a “visual force” from render-vs-observation error corrects the particles → predict the future AND stay synced to reality
  • CoRLCloth-Splatting — mesh-backed Gaussians make cloth state differentiable straight from RGB → 85% faster convergence
  • CVPRGeoPredict — couples Gaussian geometry with predicted kinematics for precise vision-language-action manipulation
04

Photoreal simulators & sim-to-real

constrain the cloud
the problem

A policy that flies a drone or works a hand has to see a huge number of realistic images to learn. Collecting them on a real robot is slow and expensive, and the cheap fix — a game-engine simulator — produces images too plasticky to fool a real camera, so the policy that aced the sim falls apart in the real world.

the approaches

Learned policies are hungry for millions of photorealistic training views, but a real robot produces them slowly and expensively. Classic simulators are fast but plasticky, and a policy trained on cartoon renders falls apart the moment it meets a real camera. The move: capture the real place once as a splat, then render unlimited novel viewpoints and poses inside a physics engine — the render is photographic enough that the policy transfers with no fine-tuning.

This is the cleanest example of splatting's real-time-plus-explicit combo paying off: the scene is realistic like a photo but cheap to re-render like a game, and because it is explicit geometry you can drop it into MuJoCo and let physics act on it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Fly through the real room once, filming as you go. Train a splat of it — a few minutes of work.
  2. Drop the splat into a physics simulator. Now the room looks like a photograph and you can act inside it.
  3. Spawn thousands of practice runs from thousands of starting points, each rendering the real-looking room from a fresh angle. Once the splat exists, each new view is nearly free.
  4. Train the policy on all those renders.
  5. Put it on the real drone. Because every training image already looked real, the policy flies on the first try — no real-world fine-tuning needed.
  • IROSDISCOVERSE — 3DGS appearance + MuJoCo physics in one framework → the first 3DGS real2sim2real, state-of-the-art zero-shot imitation transfer
  • IROSGRaD-Nav — renders the blobs inside a differentiable RL sim + a runtime context-estimator → sample-efficient, zero-shot drone gate-flying
  • CoRLGS→flight + Liquid nets — full-stack protocol: GS sim + liquid neural networks → transfers to a real quadrotor outdoors
  • CVPRVideo2Robo — turns ONE monocular video into 3DGS synthetic training data for scalable robot learning
05

Dynamic & 4D scenes

add an attribute
the problem

A splat freezes a single instant. But real scenes move — a person waves, a car passes, a heart beats — and if you fit one frozen cloud to a moving thing, all you get is a blur where the motion was. The scene needs a sense of time.

the approaches

A static cloud cannot hold a waving hand, a passing car, or a beating heart. So time becomes a new attribute: each blob gains either a deformation (where it sits at time t) or an explicit temporal extent, turning the cloud into a 4D reconstruction you can scrub through.

The catch is stability — give blobs free rein over time and they flicker and over-fit the motion. So the research is mostly about regularizing what is allowed to move: deciding which points are dynamic, how long a blob should live, and tying motion to sparse flow so it stays coherent.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Film a video instead of taking stills, so the same surface is seen at many different moments.
  2. Give each blob a position that depends on time — a little path through space, not a fixed dot.
  3. Separate the still from the moving: score each blob’s ‘am I moving?’ and let the wall blobs stay put — cheaper, and sharper.
  4. Smooth the movers: tie their motion to the sparse optical flow between frames so a hand glides instead of jittering.
  5. Give each a lifespan — when it should appear and vanish — so a hand that leaves the frame doesn’t leave a ghost behind.
  6. Scrub the time slider and the whole scene replays, in 3D, from any angle.
  • IROSEmbracing Dynamics (D4DGS-SLAM) — literal 4D Gaussians (x,y,z,t); an InfoModule scores each point's dynamics/visibility/reliability to filter unstable movers → first 4DGS SLAM
  • CVPRSharpTimeGS — a learnable “lifespan” per blob controls when it exists → sharp, stable motion instead of smear
  • CVPRMoRGS — per-Gaussian motion with light sparse-flow supervision → streamable dynamic scenes
  • CVPRReFlow — learns 3D motion directly from video via 3D↔2D consistency, no external tracker

Human avatars, faces & bodies

constrain the cloud
the problem

You want a photoreal person you can re-pose, re-light, and make speak — built from a short video. But a raw cloud of blobs of someone standing still has no idea what an ‘elbow’ or a ‘smile’ is, so there is no handle to grab to make it move.

the approaches

You want a person you can re-pose, re-light, and make talk — from a few views or one video. A free blob cloud has no notion of “elbow” or “smile,” so it cannot be driven. The move is to bind the Gaussians to a body-and-face rig: as the underlying skeleton or expression model moves, the blobs are carried along by linear blend skinning, the same weighting scheme that drives game characters.

The remaining art is where detail concentrates — creases, specular skin, hair — so several methods find the high-motion or high-detail regions and pour extra blobs there.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Fit a standard body-and-face rig to the video — a skeleton with joints, plus dials for expression.
  2. Attach each blob to the nearest bone(s), with a weight saying how much each bone owns it.
  3. Bend the elbow bone. Every blob on the forearm rotates with it; a blob straddling the joint blends the two bones’ motion, so the crease folds like real skin instead of tearing.
  4. Add detail where motion is fast: a waving hand or moving lips need more blobs, so pour extra ones into those regions.
  5. Drive the rig with any new pose or speech and the photoreal surface follows — the same skin, animated.
  • IROSSTG-Avatar — linear-blend-skinning for skeletal control + Spacetime Gaussians; optical flow finds high-motion regions and densifies there → real-time animatable
  • CVPRHumanNOVA — a photoreal, drivable avatar from a single image in under a second
  • CVPRHyperGaussians — higher-dimensional Gaussian conditioning recovers fine facial detail
  • CVPRLarge-scale Codec Avatars — million-scale avatar pretraining resolves the fidelity-vs-generalization trade-off

Generative 3D — text/image/video to scene

constrain the cloud
the problem

Reconstruction needs photographs of a place that exists. But sometimes there is no place — you want to conjure a whole 3D scene from a sentence or a single picture, where almost every viewpoint you’d need simply does not exist and never will.

the approaches

Reconstruction needs photos of a place that exists; generation conjures a scene from a prompt or a single image, where almost every viewpoint is simply missing. The move is to borrow a 2D or video diffusion model — which has already learned what worlds look like — to hallucinate the missing views, then fit or directly predict Gaussians from them. Dream the pictures, then solidify them into blobs.

Because the diffusion prior is doing the imagining, the same trick scales from a single object to a whole zoomable world, with the Gaussian layer providing the consistent, real-time-renderable 3D substrate underneath.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Start with a prompt (‘a mossy ruin’) or one lonely image.
  2. Ask a diffusion model to imagine the missing views. It has seen millions of images and knows what worlds look like, so it can dream what this scene would look like from other angles.
  3. Treat those dreamed images as your photos, even though no camera ever took them.
  4. Fit a blob cloud that agrees with all of them (or train a network to predict the blobs directly).
  5. Fly through the result: the blobs give a consistent, real-time 3D scene — solidified out of a machine’s imagination.
  • CVPRDIFF4SPLAT — repurposes a video-diffusion model to emit a dynamic Gaussian scene in one forward pass
  • CVPRWonderZoom — scale-adaptive surfels reveal new detail as you zoom into a generated world
  • CVPRGaussianZoom — VLM-guided progressive zoom-in adds semantically-correct detail
  • CVPRCUPID — jointly predicts the 3D object and the camera pose via flow matching

Language-embedded, open-vocabulary semantics

add an attribute
the problem

A finished splat map is millions of colored blobs with no labels on them. A robot told ‘go to the kitchen sink’ can do nothing with raw color — and you cannot solve this by pre-labeling every object, because you can’t list in advance everything it might ever be asked to find.

the approaches

A map of blobs is meaningless to a planner — “go to the kitchen sink” needs the blobs labelled, and you can't enumerate every possible object in advance. The move: give each Gaussian an extra feature vector, trained to match the features a 2D foundation model (CLIP, SAM, DINO) produces, rendered exactly the way color is. Then a natural-language query becomes nearest-feature lookup — open-vocabulary, no fixed class list.

Done naively this is enormous — a 512-dimensional vector bolted onto millions of blobs — so the real engineering is compression: encoding semantics as compact ids or hierarchies instead of raw embeddings.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. As you build the map, run each camera frame through a vision-language model (CLIP, SAM) that turns image regions into feature vectors — numbers that capture ‘what this looks like’.
  2. Paint those features onto the blobs, rendered the very same way color is, so every blob ends up carrying a foundation model’s sense of what it is.
  3. Later, say ‘sink’. Turn that word into the same kind of feature vector.
  4. Light up the blobs whose feature is nearest the word’s. That cluster is the sink — no class list was ever needed (this is what ‘open-vocabulary’ means).
  5. Make it fit: a 512-number tag on every blob is enormous, so compress it — a short hierarchical id, or a 2-byte code — and the semantic map still runs on the robot.
  • IROSOVSG-SLAM — SAM + VLM embeddings into the cloud with an identity-aware encoding + identity loss to cut ambiguity and cost; open-vocab, no closed set
  • ICRAHier-SLAM — encodes semantics as a compact hierarchical categorical code (built by an LLM) → 500+ classes, 2× faster, less storage
  • CVPRLightSplat — open-vocabulary understanding via compact 2-byte masks, in five seconds
  • CVPRMLLMSplat — drives a 2D multimodal-LLM to understand, generate, and edit the 3DGS without retraining

Surfaces & meshes out of blobs

constrain the cloud
the problem

Blobs are wonderful to look at, but together they are a fuzzy, overlapping fog with no crisp surface. Physics, collision-checking, and CAD all need an actual surface with a clear ‘this side faces out’ — and a fat 3D blob doesn’t have one: ask which way it faces and there is no single answer.

the approaches

Blobs are wonderful to look at but a fuzzy, overlapping cloud has no crisp surface — and physics, collision, and CAD all need one. Worse, a fat 3D Gaussian has an ambiguous surface normal, so “which way does this bit of wall face?” has no clean answer. Two moves fix it. Flatten each 3D Gaussian into an oriented 2D disk (2D Gaussian splatting) so its normal is well-defined and the disks tile a surface like scales; or tie the whole cloud to a signed-distance field that defines the true surface and pulls the blobs onto it.

The reward is a mesh with correct normals you can drop into a simulator or a game engine — the bridge from “pretty render” to “usable geometry.”

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Squash each fuzzy 3D blob flat into a thin oriented disk — a 2D Gaussian.
  2. A flat disk has exactly one facing direction (its normal), so ‘which way is out’ finally has a clean answer.
  3. Lay millions of these disks so they tile the real surface like fish scales.
  4. (Alternative) tie the cloud to a signed-distance field — a function giving the distance to the surface from anywhere — and let it pull stray blobs onto the true surface.
  5. Read off a mesh with correct normals you can drop straight into a simulator or a game engine.
  • IROSEndo-2DTAM — 2D-Gaussian surfels + surface-normal-aware tracking → sub-2mm depth on surgical tissue (we ran this one)
  • IROSGS-SDF — a neural SDF on LiDAR acts as a manifold field that regularizes the blobs into consistent geometry
  • ICRAVSS-SLAM — voxelized surfels decoded from per-voxel feature vectors → removes redundant primitives, enforces regular surfaces
  • IROSMesh-Learner — puts learnable spherical-harmonic textures on a real mesh, rasterized normally; off-screen textures swap to CPU RAM → unlimited scale, Blender-native

Sparse-view, few-shot & feed-forward

constrain the cloud
the problem

Normal splatting wants dozens of overlapping photos and a fresh optimization for every new scene, taking minutes. Often you only have three or four views — or you want an answer the instant a photo arrives, with no waiting at all.

the approaches

Vanilla 3DGS wants dozens of overlapping photos and a per-scene optimization taking minutes; often you have three or four views, or want an answer instantly. Two moves. Inject priors — learned depth, geometric warps, pseudo-views — so a handful of images still pins the cloud down. Or go fully feed-forward: train one network that predicts the entire blob cloud in a single pass, with no per-scene fitting at all.

Feed-forward is the bigger shift: it turns splatting from an optimization you run per scene into a function you evaluate once, which is what makes on-the-fly and single-image reconstruction plausible.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. With only 3–4 views, inject outside knowledge: a learned depth guess, geometric warps, a few invented in-between views — enough hints to pin the cloud down.
  2. Or skip per-scene fitting entirely: train ONE network across many scenes to look at a handful of images and directly output a blob cloud.
  3. At use time, feed it the images. In a single forward pass — no optimization — it emits the whole cloud.
  4. The shift is conceptual: splatting stops being ‘run an optimization each time’ and becomes ‘evaluate a function once’ — which is what makes single-image and on-the-fly reconstruction possible.
  • CVPRSR3R — a feed-forward net predicts Gaussians directly from low-res sparse views, skipping optimization entirely
  • CVPRTWINGS — thin-plate-spline warps align sparse views to give the cloud a good starting shape
  • CVPRHeroGS — stacks pseudo-dense labels + geometric-consistency guidance for robust 3–4-view reconstruction
  • CVPRFeed-forward head registration — predicts an avatar's Gaussians in one pass for a 10× speedup

Materials, lighting & relighting

add an attribute
the problem

When you train a splat, the lighting of the room you filmed gets baked into every blob’s color. So you cannot move the sun, or drop that object into a new scene with different light, without it looking wrong — the old highlights are painted on for good.

the approaches

A trained splat bakes the capture lighting into each blob's color — so you cannot move the sun, or drop the object into a new scene with new light, without it looking wrong. The move is inverse rendering: split each Gaussian's appearance into material (albedo, roughness) and illumination, so lighting becomes a separate factor you can change afterward.

This is strictly harder than plain reconstruction — you're asking one set of photos to reveal both what things are made of and how they were lit — so these methods lean on extra physics (path tracing, polarization) or 2D material priors to break the ambiguity.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Ask more of each blob: instead of one final color, store what it is MADE of (albedo, roughness) separately from the LIGHT that happened to fall on it.
  2. To render, combine the two: material × illumination = the color you see.
  3. Now swap the illumination — move the light, change its color — and re-combine. The highlight slides to the correct new place instead of staying painted where it was.
  4. Break the ambiguity with physics: one set of photos revealing both material and light at once is hard, so these methods add path tracing or polarization cues to pull the two apart.
  5. Relight it anywhere, like an ordinary 3D asset.
  • CVPRGHPT — differentiable Gaussian rendering + GPU path tracing → relightable at 113 fps
  • CVPRMatSpray — fuses a 2D diffusion model's material (PBR) knowledge onto the 3D blobs
  • CVPRPhyGaP — uses polarization cues to separate reflection and recover material
  • CVPRSGS-Intrinsic — semantic-aware Gaussians disentangle material vs illumination from sparse indoor views

Beyond RGB — event, thermal, radar, touch

constrain the cloud
the problem

An ordinary camera fails exactly where robots most need to see — in the dark, at high speed where frames smear, in smoke, or on glass. But splatting never actually required RGB pixels; it required a rule for turning a blob into a measurement. Rewrite that one rule and the whole machine keeps working.

the approaches

An ordinary camera dies in the dark, at high speed, in smoke, or on transparent objects. But splatting doesn't actually require RGB — it requires a differentiable observation model, the rule that turns a blob into a measurement. Rewrite that rule for another sensor's physics and the whole machine still works.

So an event camera's asynchronous brightness spikes, a thermal camera's emitted radiation, a radar's returns, or a fingertip's contact each get their own forward model, and the blobs are optimized against that signal instead of pixels.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Keep the blob cloud unchanged.
  2. Replace the observation model — the step saying ‘a blob at this depth and brightness produces THIS measurement’ — with another sensor’s physics.
  3. For an event camera, the measurement is a stream of brightness-change spikes; optimize the blobs to reproduce the spikes instead of pixels — and you can now reconstruct straight through fast motion.
  4. For a thermal camera, give each blob a temperature and emissivity and model heat radiation; the blobs learn from warmth, not color.
  5. Same optimizer, same blobs — only the forward model changed.
  • IROSEvent3DGS — reconstructs from an event camera's brightness spikes → +3dB and 95% less compute under high-speed motion
  • CVPRPhysIR-Splat — assigns a temperature + emissivity to each blob with full thermal radiative-transfer physics
  • IROSFusionSense — fuses vision + touch + priors to reconstruct transparent/reflective objects vision alone can't see
  • CVPR3DGS from spike streams — pose-free reconstruction straight from an event camera, no depth sensor

Compression & on-device speed

constrain the cloud
the problem

A single room comes out as a few million blobs. That is far too much memory and bandwidth for the machines robots actually are — a drone, a headset, a phone. The picture is beautiful; the file is impossible.

the approaches

A single room is millions of blobs — too much memory and bandwidth for a drone, a headset, or a phone, which is where robots actually live. The move is to shrink the cloud without visibly hurting the render: merge near-duplicate blobs, quantize their attributes into small codebooks, prune the ones that contribute no visible light, and cut the training time.

This is unglamorous but decisive — it is the difference between a splat that lives in a datacenter and one that runs on the machine in front of you.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Find near-duplicate blobs — same spot, same color — and merge each group into one.
  2. Quantize the survivors: instead of full-precision numbers, snap their attributes to a small shared codebook of values.
  3. Prune the invisible: blobs so faint they add no visible light are deleted outright.
  4. Check the render still looks the same. It does — because you removed only redundancy, not detail.
  5. A cloud that was millions of blobs now fits on the device in front of you.
  • CVPRFastGS — trains a full scene in under 100 seconds
  • CVPRTurbo-GS — dilated rendering sidesteps the expensive depth-sort bottleneck
  • IROSMemGS — voxel-space merging of redundant blobs shrinks the GPU footprint (also the SLAM workhorse)
  • IROSCGS-SLAM — geometry-codebook quantization compresses the per-blob attributes

Localization, navigation & safety on GS maps

constrain the cloud
the problem

Once you HAVE a splat map, a robot still has to answer two live questions: where am I inside this map right now, and how do I move through it without hitting anything? The map’s realism and its explicit shape are exactly what make both answerable.

the approaches

Once you have a splat map, a robot still has to answer two questions: where am I in it, and how do I move without hitting anything? The map's photorealism helps the first — you can localize by rendering candidate viewpoints and sliding the pose until the render matches the live camera (analysis-by-synthesis). Its explicit geometry helps the second: because a blob has a shape, you can compute how much a proposed path overlaps the cloud and treat that overlap as a differentiable collision cost.

That makes planning and even formal safety filtering run on the map itself, in real time — the kind of thing NeRF's slow implicit geometry made impractical.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. To localize, guess a camera pose and render the map from it.
  2. Compare that render to the live camera and nudge the guess until they match; try a few guesses, keep the best — that is ‘you are here’.
  3. To move safely, propose a path.
  4. Because every blob has a real shape, measure how much the path overlaps the blobs — that overlap is a differentiable collision cost.
  5. Push the path to drive that cost to zero (or hard-block any motion that would raise it) — all on the live map, in real time.
  • IROSGSplatLoc — distills keypoint descriptors into the blobs, then refines the pose photometrically
  • IROSLoGS — builds a structure-from-motion Gaussian map, PnP for a coarse pose, refine on the render — with fewer images
  • IROSFOCI — optimizes a trajectory directly against a differentiable Gaussian-overlap collision cost
  • IROSCBF on GS maps — a control-barrier-function safety filter on the live map — 20–50× faster than NeRF-based safety

Medicine — surgery, endoscopy, MRI

constrain the cloud
the problem

A surgeon’s endoscope breaks every assumption plain splatting likes, all at once: the light is bolted right beside the camera (so glare moves with you), the tissue is wet and textureless, and it deforms as the patient breathes. Get it right and you can rebuild the anatomy to sub-millimeter accuracy.

the approaches

A surgeon's endoscope is a moving camera with its light source rigidly bolted alongside, staring at wet, textureless, deforming tissue. Every assumption plain splatting likes — fixed lighting, static scene, rich texture — is violated at once. The move is to put the missing physics back: model the co-located light so specular glare is explained rather than baked in, use 2D-Gaussian surfaces for honest sub-millimeter depth, and add a deformation field for the non-rigid anatomy.

The stakes make the accuracy worth it — this is the difference between a pretty reconstruction and one a surgeon could navigate by.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Model the co-located light: because the lamp is fixed to the camera, the bright specular spot is predictable — explain it physically instead of baking it into the tissue’s color.
  2. Use flat 2D-Gaussian surfaces (not fat blobs) so the depth of the tissue wall is honest.
  3. Add a deformation field so the surface can flex frame to frame as the anatomy moves.
  4. Track the scope and map the tissue together — it is SLAM, performed inside a body.
  5. Out comes a sub-2mm reconstruction a surgeon could navigate by.
  • IROSEndo-2DTAM — 2D-GS surfaces + normal-aware tracking → sub-2mm depth on live surgical scenes (our medical demo)
  • IROSReflectance-regularized endoscopy — an inverse-rendering reflectance term handles the endoscope's co-located illumination
  • IROSStylized-GS endoscopy — GS-generated synthetic endoscopy enables a 5-minute sim-to-real domain transfer
  • CVPRAnisotropic GS for MRI — 2D Gaussian splatting for arbitrary-scale MRI super-resolution with anatomy guidance

Creative capture & VFX / production

constrain the cloud
the problem

Film and advertising want real places and objects as 3D assets they can relight, refocus, and composite — captured in minutes, where classic photogrammetry meant weeks of shooting and cleanup. Here the new idea is not a change to the Gaussian at all; it is the pipeline around it.

the approaches

Film and advertising want real places and objects as editable, relightable 3D assets captured in minutes — where classic photogrammetry meant weeks of shooting and cleanup. Here the twist is not a new attribute on the Gaussian; it is the pipeline. A fast multi-camera capture is posed, trained into a splat, and dropped into a production renderer as one more 3D layer — with lens effects added at render time precisely because the splat is real explicit geometry the renderer can focus, defocus, and composite.

The viral VW “Think Small” Beetle scan is the clean example: five DJI Osmo Action cameras, a three-minute capture, aligned in RealityCapture, trained in Postshot, then rendered live in OctaneRender inside Cinema 4D. The depth-of-field you see is not in the scan — it is simulated in-render, and the car breaks out of the vintage ad frame because it is a second splat composited in front via Octane's AOV compositor. Every reconstruction, relighting, and feed-forward advance above is what keeps this pipeline getting faster and cleaner.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Capture fast: a small rig of five action cameras films the object for about three minutes.
  2. Align the footage into camera poses (RealityCapture) and train a splat of the object (Postshot).
  3. Load the splat into a production renderer (OctaneRender inside Cinema 4D) as one more 3D layer.
  4. Add lens effects at render time — the depth-of-field is simulated, not captured, precisely because the splat is real geometry the renderer can focus and defocus.
  5. Composite it: the VW Beetle is a second splat rendered in front of the vintage ad, breaking out of the frame — a real backyard scan turned into a finished shot.

Part IV

Where it stands

Splatting is settled as the default way to turn photos into an explorable 3D scene. What is still hard:

Truly one-shot capture. Single-image and ultra-sparse reconstruction still lean on hallucinated priors that can invent geometry that isn’t there.
Watertight, simulation-ready meshes. Surfaces come out fast but not always closed and physical enough to drop straight into a simulator.
City scale. Kilometer-scale scenes need careful hierarchical, streaming engineering — memory is the wall.
Hard materials. Caustics, glass, subsurface scattering and true reflections still break the simple blob model.
Long, live video. Continuous online reconstruction over long horizons runs into a growing-memory problem.
Theory. There is still no clean account of why Gaussians work this well, or how to predict the right number of them up front.
