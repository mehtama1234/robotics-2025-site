Autonomous driving — the real problem

What a self-driving stack is really up against, stage by stage — the industry that lives it, the crashes when it fails, how it is actually solved, and what our clean demo leaves out.

The demo captures the essence; this is the world it lives in.

1

Detect the vehicle — sensor fusion

Our demo: camera (bearing) + radar (range) fused by inverse covariance beats either sensor (3.0 m → 0.69 m).

Who actually hits it

Every production ADAS and robotaxi. Radar (good range + Doppler velocity, works in rain and dark) and camera (good classification + bearing) are fused in Mobileye-based cars, Waymo, Zoox and most OEM automatic emergency braking (AEB) systems; lidar adds a third channel on robotaxis.

What it costs when it fails

Radar alone struggles to tell a stopped truck from an overhead sign, so it is often filtered out — a factor in several Autopilot crashes into stopped fire trucks and semis (2018–2021, NHTSA investigations). Camera alone fails at night, in glare, and against low-contrast objects.

How it's solved in production

Per-sensor covariance fusion or track-to-track fusion; radar owns the longitudinal estimate, camera the lateral and the class. Mobileye pushes 'true redundancy' — independent camera-only and radar-only subsystems that must agree. AEB is being mandated (Euro NCAP; US FMVSS 127 by 2029).

Toy demo → real system

The real problem is asynchronous sensors, tens of milliseconds of latency, extrinsic-calibration drift, radar multipath and hundreds of simultaneous objects. Our demo fuses one static object with fixed Gaussians.

In the ICRA/IROS 2025 papers Hybrid Robust Collaborative Perception with LiDAR-4D Radar Fusion
2

Track it over time — data association + gating

Our demo: gating detections against the Kalman prediction rejects clutter (2.50 m → 0.47 m RMSE).

Who actually hits it

Multi-object tracking sits in every AV perception stack — and the algorithms (JPDA, MHT, global nearest-neighbour) came from decades of aerospace and defence radar tracking before that.

What it costs when it fails

An ungated tracker snaps onto guardrail reflections and ghost returns, causing ID switches and phantom objects — the planner then brakes for nothing or, worse, drops a real vehicle mid-manoeuvre.

How it's solved in production

Chi-square validation gates around each predicted position, global-nearest-neighbour or JPDA/MHT association, and Kalman / interacting-multiple-model filters; benchmarked on nuScenes and the Waymo Open Dataset.

Toy demo → real system

Real trackers manage track birth and death, long occlusions, hundreds of objects, appearance re-identification, and sensor-specific noise. Ours is one target in uniform clutter.

In the ICRA/IROS 2025 papers MER-Tracker — Event-RGB 3D Multi-Object Tracking · Asynchronous Multi-Object Tracking with an Event Camera · Dual-level Adaptation for Multi-Object Tracking: Building Test-Time Calibration from Experience
3

Predict where it goes — motion forecasting

Our demo: a constant-turn-rate model beats straight-line constant velocity on a cornering car (13.7 m → 0.45 m at 3 s).

Who actually hits it

Prediction is widely considered the hardest open module in self-driving. Waymo, Cruise and every serious player run learned multimodal predictors; the Argoverse and Waymo Open Motion challenges exist specifically for it.

What it costs when it fails

Mispredict a cut-in or an unprotected left turn and the car either plans into a collision or freezes in the intersection — Cruise's well-publicised stalls and hesitations trace largely to prediction uncertainty.

How it's solved in production

Kinematic CTRV/CTRA models for the short horizon; learned models (VectorNet, MultiPath, transformer predictors) for multimodal intent, usually ensembled with a conservative fallback.

Toy demo → real system

Real prediction is multi-agent and interactive — your plan changes their behaviour — multimodal, and must run for hundreds of agents at once. Ours is one car on a fixed arc.

4

Keep a safe gap — following distance

Our demo: a fixed 2 m gap becomes a crash when the lead brakes; the speed-dependent gap holds (−26 m → +2 m).

Who actually hits it

Adaptive cruise control and AEB in every modern car; robotaxis. Mobileye's Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS) turns the safe-distance idea into a formal model now cited in AV regulation.

What it costs when it fails

Too short a gap causes the most common crash type — the rear-end. Too aggressive an AEB causes 'phantom braking', which gets the car rear-ended from behind (thousands of NHTSA complaints).

How it's solved in production

Time-headway rules (the 2-second rule), the RSS safe-longitudinal-distance formula (your reaction + braking distance minus the lead's), and standards ISO 15622 (ACC) and 22839 (forward collision mitigation).

Toy demo → real system

Real gap-keeping must estimate road friction (wet/ice halves it), react to instant cut-ins, and trade comfort against safety under sensor latency. Ours is a 1-D deterministic brake.

5

Hold the lane — lateral control

Our demo: a cross-track-only controller weaves; adding the heading term (Stanley) settles (2.0 m overshoot → 0.08 m).

Who actually hits it

The Stanley controller won the 2005 DARPA Grand Challenge for Stanford; pure pursuit is older and ubiquitous; production lane-centring uses these plus MPC on higher-end systems.

What it costs when it fails

A weaving lane-keeper is uncomfortable and unsafe — it ping-pongs in the lane — and bad tuning turns into over- or understeer at highway speed.

How it's solved in production

Stanley (cross-track + heading), pure pursuit with speed-scheduled lookahead, and model-predictive lateral control that respects the vehicle model.

Toy demo → real system

Real control fights tyre slip, actuator lag, road bank and camber, changing speed, and plain model mismatch. Ours is a kinematic unicycle.
