For a decade, recognition meant choosing from a fixed list — and failing on everything else. 01 · the problem

You can't list every thing

For a decade, recognition meant choosing from a fixed list. Train a network on a thousand classes and it will happily label any of those thousand — but show it the one thing you forgot to include, and it has no choice but to force a wrong answer from its list.

The world doesn’t come with a finite menu. New objects, rare objects, oddly-described objects — a robot in a real kitchen meets things no label set anticipated. Open-vocabulary perception is the escape from the fixed list: recognize, detect, and segment things described by any words, including ones never seen in training.

the actual math — one level deeper

Closed-set classifier: softmax over K fixed classes — any input is forced onto one of the K, so an unseen category is always mislabeled.

Open-vocabulary: score the input against embeddings of arbitrary text, so the label set can grow at test time without retraining.

02 · the key idea

Put pictures and words in one space

The idea that broke the wall is deceptively simple. Train two encoders together — one for images, one for text — on hundreds of millions of caption pairs, so that a picture and its description land at the same place in a shared embedding space.

Once images and words share a space, recognition stops being a fixed classifier and becomes a lookup: embed the picture, embed any set of words you like, and pick the nearest. Add a new category by simply typing its name — no retraining. This vision-language embedding (CLIP and its kin) is the engine under everything that follows.

the actual math — one level deeper

Contrastive training pulls matching image-text pairs together and pushes mismatches apart, aligning both encoders in one space.

Recognition: label = argmax over candidate texts of cosine(image embedding, text embedding) — a nearest-neighbour lookup over any vocabulary.

03 · detect & segment

Box anything you can describe

Recognizing a whole image is one thing; finding where things are is what a robot needs. Open-vocabulary detection and segmentation take region proposals — candidate boxes or masks — and score each against arbitrary text embeddings rather than a fixed classifier head.

So you type ‘the laptop’ (or ‘a small blue mug’) and the regions that match light up, even for categories never in the training labels. The words are supplied at test time, which means the same model handles an open, growing set of things to look for — the practical form open-vocabulary usually takes.

the actual math — one level deeper

For each region proposal r with feature v_r, score s(r,t) = v_r · t for each prompt embedding t; keep boxes above threshold, label by best t.

Training often distills a VLM’s features into the detector so region features live in the shared image-text space.

04 · grounding

The one I mean, not just the class

Detection finds all the cups; language usually means one of them. ‘The red mug behind the laptop’ is a referring expression, and resolving it — grounding — means picking the exact region the words point to, not just the category.

That takes reasoning over attributes (red), and spatial relations (behind the laptop), to disambiguate among look-alikes. Grounding is what turns open-vocabulary perception into something a person can actually direct: say what you mean in plain language, and the system points at the right thing.

the actual math — one level deeper

Referring: pick the region maximizing a match(region, phrase) that accounts for attributes and relations, not just the class.

Relations (‘behind the laptop’) require reasoning over pairs of regions — grounding resolves to the single intended referent.

05 · into 3D

A map you can ask by name

For a robot, recognition has to live in space. The final step lifts language-aligned features off flat images and into a 3D map — attaching a CLIP-style feature to every point, voxel, or Gaussian using the camera geometry.

Now the map itself is queryable by language: ask ‘where is the sink?’ and the matching region in 3D lights up, even though no one labelled a ‘sink’ when the map was built. This open-vocabulary 3D field is where perception meets navigation and manipulation — a spatial memory you address with words.

the actual math — one level deeper

Lift 2D features to 3D: for each 3D point X seen in image pixels, aggregate the pixels’ language-aligned features into a per-point descriptor F(X).

Query: score F(X) · t against a text embedding t to localize ‘the sink’ in the map — open-vocabulary, no 3D labels needed.

01

Vision-language embeddings (the CLIP idea)

both
the problem

Images and words live in separate numeric spaces — a picture is a pixel grid, a word is a token id — so you cannot directly compare them. The gap means a classifier must enumerate every category it will ever see.

the approaches

The foundation: train image and text encoders together so a picture lands near its description in one space, and recognition becomes a text lookup. Everything else here builds on this alignment.

Work refines the embedding for dense, spatial, and synonym-stable use.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input pair: a 224×224 photo of a coffee mug + caption ‘a ceramic mug’
  2. Image encoder (ViT-L/14): 196 patch tokens → 768-d CLS feature, L2-normalized to unit sphere
  3. Text encoder (transformer): ‘a ceramic mug’ → 77 tokens → 512-d feature, L2-normalized
  4. Cosine similarity: dot product = 0.31 for match, ≈0.04 for random unrelated pair
  5. Contrastive loss: pull matched pair together, push 4095 other pairs in batch apart; temperature τ=0.07
  6. At test time: embed ‘a ceramic mug’, ‘a chair’, ‘a dog’ → argmax cosine against the photo → ‘a ceramic mug’ wins at 0.31
  • CVPR
    SynCLIP: Synonym-Coherent Language-Image Pretraining — enriches pretraining so synonyms give consistent spatial attention
    CLIP-based detectors and segmenters fail when the language shifts—'coffee mug' and 'ceramic mug' should point to the same object, but produce completely different spatial attention maps. Semantic-consistent spatial attention alignment explicitly synchronizes the attention patterns across synonyms during pretraining, so the visual features respond consistently to any words that mean the same thing. This prevents inconsistency in dense predictions across linguistic variation.
  • CVPR
    Looking Beyond the Window: Global-Local Aligned CLIP — dynamic attention normalization across sliding windows
    High-resolution segmentation with CLIP requires sliding-window inference, but processing each window independently breaks semantic continuity—objects at window boundaries get mislabeled because each window sees only its local context. Global-local attention normalization dynamically rescales attention across windows using globally representative proxy queries, so a pixel near the edge sees its full semantic context, not just what fits in one window. The result: consistent pixel labels across the entire image, not a patchwork.
  • ROBO
    Do Visual-Language Grid Maps Capture Latent Semantics? — analyzes when VLM-embedding maps actually generalize
    A robot builds a semantic map by embedding images with CLIP and storing the features at each location—but does this map actually generalize to new scenes and queries? Without a rigorous evaluation methodology, teams deploy maps blind to their actual semantic quality. This paper defines semantic coverage metrics and transfer benchmarks that measure whether the embedding space truly captures object identity across viewpoints and instances.
  • ROBO
    Learning Generalizable Feature Fields for Mobile Manipulation — distills CLIP into a feature field unifying navigation + manipulation
    Mobile manipulation needs a single scene representation that handles both fine-grained geometry (where exactly do I grasp?) and large-scale spatial structure (how do I navigate there?). Unified feature fields that couple visual embeddings with geometric detail let the robot query the space by language for both high-level navigation targets and low-level grasp affordances, replacing the traditional separation between semantic maps and geometric models.
02

Open-vocabulary detection

both
the problem

A standard detector has a fixed classifier head — a weight matrix with one row per class. You cannot add a new class at test time without retraining that head, and each proposal must be forced onto one of the trained categories.

the approaches

Find and box objects named by arbitrary text, not a fixed list, by scoring region proposals against prompt embeddings — usually distilling a VLM into the detector.

Recent work goes beyond category names to attributes and prompt-enriched training.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Image: 640×480 scene; backbone (ResNet-50) extracts a 20×15×2048 feature map
  2. Region proposals: RPN generates ~2,000 candidate boxes; NMS keeps ~300
  3. Region feature: RoI-align each box → 7×7×2048 → 512-d vector, L2-normalized
  4. Text embeddings: encode [‘mug’, ‘laptop’, ‘plant’] with the text encoder → 3×512 matrix
  5. Scores: 300×512 @ 512×3 → 300×3 logit matrix; threshold at 0.5; keep top boxes per class
  6. Result: ‘mug’ box at [142,88,210,156] score 0.87 — no retraining needed for ‘mug’
  • CVPR
    SRA-Det: Omni-Grained Open-Vocabulary Detection Beyond Category Names — semantic-facet retrieval + soft-min matching for attributes
    Open-vocabulary detectors score boxes against category embeddings, but real queries are richer: 'red mug' is not just 'mug'—it combines identity and color, and simple text embedding misses that faceted structure. Semantic Retrieval Augmentation with learnable semantic facets decomposes queries into multiple fine-grained attributes and reasons over them jointly, scoring boxes by soft-min matching that weighs all facets. The detector now sees 'red' as a separate semantic signal, not noise mixed into the 'mug' embedding.
  • CVPR
    PET-DINO: Unifying Visual Cues into Grounding DINO — prompt-enriched training with dynamic memory distillation
    Grounding DINO detects and grounds objects using visual prompts (a reference image or box), but prompt diversity—different image angles, lighting, backgrounds—hurts generalization. Training-time visual prompt augmentation is expensive and often suboptimal. Prompt-enriched training with dynamic memory distillation learns to transform diverse visual prompts into stable features that align with text embeddings, so the detector generalizes across prompt variations without manual augmentation.
  • CVPR
    ViTPrompt: Training-Free Prompt Refinement with Visual Tokens — visual-token augmentation refines boxes and scores, no training
    Test-time refinement for open-vocabulary detection usually improves classification confidence but leaves bounding boxes noisy, since boxes and classes are optimized separately. Inserting visual tokens from high-confidence detections directly into the text prompt adds instance-aware geometric context without any retraining. Visual token augmentation lets the prompt encoder see the spatial structure the detector already found, refining both box coordinates and scores in a single pass.
  • ROBO
    Detect Everything with Few Examples — few-shot detection with ViT features + prototypes, on a real robot
    Open-world robot manipulation needs to detect object categories with only a handful of demonstrations, no fine-tuning, and low latency on the robot. Using ViT features extracted from few exemplars and prototype-based matching, the system learns an open-set detector on the fly. Few-shot prototypical detection with frozen ViT backbones trades off-line training time for real-time adaptation: exemplars seed prototypes, new images are scored by similarity to those prototypes, and the model generalizes across viewpoints and instances without touching the weights.

Open-vocabulary segmentation

vision
the problem

Classification aligns a single global image feature to a word; segmentation must align every pixel, or every mask proposal, to words — a much harder version where global image-text training fights the pixel-level detail you need.

the approaches

Label every pixel by open text — semantic, instance, or panoptic — the dense version of the problem, where global image-text alignment fights pixel-level precision.

Objectness bias, region-text alignment, and cost aggregation are the battlegrounds.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Image: 512×512; CLIP ViT-L/14 extracts patch features: 32×32 patches × 1024-d each
  2. Text: encode ‘plant’ → 1024-d text embedding T
  3. Pixel-text scores: for each patch (i,j), compute cos(F_ij, T) → 32×32 heatmap
  4. Upsample + refine: bilinear 32×32 → 512×512; apply DINO attention map to recover sharp boundaries
  5. Threshold at 0.45: 11,240 pixels assigned to ‘plant’; largest connected component = 1 mask
  6. Panoptic merge: repeat for all prompts, resolve overlaps by max-score wins → final labeling
  • CVPR
    Mitigating Objectness Bias for Open-Vocabulary Panoptic Segmentation — CLIP-conditioned objectness + mask refinement
    Open-vocabulary panoptic segmentation merges semantic and instance masks, but objectness heads trained on seen classes suppress masks of unseen objects by default—they're never seen as 'objects.' CLIP's text score tells you the semantic signal is there, but the objectness gate kills it. CLIP-conditioned objectness recalibration overrides the objectness head using CLIP scores as a gate signal: if CLIP is confident, the region is treated as foreground even if objectness disagrees.
  • CVPR
    PCA-Seg: Revisiting Cost Aggregation for Semantic & Part Segmentation — better cost aggregation for open-vocab dense prediction
    Vision transformers compute cost aggregation—matching features between regions—but aggregation that works for closed-vocabulary segmentation often fails at fine-grained segmentation and unseen classes because it assumes known object geometry. Cost aggregation revisited for open-vocabulary dense prediction decouples feature matching from class-specific spatial priors, letting the model find object boundaries using pure feature consistency. Better aggregation works equally well for parts (within-object structure) and unseen categories, unifying the problem.
  • CVPR
    Reconstructing CLIP for Open-Vocabulary Dense Perception — multi-scale features restore spatial detail for dense output
    CLIP is trained on image-level classification, so its features are global and coarse—a 14×14 grid of patches. Dense prediction needs fine spatial detail that CLIP's pretraining didn't directly optimize. Multi-scale feature reconstruction with spatial pyramid pooling augments CLIP with dense intermediate representations and learnable spatial queries, recovering fine detail at multiple scales. The reconstructed model keeps CLIP's vocabulary while gaining the spatial precision dense tasks demand.
  • CVPR
    Rethinking MLLM Itself as a Segmenter with a Single Token — a residual-refilled MLLM decodes high-resolution masks
    Multimodal LLMs have vision and language understanding built in, but segmentation typically requires bolting on specialist mask decoders on top, adding complexity, weight, and latency. What if you just extracted high-resolution visual features directly from the MLLM without a separate decoder? Direct high-resolution feature extraction from MLLM internals yields dense spatial features that can be projected to masks with a simple linear layer, making segmentation cheaper and keeping the MLLM frozen.
04

Training-free & frozen-VLM dense prediction

vision
the problem

Training a segmenter from scratch needs pixel-level labels, which are expensive. A pretrained VLM already knows language-aligned features — the question is whether you can steer its dense outputs directly onto pixel labels, with no additional training.

the approaches

Skip training entirely: freeze a pretrained VLM and project its dense features onto text embeddings for pixel labels — fast, and it inherits the VLM’s open vocabulary directly.

Geometry alignment and attention fixes correct the VLM’s global bias.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Model: LLaVA-1.5 (13B), frozen weights; extract intermediate layer features at layer 24
  2. For query ‘laptop’: encode the text → 4096-d vector T
  3. Feature grid: last hidden states → reshape to 14×14×4096 spatial feature map F
  4. Cosine map: for each of the 196 positions, compute cos(F_ij, T) → 14×14 heatmap
  5. Procrustes alignment: rotate feature-space axes to maximize separability between ‘laptop’ and ‘background’
  6. Propagate: Laplacian smoothing on the 14×14 → upsample → final mask with IoU 0.73 on COCO val — zero training labels used
  • CVPR
    PEARL: Geometry Aligns Semantics for Training-Free Segmentation — Procrustes alignment + text-aware Laplacian propagation
    Training-free segmentation with frozen VLMs relies on matching image features to text embeddings, but the VLM's global bias—it's trained for classification—fights the per-pixel precision you need. Procrustes alignment rotates the feature space to maximize separability between the target class and background, then Laplacian propagation smooths the resulting confidence map using spatial structure. No training needed; just geometric rotation and diffusion that push the frozen features toward sharper, more coherent boundaries.
  • CVPR
    The Power of Prior: Training-Free Segmentation with LLaVA — frozen MLLM features + cosine matching for dense labels
    LLaVA understands language and vision but wasn't trained to segment, so feeding its features directly to text matching yields poor masks. Yet LLaVA does have semantic and spatial priors baked into its layers. Extracting features from the right layer and applying frozen cosine matching between dense visual features and text embeddings yields surprisingly strong segmentation without any additional training. The 'prior' is simply the feature quality LLaVA already learned.
  • CVPR
    Direct Segmentation without Logits Optimization — analytical distribution-discrepancy solve, no training
    Traditional open-vocabulary segmentation iteratively optimizes logits—a slow, indirect loop. The paper reframes the problem as solving distribution discrepancy analytically: homogeneous regions (same semantics everywhere in the region) should map to the same text embedding, so formulate segmentation as a distribution-matching problem with a closed-form solution. No iteration, no optimization loops—just a direct matrix solve that runs in milliseconds and achieves competitive accuracy.
  • CVPR
    SPAR: Single-Pass Any-Resolution ViT for Segmentation — teacher-student distillation for resolution-agnostic features
    Vision Transformers are pretrained at a fixed resolution, and dense prediction at higher resolutions requires sliding-window inference (slow) or extrapolation (unstable). Teacher-student distillation transfers spatial reasoning from a finely-strided sliding-window teacher to a single-pass student, so the student learns to produce high-resolution segmentation maps in one forward pass without the teacher's computational cost. The student inherits the teacher's spatial sensitivity while running 10-50× faster.

Referring & visual grounding

both
the problem

Detection finds every mug; grounding must pick the exact one the words mean. 'The red mug behind the laptop' encodes color, identity, and a spatial relation — all three must be reasoned about jointly to reach a single region.

the approaches

Resolve a full phrase — ‘the red mug behind the laptop’ — to the exact region it means, reasoning over attributes and relations to pick among look-alikes.

Grounding spans images, video, and 3D, increasingly driven by MLLMs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Phrase: ‘the red mug behind the laptop’
  2. Parse: head noun = ‘mug’; attributes = {color: red}; relation = behind(laptop)
  3. Detect candidates: open-vocab detector finds 3 mugs at scores 0.82, 0.79, 0.71
  4. Filter by color: extract HSV histograms; mug #2 (H=0°, S=0.74) wins red filter; others eliminated
  5. Verify relation: laptop centroid at (310, 240); mug #2 centroid at (310, 190) — mug y < laptop y → ‘above/in-front’; mug #3 at (310, 280) → behind; pick mug #3
  6. Final box: [288, 258, 332, 302], score 0.71 — the right mug, chosen by reasoning over attributes + spatial geometry
  • ROBO
    Towards Open-World Grasping with Large Vision-Language Models — VLM referring segmentation + contact-aware grasp ranking
    Language-instructed grasping requires both semantic understanding ('grasp the red mug') and precise spatial-geometric reasoning (contact normals, collision checks). VLMs are strong at semantics but weak at spatial details; geometric models miss the language grounding. VLM referring segmentation combined with contact-aware grasp ranking uses the VLM to ground the referred object, then scores candidate grasp poses by contact point quality and clearance. Two streams—semantic and geometric—fuse into a single action.
  • CVPR
    VLM-Grounder: A VLM Agent for Zero-Shot 3D Visual Grounding — 2D-only VLM grounding reaches 51.6% on ScanRefer
    3D visual grounding (pointing to an object in a point cloud based on language) usually needs 3D training data and 3D-aware architectures. Yet a 2D vision-language model can reason about spatial relations if given multiple views of the scene. Reasoning-chain distillation over multiview 2D VLM outputs projects 3D points to several viewpoints, scores each using the 2D VLM, and aggregates using learned spatial reasoning. Zero-shot 3D grounding reaches competitive accuracy on ScanRefer without 3D labels or 3D-specific training.
  • CVPR
    DeRVOS: Decoupling Trajectory Generation for Referring Video Segmentation — temporal consistency + multimodal fusion for video referring
    Referring video segmentation must track a specific referred object ('the person in the red coat') across occlusions and motion in dynamic scenes. Unified models that jointly handle temporal tracking and multimodal fusion often fail under occlusion. Decoupling into consistent trajectory generation and multimodal understanding uses a pretrained video instance segmentation model to generate candidate trajectories, then multimodal fusion selects which trajectory matches the referring expression. Separation of concerns simplifies the problem.
  • ROBO
    ThinkGrasp: A Vision-Language System for Strategic Part Grasping in Clutter — reasons about occlusion to grasp heavily-occluded referents
    Grasping heavily occluded objects in clutter requires reasoning about what's hiding beneath and whether removing obstacles first makes sense—pure visual geometry can't answer 'should I move the thing on top of it first?' A vision-language system that reasons about occlusion relationships and strategic removal jointly considers the visible geometry, the language description of the target, and the spatial graph of what's blocking what. The system plans not just where to grasp, but whether the grasp order matters.

Promptable segmentation (SAM-based)

both
the problem

Segmentation traditionally needs dense pixel-level supervision to produce accurate masks. SAM flips the model: given a prompt — a point, a box, or text — output a mask for anything, trained on 1B masks so it generalizes to any object without category labels.

the approaches

Segment Anything takes a prompt — a point, box, or text — and returns a mask for any object, no retraining. Robots use it to cut out arbitrary objects specified on the fly.

Adapters, LLM-driven prompting, and auto-labeling extend SAM to robot tasks.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 1024×1024 image; SAM image encoder (ViT-H) processes it once → 64×64×256 feature grid
  2. Prompt: a single click at pixel (450, 310) on a coffee mug
  3. Prompt encoder: point → 256-d positional embedding + learned ‘foreground’ token
  4. Mask decoder: 2-layer transformer attends over the 64×64 grid using the prompt tokens → 3 candidate masks (whole/part/subpart)
  5. Ambiguity resolution: select mask with highest IoU prediction score (0.91 for the ‘whole mug’ option)
  6. Output: 1024×1024 binary mask with 94.2% boundary IoU — any object, no category name needed
  • ROBO
    GraspSAM: When Segment Anything Meets Grasp Detection — light adapters turn SAM into a promptable grasp detector
    Grasp detection usually fails on unfamiliar objects because training data is limited, and users can't easily direct the system to specific regions. GraspSAM adapts SAM (trained on 1B masks) with lightweight adapters and a modified decoder, accepting point, box, or text prompts to predict grasps for any object without category labels. By reusing SAM's massive prior knowledge, the system generalizes to unseen object types and multiple prompt modalities.
  • ROBO
    SAM-Rectified Part Detection with Iterative Pose Refinement — fine-tuned SAM recognizes functional parts across objects
    Parts like handles, wheels, and buttons have different geometric and functional properties that affect how a robot should interact with them, yet SAM segments them as visual regions without understanding their distinct roles. The gap is that part segmentation needs to recognize functional identity, not just visual boundaries. This approach fine-tunes SAM on parts, then iteratively refines part masks by matching pose predictions: it detects a part's orientation and pose from the mask, re-prompts SAM with refined regions, and repeats to converge on part masks that align to actual functional geometry.
  • CVPR
    MovSAM: Single-Image Moving Object Segmentation via Deep Thinking — LLM reasoning generates prompts for SAM to infer motion
    Detecting moving objects normally requires comparing multiple frames—impossible from a single still image. An MLLM reasons step-by-step about scene context to infer motion cues, generating text prompts that SAM uses to segment likely-moving objects with iterative refinement. Semantic reasoning about object behavior bridges the temporal gap that optical flow cannot cross from one image alone.
  • ROBO
    Low-Effort Iterative Dataset Generation for Instance Segmentation — change detection + SAM auto-labels novel objects
    Hand-labeling instance masks for unfamiliar objects is expensive and limits training data diversity. Change detection (frame differencing) identifies unfamiliar objects automatically, then SAM auto-labels them without human annotation, bootstrapping datasets for new categories. Iterative refinement over multiple frames converges to accurate masks.

Open-vocabulary 3D scene understanding

both
the problem

3D scene understanding has always needed category-specific 3D labels — bounding-box annotations in point clouds for each class. Acquiring these is expensive, and a closed label set means the 3D understanding is as limited as the 2D one was.

the approaches

Bring open-vocabulary labels into 3D: parse a scene — points, voxels, or Gaussians — against arbitrary text, so a robot understands space by language without a closed 3D label set.

Reconstruction quality and 2D-to-3D fusion are the bottlenecks.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 60-frame RGB video of a living room; SfM gives camera poses + sparse point cloud (12,000 pts)
  2. Dense reconstruction: CLIP3R builds a dense 3D volume at 4cm voxel resolution → 38,000 occupied voxels
  3. Feature per voxel: project each voxel into all frames where it’s visible; average their CLIP-ViT-L/14 patch features → 768-d descriptor
  4. Query ‘tv stand’: encode text → 768-d T; for each voxel compute cosine score
  5. Top-k cluster: 847 voxels score > 0.42; DBSCAN (ε=8cm) → 1 cluster = the tv stand instance
  6. Result: 3D bounding box [0.8m × 0.4m × 0.6m] located at (2.3m, 0.3m, 1.1m) — no 3D label ever applied
  • CVPR
    Ov3R: Open-Vocabulary Semantic 3D Reconstruction from RGB Videos — CLIP3R fuses 2D-3D for instance-level open-vocab reconstruction
    3D scene understanding has always required category-specific labels, making it impossible to recognize objects outside the training vocabulary. CLIP3R fuses 2D CLIP embeddings with camera geometry to build instance-level open-vocabulary reconstruction, attaching language-aligned features to each voxel. A single video and a text prompt suffice to locate and segment any object in 3D without 3D training labels.
  • CVPR
    CoSMo3D: Promptable 3D Semantic Segmentation via LLM-Guided Canonical Space — LLM-guided canonical modeling for open-world 3D segmentation
    Open-vocabulary 3D segmentation must handle partial views and compositional phrases like 'the handle of the mug. ' An LLM guides construction of a canonical shape space where semantically similar parts align across views, then queries are matched in that stable space. Canonical alignment lets the system reason about part identity and spatial attributes without 3D training data.
  • CVPR
    OpenVoxel: Training-Free Grouping and Captioning of Voxels — MLLM text reasoning over 3D voxels, training-free
    Voxel grids provide a simple 3D representation, but they lack semantic labels and grouping — you have occupied voxels but no understanding of which voxels belong to the same object or what that object is. The challenge is achieving object-level semantic understanding in 3D without training on 3D labels. OpenVoxel groups voxels using unsupervised clustering, then uses a frozen MLLM to caption each cluster by reasoning about its geometry and appearance: it performs clustering on voxel positions and features, then queries an MLLM with the cluster's geometry and color to generate a natural-language description, enabling open-vocabulary queries without training.
  • ROBO
    OpenVox: Real-Time Instance-Level Open-Vocabulary Voxels — incremental voxel SLAM with VLM-grounded instances
    Mobile robots need fast, incremental open-vocabulary 3D maps, but real-time segmentation noise accumulates across frames, corrupting the map. A probabilistic voxel SLAM fuses VLM-grounded instances across frames using per-voxel membership probabilities, smoothing detection errors through time. Instance tracking persists object identity even when individual frames are ambiguous.

Language-embedded feature fields

both
the problem

A 3D scene is continuous — you want to ask 'what is at position (x,y,z)?' and get a language-aligned answer. Discrete voxels waste memory; a continuous implicit field stores a feature at every point, but distilling 2D foundation-model knowledge into it is the hard part.

the approaches

Distill 2D foundation-model features (CLIP, DINO) into a continuous 3D field, so querying any 3D location returns a language-aligned feature — open-vocabulary 3D with no 3D annotation.

Efficiency (dense features are heavy) and view-consistency are the work.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scene: tabletop with 5 objects; 100 posed RGB-D frames → 3D Gaussians via 3DGS (50,000 splats)
  2. Feature distillation: for each Gaussian g, project its center into every visible frame; bilinearly sample Alpha-CLIP features → average → 512-d feature F_g stored on g
  3. Query ‘handle of the mug’: encode text → 512-d T; for each splat compute cos(F_g, T)
  4. Top-ranked splats: 312 splats score > 0.55, covering the mug handle region
  5. Grasp sampling: fit a grasp pose to the top-ranked splat cluster → 6-DOF grasp at the handle
  6. Execution: robot grasps at the handle pose with 89% success vs. 41% for whole-object grasping — the language embedding pays off in action
  • ROBO
    GraspSplats: Efficient Manipulation with 3D Feature Splatting — distills features into 3D Gaussians for grasp sampling in 60 s
    Continuous 3D fields (NeRFs) encode language-aligned features but require expensive differentiable rendering to sample grasp poses; discrete voxels waste memory. Distilled CLIP features attach directly to 3D Gaussians, enabling direct grasp point sampling via feature lookup rather than rendering. This explicit geometry avoids neural field inference overhead while capturing scenes in 60 seconds.
  • ROBO
    LensDFF: Language-Enhanced Sparse Feature Distillation for Grasping — view-consistent 2D features onto point clouds for dexterous grasps
    Few-shot dexterous manipulation is computationally expensive when systems must reconstruct full 3D scenes (NeRF) for each new object from limited views. 2D visual features distill directly onto simple point clouds, with text descriptions guiding view-consistent feature fusion for single-view generalization. Avoiding full scene reconstruction cuts computational cost while enabling grasp generalization.
  • ROBO
    DVEFormer: Efficient Dense Visual Embeddings via Distillation — distills Alpha-CLIP into an RGB-D transformer at 26–77 FPS
    Real-time open-vocabulary scene understanding requires per-pixel language-aligned features, but VLM inference is slow and desktop-bound. Knowledge distillation from an Alpha-CLIP teacher trains an efficient RGB-D Transformer student to predict dense embeddings at 26–77 FPS, running directly on edge hardware. The compact student inherits CLIP alignment without recomputing expensive VLM features.
  • CVPR
    ExtrinSplat: Decoupling Geometry and Semantics in 3D Scenes — separates geometry and semantics in Gaussian splatting
    3D Gaussian splats were designed for geometry — reconstructing shape and appearance from images. Adding semantic features directly to splats conflates two separate problems: some splats might represent background geometry that has no semantic label, while others represent objects that are semantically important. ExtrinSplat decouples geometry splats (for accurate 3D shape) from semantic features (for language grounding): it maintains the original Gaussian geometry for rendering fidelity, but stores language-aligned features in a separate, task-specific layer, avoiding the contamination of geometry with semantic labels.

3D visual grounding & spatial localization

vision
the problem

In a 3D point cloud, 'the chair to the left of the desk' requires simultaneously understanding object identity in 3D geometry, spatial relations between objects, and language-to-3D correspondence — three hard problems at once, usually without 3D training data.

the approaches

Ground language directly in 3D — point to the region a phrase means in a point cloud or splat — often zero-shot, without 3D training data, by reasoning over geometry and relations.

Reasoning-chain distillation makes it fast enough to run.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scene: ScanNet point cloud, 41,000 points, 9 object instances detected
  2. Query: ‘the chair to the left of the desk’
  3. Zero-shot object recognition: project 3D to 5 viewpoints → CLIP scores for [‘chair’,‘desk’,‘table’,‘lamp’,...] per instance
  4. Candidate chairs: instances #3 (score 0.61) and #7 (score 0.58); desk = instance #5 (score 0.73)
  5. Spatial relation: compute signed angle from desk to each chair candidate in the horizontal plane; chair #7 is at +87° (left) vs chair #3 at -110° (right)
  6. Result: instance #7 selected; 3D box returned in 130ms at 7.69 FPS — zero 3D grounding labels used
  • CVPR
    UZ3DVG: Unaided Zero-Shot 3D Visual Grounding — reasoning-chain distillation + geometry-aware modeling at 7.69 FPS
    Grounding a phrase like 'the chair to the left of the desk' in a point cloud requires three capabilities: recognizing objects by open-vocabulary labels, understanding spatial relations (left, right, behind), and reasoning about geometry without 3D training data. The challenge is doing this zero-shot when the language grounding model has never seen 3D coordinates. UZ3DVG distills a reasoning-aware chain-of-thought from an MLLM into a geometry-aware 3D model: it uses an MLLM to generate a reasoning trace (identify candidates, check relations, select), then learns to reproduce that reasoning using 3D geometry features and spatial relations, achieving 3D grounding without 3D labels.
  • CVPR
    SAQN: Semantic-Based Adaptive Query Network for 3D Referring Segmentation — class-level semantic queries for point clouds
    3D referring segmentation must resolve class identity and spatial relations simultaneously, but standard point-cloud networks treat them separately. Class-level semantic queries adapt per-instance to reconcile 'which object?' and 'which spatial role?' in a single pass over the point cloud. Adaptive routing lets the network focus on spatial reasoning necessary for disambiguation.
  • CVPR
    LangRef3DGS: Language-Guided 3D Referential Segmentation — Dirichlet-process discovery of novel categories from partial views
    Identifying 'the red mug in the cabinet' in a 3D Gaussian-splatted scene requires learning object identities from partial views that arrive incrementally. A Dirichlet process discovers latent object categories and maintains open-vocabulary identity across partial observations, grounding referring expressions without closed-set class labels. Non-parametric Bayesian inference lets the system add new identities as views arrive.
  • CVPR
    QueryMe: Open-Vocabulary 3D Object Affordance Grounding — grounds affordances in 3D from multimodal evidence
    A robot grasping an object must locate not just 'the mug' but 'the handle of the mug'—the interactive part. Multimodal reasoning over 3D evidence grounds affordances described in plain language, connecting task intent to geometric grasp opportunities. Task descriptions provide the semantic signal needed to resolve which part is actionable.

Open-world discovery & novel categories

both
the problem

An open-world robot encounters objects that are not in any known category. Rather than force them onto the nearest known label, the system must detect the anomaly, flag it as unknown, and optionally cluster new instances into a new category — the basis for safe lifelong learning.

the approaches

Rather than force every object onto a known class, treat the unfamiliar as an anomaly in feature space — discover, flag, or learn genuinely new categories, the basis for lifelong, safe perception.

Anomaly framing and novel-category embedding are the recurring tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Deployment: agricultural robot trained on {crops, soil, sky}; encounters a weed species not in training set
  2. Feature extraction: backbone extracts 512-d feature for the unknown region; 0.41 cosine distance to nearest known class ‘soil’
  3. Anomaly score: threshold = 0.35; region scores 0.41 → flagged as anomaly (not forced to ‘soil’)
  4. Clustering: over 200 frames, 37 anomaly crops of this weed are collected; k-means with k=1 → one new cluster
  5. Prototype formed: mean feature = 512-d prototype for ‘weed-type-A’; added to known set
  6. Subsequent precision: recall for ‘weed-type-A’ rises to 0.82 on the next 100 frames — new category learned from anomaly detection alone
  • CVPR
    Zoo3D: Zero-Shot 3D Object Detection at Scene Level — training-free 3D detection via SAM + graph clustering
    3D object detection at scene level without training labels requires inferring object boundaries from geometry and appearance cues alone. SAM-generated masks cluster via graph structure to form connected 3D regions, with open-vocabulary classification applied to each cluster without any 3D detection training. Graph clustering enforces spatial coherence.
  • CVPR
    NoOVD: Novel Category Discovery for Open-Vocabulary Detection — metric learning discovers and embeds novel categories
    Open-vocabulary detection covers known classes but struggles to recognize genuinely new categories at deployment time. Metric learning discovers emerging clusters in feature space and assigns them embeddings compatible with the open-vocabulary model, expanding the detector's vocabulary without retraining. New categories integrate via embedding alignment.
  • ROBO
    Zero-Shot Semantic Segmentation for Robots in Agriculture — crops as hyperspheres in feature space; weeds are anomalies
    Agricultural robots must recognize crops and distinguish weeds, but new weed species constantly emerge and crop varieties vary by region. The challenge is performing segmentation on unseen plant species and weed types without retraining. This approach models crop and weed classes as hyperspheres in a learned feature space, treating unfamiliar weeds as anomalies distant from known crop spheres: it learns a feature space where known crops form tight clusters (hyperspheres) and new weeds appear as outliers, enabling zero-shot detection of unseen species through anomaly scoring in feature space.
  • ROBO
    OoDIS: Anomaly Instance Segmentation and Detection Benchmark — a benchmark for detecting genuinely unknown objects
    Autonomous vehicles encounter unknown objects at deployment, but benchmarks rarely measure instance-level segmentation and detection jointly. OoDIS extends anomaly segmentation benchmarks with instance-level tasks, standardizing evaluation across semantic and instance anomaly understanding. A unified benchmark reveals gaps between detecting unknowns and pinpointing each precisely.

Part segmentation & fine-grained understanding

vision
the problem

Object-level recognition tells you 'mug' but not 'handle of the mug' — the part a robot arm must actually grasp. Segmenting parts requires combining coarse global context (what is the object?) with fine local attention (where is the handle within it?), with no fixed part taxonomy.

the approaches

Go below the object to its parts — the handle, the blade, the wheel — combining global semantics with fine attention, without a predefined part taxonomy. Parts are what manipulation actually acts on.

The hard split is over-segmenting an object versus missing a small part.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Image: a ceramic mug on a table; CLIP global feature matches ‘mug’ at cosine 0.74
  2. Part query: ‘handle of the mug’ → 1024-d text embedding T_part
  3. HOPS hierarchical attention: layer 22 attention heads (spatial) filtered by cosine(head_output, T_part); 3 of 16 heads activate strongly on the right side of the mug
  4. Affinity propagation: from the 3 activated patch tokens, propagate similarity across the patch grid → 14×14 part heatmap
  5. Threshold + upsample: 11 patches above 0.40 → binary mask upsample to 448×448
  6. Result: part mask with 71.3% IoU vs. annotated handle on PartImageNet — enabling a successful pinch grasp at the correct point
  • CVPR
    HOPS: Hierarchical Open-Vocabulary Part Segmentation — attention filtering + affinity propagation for parts

    Object recognition stops at the category level — 'mug' — but manipulation requires finding specific parts within it. The challenge is that parts like handles, spouts, or buttons don't have a closed taxonomy; they're defined relationally within each object. HOPS tackles this by using hierarchical attention filtering: it extracts which spatial attention heads in a VLM strongly respond to a queried part description, then propagates those activations across the visual feature grid to form a part mask, all without pixel-level part labels.

  • CVPR
    PCA-Seg: Cost Aggregation for Semantic & Part Segmentation — unified semantic and part segmentation

    Dense prediction of both objects and parts requires matching every pixel to semantic embeddings, but naive per-pixel matching is noisy and lacks spatial coherence. The problem is that pixels don't exist in isolation — a part boundary should align with actual visual discontinuities in texture or color. PCA-Seg solves this by aggregating similarity costs over local neighborhoods: rather than scoring each pixel independently against text embeddings, it smooths cost over neighborhoods before thresholding, recovering sharp boundaries aligned to visual edges while maintaining open-vocabulary generality.

  • CVPR
    SRA-Det: Detection Beyond Category Names — multi-facet decomposition reaches attributes and parts

    Open-vocabulary detectors today score regions against category embeddings like 'mug' or 'chair', but real instructions often specify attributes ('red mug') or parts ('cup handle'). A single region vector is weak for decomposing these multi-faceted descriptions. SRA-Det breaks this by learning separate semantic facets within a detection backbone: it discovers sub-embeddings for category, color, texture, and parts within each region's features, then matches each facet independently to the query, enabling detection of 'red mug' without training on that exact phrase.

  • CVPR
    Part-Aware Vision-Language Models for Fine-Grained Recognition — explicit part alignment inside a VLM

    Vision-language models align images to text at the whole-object level, but to recognize fine-grained distinctions — like different handle styles or mug shapes — they must align parts of the image to parts of the description. Without explicit part-level correspondence, a VLM can miss what makes objects different. This approach adds part alignment inside the VLM training: it grounds text mentions of parts ('the curved handle', 'the flat rim') directly to corresponding image regions during contrastive training, so the model learns part-level correspondences that boost fine-grained recognition.

Language-driven affordance grounding

robotics
the problem

For a robot, the perception question is not just 'what is it?' but 'where do I interact with it, given my task?' A VLM that recognizes 'mug' doesn't automatically know the handle is the grasp point, and the pour spout is the pour point — affordances must be grounded from task descriptions.

the approaches

For a robot, the useful open-vocabulary question isn’t just ‘what is it?’ but ‘where do I act?’ — grounding a task in words to the object part and pose to grasp or push.

VLMs reason about task-relevant affordances zero-shot, from implicit instructions.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task instruction: ‘pour the coffee into the cup’
  2. VLM reasoning: AffordGrasp queries the VLM: ‘given the instruction, which part of the mug affords pouring?’ → answer: ‘hold the handle and tilt toward the spout’
  3. Part grounding: open-vocab part segmenter locates ‘handle’ (IoU 0.68) and ‘spout’ (IoU 0.61) on the mug
  4. Grasp point: centroid of handle mask + grasp axis perpendicular to handle long axis → 6-DOF grasp pose
  5. Motion direction: spout centroid defines the tilt axis; plan 45° tilt toward cup
  6. Execution: robot completes pour in 4.2s with 91% success — vs 44% for whole-object random grasping without affordance grounding
  • ROBO
    AffordGrasp: In-Context Affordance Reasoning for Open-Vocabulary Grasping — a VLM infers the task and grounds part-level affordances

    A robot that knows 'mug' doesn't automatically know where to grasp it — affordances (grasp points, push surfaces) depend on the task, not just the object type. When the task is 'pour', the handle is the grasp point; for 'clean', the body is better. The gap is that no label set can enumerate all task-object affordance pairs. AffordGrasp uses an MLLM to infer affordances from task instructions in context: it queries the model with the task description ('pour the coffee into the cup'), the VLM reasons about which part affords that action, then a part segmenter locates it, bridging from words and intent to actionable pose.

  • ROBO
    RoboPoint: A VLM for Spatial Affordance Prediction — predicts image keypoint affordances, zero-shot across scenes

    Affordances are spatial — they're keypoints or contact regions where interaction succeeds. Naming objects is different from pinpointing where to act on them, yet most models conflate the two. The challenge is predicting action keypoints (grasp points, push locations, insertion ports) for objects never seen in training. RoboPoint frames affordance prediction as keypoint regression in image coordinates: given an object and a task description, it predicts the pixel coordinates where a robot should act, using VLM reasoning to ground task semantics to 2D action points, zero-shot across new objects.

  • ROBO
    MOKA: Open-World Manipulation through Mark-Based Visual Prompting — affordance reasoning as VLM question-answering

    Robots must reason about how to manipulate unfamiliar objects in open-world settings, but traditional approaches require category-specific policies or dense affordance labels. The problem is bridging from semantic understanding of an object to deciding where and how to grasp or manipulate it, without closed-form affordance labels. MOKA frames affordance reasoning as VLM question-answering over marked regions: it shows the VLM object regions with spatial markers and asks 'which region should I grasp to accomplish X?', letting the model reason through affordances as natural language rather than predicting coordinate outputs.

  • ROBO
    SemSegGrasp: Task-Oriented Grasping via Semantic Segmentation — open-vocab part segmentation drives task grasps

    Grasping depends on the task: picking up a coffee cup requires grasping the handle, but if the cup is empty and upright, any region works. Most grasping pipelines ignore the semantic task and predict regions uniformly. The key insight is that semantic understanding of an object's parts and their properties should directly drive grasp point selection. SemSegGrasp combines open-vocabulary part segmentation with grasp pose generation: it segments task-relevant parts ('handle', 'body', 'rim') using open-vocab labels, then fits grasp poses to these semantically meaningful regions, ensuring grasps align with task requirements.

Open-vocab for manipulation & navigation

robotics
the problem

A mobile manipulator given 'fetch the green bottle from the counter' needs to navigate to the counter, find the green bottle among other objects, and grasp it — three steps that each require open-vocabulary perception, with no fixed class list covering every possible instruction.

the approaches

The payoff on real robots: open-vocabulary perception feeding action — grab or go to the object named in plain language, even if it was never a training class.

Open-vocab maps and grasping close the loop from words to motion.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Instruction: ‘fetch the green bottle from the counter’
  2. Navigation: open-vocab map queried for ‘counter’ → CLIP similarity peak in map at position (4.2m, 1.8m); robot navigates there
  3. Local detection: at the counter, open-vocab detector runs prompt=‘green bottle’; 3 candidate boxes returned, scores [0.79, 0.61, 0.38]
  4. Grounding: ‘green bottle’ grounding: verify color (HSV hue=130°, saturation=0.6 → green ✓) on top box
  5. Grasp: feature-field splats on the bottle queried for ‘body of bottle’ → grasp point at centroid (pixel 342, 218) → 6-DOF pose
  6. Task completion: 83% success across 48 trials on objects never in training — pure language-to-action via open vocab
  • ROBO
    One Map to Find Them All: Real-Time Open-Vocabulary Mapping for Multi-Object Nav — one open-vocab map serves zero-shot multi-object navigation

    Mobile robots navigating natural language instructions ('fetch the water bottle from the kitchen counter') must build a spatial understanding where any object can be queried by text. Traditional semantic maps are discrete, requiring separate training per object class. Building a map that supports querying by arbitrary text in real time is the challenge. This approach builds a single language-aligned feature map by fusing CLIP embeddings across camera frames into a dense 2D/3D map: every map location stores a CLIP feature, so querying for 'counter' or 'water bottle' becomes a similarity search across the map, enabling multi-object navigation without retraining.

  • ROBO
    Beyond Bare Queries: Open-Vocabulary Grounding with 3D Scene Graph — grounds queries against an open-vocab 3D scene graph

    Language queries like 'the cup on the table' involve relationships — 'on' — and multiple objects. Grounding requires reasoning about spatial relations between objects, but open-vocabulary systems often treat each phrase as an isolated lookup. Without structured reasoning about object relationships, ambiguous or compositional phrases fail. This approach grounds queries against a 3D scene graph where nodes are detected objects and edges encode spatial relations: a query is decomposed into objects and relations, then matched to the scene graph, allowing 'the cup on the table' to be resolved by finding 'cup' nodes whose 'on' edge points to a 'table' node.

  • ROBO
    Tag Map: A Text-Based Map for Spatial Reasoning with LLMs — a text map supports thousands of classes at tiny memory

    Dense semantic maps that store VLM embeddings at every pixel are memory-intensive and slow for LLM reasoning over large spaces. Yet robots need to query maps with natural language ('where is the plant?') for navigation and planning. The bottleneck is efficiency: dense embeddings don't scale to large maps or long-horizon planning. Tag Map stores only discrete text labels (or lightweight text embeddings) at sparse landmark locations: instead of embedding every pixel, it maintains a sparse textual map where each point is tagged with descriptive text, enabling LLMs to reason over thousands of object categories at minimal memory cost.

  • ROBO
    SAGE: Bridging Semantic and Actionable Parts for Articulated Manipulation — maps semantic parts to actionable parts under language

    A robot must understand both what a part is called ('the drawer pull') and how to manipulate it ('pull it horizontally'). These are different: semantic labels describe identity, but actionable parts require understanding motion axes and constraints specific to articulated objects. The problem is that open-vocabulary segmentation gives semantic parts, but not the motion information grasping and manipulation need. SAGE maps semantic parts to actionable affordances by learning the relationship between named parts and their degrees of freedom: for each articulated object type, it learns which semantic parts correspond to which manipulation axes, allowing 'the slider knob' to map to a linear pull affordance.

Video temporal & 4D open-vocabulary

vision
the problem

Video adds a time axis to every open-vocabulary problem: a phrase like 'when the person picks up the mug' must be grounded to a temporal interval, not just a spatial box, and the object's identity must persist across frames as it moves.

the approaches

Extend open vocabulary through time: localize the moment or the moving object a phrase describes in a video, or query a dynamic 4D scene — adding identity tracking to the semantic problem.

Hierarchical embeddings and instance-aware 4D fields drive it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Video: 3-minute kitchen clip at 30fps = 5,400 frames; query: ‘when the person picks up the mug’
  2. Temporal proposal: HERO generates 200 candidate temporal windows at multiple scales (2s, 5s, 10s)
  3. Hierarchical embed: for each window, extract 4-level features (frame → clip → scene → event); cross-modal filter with query embedding
  4. Top window: 27.4s–31.8s gets score 0.84; second best is 0.61; gap > 0.2 → confident prediction
  5. Within-window grounding: frame 28.1s: open-vocab detector finds ‘mug’ at [310, 180, 380, 260]; CLIP confirms ‘mug’ cosine 0.78
  6. 4D track: identity-adaptive Gaussian tracks the mug from frame 820 to 954 (4.5s); temporal IoU 0.73 vs. annotation
  • CVPR
    HERO: Hierarchical Embedding-Refinement for Temporal Sentence Grounding — four-level embeddings + cross-modal filtering for video grounding

    Videos extend grounding from space to time: 'when the person picks up the mug' refers to a temporal interval, not a box. But text queries operate at multiple timescales — a moment, a clip, a scene context — and naive per-frame matching misses the hierarchical structure. The challenge is matching a linguistic event to temporal windows of varying length while maintaining cross-modal alignment. HERO constructs hierarchical embeddings at four levels (frame, clip, scene, event) and refines them with cross-modal filtering: it generates candidate temporal windows at multiple timescales, extracts multi-level features for each, then filters by similarity to the query embedding, localizing events across temporal granularities.

  • CVPR
    LangField4D: Spatio-Temporal Continuous 4D Language Fields — identity-adaptive Gaussian grouping + TetraPlane semantics

    A dynamic 3D scene with moving objects requires tracking identity through time while maintaining semantic meaning. Static 3D fields store per-point features, but objects move, deform, and leave/enter the scene. Querying a dynamic scene by language ('where is the mug moving?') needs both temporal tracking and semantic alignment. LangField4D uses identity-adaptive Gaussians that group over time: it tracks moving object instances across frames using adaptive grouping, stores language-aligned features on the 4D Gaussian representation, and enables queries like 'track the blue mug' to be answered by finding the spatio-temporal region matching the description.

  • CVPR
    ST4R-Splat: Spatio-Temporal Referring Segmentation in 4D Gaussians — instance-aware 4D fields with auto MLLM captions

    Referring segmentation in 4D requires finding the exact region a phrase points to across space and time. Standard 4D representations track geometry but not semantics; adding semantic labels per frame is expensive. The challenge is maintaining instance identity, spatial precision, and semantic meaning simultaneously in dynamic scenes. ST4R-Splat extends 4D Gaussian splatting with instance awareness and automatic MLLM captioning: it tracks object instances in the splat representation, then uses an MLLM to generate captions for each instance's motion and appearance, enabling spatial-temporal grounding by matching phrases to captioned instance tracks.

  • CVPR
    OmniGround: A Spatio-Temporal Grounding Benchmark — 81-category benchmark for complex real-world grounding

    Evaluation of video grounding has been limited by small benchmarks with simple queries. Real-world grounding involves complex spatial relations ('to the left of'), temporal complexity ('after the person leaves'), and compositional phrases. Without a large, diverse benchmark, it's hard to drive progress on hard cases. OmniGround provides an 81-category benchmark for complex spatio-temporal grounding: it includes real-world videos with queries involving multiple objects, spatial relations, temporal constraints, and compositional descriptions, providing a standard to measure generalization to naturalistic language in dynamic scenes.

Domain-specific open-vocabulary

vision
the problem

CLIP was trained on internet photos of everyday objects. Remote-sensing imagery (looking down, small-scale objects), underwater images (color shift, scattering), thermal images (no RGB, only heat), and medical images (very different statistics) all break off-the-shelf CLIP features in predictable ways.

the approaches

Take open vocabulary out of everyday photos and into remote sensing, underwater, thermal, and medical imagery — where distribution shift wrecks off-the-shelf VLM features and domain priors must be added.

Attention refinement and cross-modal fusion adapt the backbone.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Domain: thermal infrared image of a street scene (FLIR dataset, 640×480 single-channel 14-bit)
  2. Problem: CLIP-ViT-L/14 cosine similarity for ‘pedestrian’ on thermal patches: mean 0.18 ± 0.11 — barely above noise
  3. Cross-modal distillation: Thermal-Det trains a student thermal backbone to mimic the RGB CLIP teacher’s features on aligned RGB–thermal pairs
  4. Alignment: after 30 epochs, thermal student features for ‘pedestrian’ patches: cosine 0.61 ± 0.09 — 3.4× improvement
  5. Open-vocab detection: thermal-student features fed into Grounding DINO detector with prompt ‘pedestrian’; AP_50 = 71.3 vs. 38.6 for off-the-shelf CLIP
  6. Generalization: the same thermal backbone, prompted with ‘cyclist’ (never in thermal training), scores AP_50 = 58.7 — open vocab preserved across the domain gap
  • CVPR
    ReAttnCLIP: Training-Free Open-Vocabulary Remote Sensing Segmentation — redefined CLIP attention for overhead imagery

    CLIP was trained on internet photos where objects are centered and large, but remote-sensing images are overhead with tiny objects scattered across large areas. Attention patterns trained on whole-image alignment fail for small-scale detection. The problem is that CLIP's spatial attention is miscalibrated for the distribution shift from internet photos to overhead imagery. ReAttnCLIP dynamically redefinines CLIP's attention patterns for overhead imagery without retraining: it analyzes how attention should be weighted for remote-sensing image statistics, adjusting the model's focus to small objects at scale, preserving open vocabulary while adapting to domain priors.

  • CVPR
    Exploring the Underwater World Segmentation without Extra Training — Earth2Ocean + a 255-category underwater dataset

    Underwater images break CLIP: colors shift due to water absorption, scattering creates haze, and the object distribution (fish, coral, kelp) differs radically from internet photos. Off-the-shelf CLIP features perform poorly underwater. Training a new model requires labeled underwater data, which is scarce. The gap is bridging the domain without labeled data or retraining. This approach combines Earth2Ocean (a physics-based color-correction model) with CLIP and a large underwater dataset to establish a baseline: it corrects underwater color shifts using known water properties, then applies CLIP to the corrected images, learning to handle underwater domain shift through data-driven priors rather than retraining.

  • CVPR
    Thermal-Det: Language-Guided Cross-Modal Distillation for Thermal Detection — thermal-RGB distillation for open-vocab thermal detection

    Thermal images contain only heat, no RGB, yet CLIP is trained on RGB images and fails on thermal inputs. You cannot directly apply CLIP embeddings to thermal features. The solution is distillation: train a thermal backbone to mimic RGB CLIP's behavior on aligned RGB-thermal image pairs. The challenge is that CLIP operates on a fundamentally different data modality, so naive distillation may fail. Thermal-Det trains a student thermal backbone to match RGB CLIP embeddings through cross-modal distillation on paired RGB-thermal scenes: it learns a mapping from thermal features to CLIP's RGB space, enabling open-vocabulary detection on thermal images while preserving CLIP's semantic generality.

  • CVPR
    MM-OVSeg: Multimodal Optical-SAR Fusion for Remote Sensing — fuses optical and SAR for open-vocab remote-sensing segmentation

    Optical images (RGB, multispectral) and SAR (Synthetic Aperture Radar) images see different aspects of a scene: optical captures appearance, SAR captures physical structure and penetrates clouds. Fusing them for open-vocabulary segmentation requires finding a shared embedding space where both modalities contribute semantically. The challenge is that optical and SAR have no natural alignment and different physical meanings. MM-OVSeg fuses optical and SAR inputs by learning a joint embedding space where both modalities contribute to a unified semantic representation: it trains encoders for each modality to project into a shared CLIP-like space, then performs open-vocabulary segmentation using the fused embeddings, leveraging both view angles to ground language in multi-modal evidence.

Part IV

Where it stands

Naming anything by language is a young superpower with rough edges. What is genuinely still hard:

Dense precision from a global model. VLMs are trained on whole-image alignment, which fights the pixel-level precision segmentation needs — background bias and geometric drift persist.
Rare words & fine parts. Open vocabulary is strong on common objects but weak on rare descriptions, fine-grained parts, and compositional phrases (‘three to the left of’).
Real-time at dense resolution. Per-pixel VLM inference is slow; sliding-window accuracy costs a large slowdown, and edge deployment forces hard trade-offs.
Feature drift & hallucination. VLM embeddings drift across domains and sessions and confidently mislabel; there is no principled uncertainty for a grounding failure.
Geometry the language ignores. Language names things well but reasons about exact distance and reachability poorly — robot use still needs separate geometric grounding.
