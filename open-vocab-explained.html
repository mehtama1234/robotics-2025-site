For a decade, recognition meant choosing from a fixed list — and failing on everything else. 01 · the problem

You can't list every thing

For a decade, recognition meant choosing from a fixed list. Train a network on a thousand classes and it will happily label any of those thousand — but show it the one thing you forgot to include, and it has no choice but to force a wrong answer from its list.

The world doesn’t come with a finite menu. New objects, rare objects, oddly-described objects — a robot in a real kitchen meets things no label set anticipated. Open-vocabulary perception is the escape from the fixed list: recognize, detect, and segment things described by any words, including ones never seen in training.

the actual math — one level deeper

Closed-set classifier: softmax over K fixed classes — any input is forced onto one of the K, so an unseen category is always mislabeled.

Open-vocabulary: score the input against embeddings of arbitrary text, so the label set can grow at test time without retraining.

02 · the key idea

Put pictures and words in one space

The idea that broke the wall is deceptively simple. Train two encoders together — one for images, one for text — on hundreds of millions of caption pairs, so that a picture and its description land at the same place in a shared embedding space.

Once images and words share a space, recognition stops being a fixed classifier and becomes a lookup: embed the picture, embed any set of words you like, and pick the nearest. Add a new category by simply typing its name — no retraining. This vision-language embedding (CLIP and its kin) is the engine under everything that follows.

the actual math — one level deeper

Contrastive training pulls matching image-text pairs together and pushes mismatches apart, aligning both encoders in one space.

Recognition: label = argmax over candidate texts of cosine(image embedding, text embedding) — a nearest-neighbour lookup over any vocabulary.

03 · detect & segment

Box anything you can describe

Recognizing a whole image is one thing; finding where things are is what a robot needs. Open-vocabulary detection and segmentation take region proposals — candidate boxes or masks — and score each against arbitrary text embeddings rather than a fixed classifier head.

So you type ‘the laptop’ (or ‘a small blue mug’) and the regions that match light up, even for categories never in the training labels. The words are supplied at test time, which means the same model handles an open, growing set of things to look for — the practical form open-vocabulary usually takes.

the actual math — one level deeper

For each region proposal r with feature v_r, score s(r,t) = v_r · t for each prompt embedding t; keep boxes above threshold, label by best t.

Training often distills a VLM’s features into the detector so region features live in the shared image-text space.

04 · grounding

The one I mean, not just the class

Detection finds all the cups; language usually means one of them. ‘The red mug behind the laptop’ is a referring expression, and resolving it — grounding — means picking the exact region the words point to, not just the category.

That takes reasoning over attributes (red), and spatial relations (behind the laptop), to disambiguate among look-alikes. Grounding is what turns open-vocabulary perception into something a person can actually direct: say what you mean in plain language, and the system points at the right thing.

the actual math — one level deeper

Referring: pick the region maximizing a match(region, phrase) that accounts for attributes and relations, not just the class.

Relations (‘behind the laptop’) require reasoning over pairs of regions — grounding resolves to the single intended referent.

05 · into 3D

A map you can ask by name

For a robot, recognition has to live in space. The final step lifts language-aligned features off flat images and into a 3D map — attaching a CLIP-style feature to every point, voxel, or Gaussian using the camera geometry.

Now the map itself is queryable by language: ask ‘where is the sink?’ and the matching region in 3D lights up, even though no one labelled a ‘sink’ when the map was built. This open-vocabulary 3D field is where perception meets navigation and manipulation — a spatial memory you address with words.

the actual math — one level deeper

Lift 2D features to 3D: for each 3D point X seen in image pixels, aggregate the pixels’ language-aligned features into a per-point descriptor F(X).

Query: score F(X) · t against a text embedding t to localize ‘the sink’ in the map — open-vocabulary, no 3D labels needed.

01

Vision-language embeddings (the CLIP idea)

both
the problem

Images and words live in separate numeric spaces — a picture is a pixel grid, a word is a token id — so you cannot directly compare them. The gap means a classifier must enumerate every category it will ever see.

the approaches

The foundation: train image and text encoders together so a picture lands near its description in one space, and recognition becomes a text lookup. Everything else here builds on this alignment.

Work refines the embedding for dense, spatial, and synonym-stable use.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input pair: a 224×224 photo of a coffee mug + caption ‘a ceramic mug’
  2. Image encoder (ViT-L/14): 196 patch tokens → 768-d CLS feature, L2-normalized to unit sphere
  3. Text encoder (transformer): ‘a ceramic mug’ → 77 tokens → 512-d feature, L2-normalized
  4. Cosine similarity: dot product = 0.31 for match, ≈0.04 for random unrelated pair
  5. Contrastive loss: pull matched pair together, push 4095 other pairs in batch apart; temperature τ=0.07
  6. At test time: embed ‘a ceramic mug’, ‘a chair’, ‘a dog’ → argmax cosine against the photo → ‘a ceramic mug’ wins at 0.31

  • CVPRLooking Beyond the Window: Global-Local Aligned CLIP — dynamic attention normalization across sliding windows
  • ROBODo Visual-Language Grid Maps Capture Latent Semantics? — analyzes when VLM-embedding maps actually generalize
  • ROBOLearning Generalizable Feature Fields for Mobile Manipulation — distills CLIP into a feature field unifying navigation + manipulation

Open-vocabulary detection

both
the problem

A standard detector has a fixed classifier head — a weight matrix with one row per class. You cannot add a new class at test time without retraining that head, and each proposal must be forced onto one of the trained categories.

the approaches

Find and box objects named by arbitrary text, not a fixed list, by scoring region proposals against prompt embeddings — usually distilling a VLM into the detector.

Recent work goes beyond category names to attributes and prompt-enriched training.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Image: 640×480 scene; backbone (ResNet-50) extracts a 20×15×2048 feature map
  2. Region proposals: RPN generates ~2,000 candidate boxes; NMS keeps ~300
  3. Region feature: RoI-align each box → 7×7×2048 → 512-d vector, L2-normalized
  4. Text embeddings: encode [‘mug’, ‘laptop’, ‘plant’] with the text encoder → 3×512 matrix
  5. Scores: 300×512 @ 512×3 → 300×3 logit matrix; threshold at 0.5; keep top boxes per class
  6. Result: ‘mug’ box at [142,88,210,156] score 0.87 — no retraining needed for ‘mug’
  • CVPRSRA-Det: Omni-Grained Open-Vocabulary Detection Beyond Category Names — semantic-facet retrieval + soft-min matching for attributes
  • CVPRPET-DINO: Unifying Visual Cues into Grounding DINO — prompt-enriched training with dynamic memory distillation
  • CVPRViTPrompt: Training-Free Prompt Refinement with Visual Tokens — visual-token augmentation refines boxes and scores, no training
  • ROBODetect Everything with Few Examples — few-shot detection with ViT features + prototypes, on a real robot

Open-vocabulary segmentation

vision
the problem

Classification aligns a single global image feature to a word; segmentation must align every pixel, or every mask proposal, to words — a much harder version where global image-text training fights the pixel-level detail you need.

the approaches

Label every pixel by open text — semantic, instance, or panoptic — the dense version of the problem, where global image-text alignment fights pixel-level precision.

Objectness bias, region-text alignment, and cost aggregation are the battlegrounds.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Image: 512×512; CLIP ViT-L/14 extracts patch features: 32×32 patches × 1024-d each
  2. Text: encode ‘plant’ → 1024-d text embedding T
  3. Pixel-text scores: for each patch (i,j), compute cos(F_ij, T) → 32×32 heatmap
  4. Upsample + refine: bilinear 32×32 → 512×512; apply DINO attention map to recover sharp boundaries
  5. Threshold at 0.45: 11,240 pixels assigned to ‘plant’; largest connected component = 1 mask
  6. Panoptic merge: repeat for all prompts, resolve overlaps by max-score wins → final labeling
  • CVPRMitigating Objectness Bias for Open-Vocabulary Panoptic Segmentation — CLIP-conditioned objectness + mask refinement
  • CVPRPCA-Seg: Revisiting Cost Aggregation for Semantic & Part Segmentation — better cost aggregation for open-vocab dense prediction
  • CVPRReconstructing CLIP for Open-Vocabulary Dense Perception — multi-scale features restore spatial detail for dense output
  • CVPRRethinking MLLM Itself as a Segmenter with a Single Token — a residual-refilled MLLM decodes high-resolution masks

Training-free & frozen-VLM dense prediction

vision
the problem

Training a segmenter from scratch needs pixel-level labels, which are expensive. A pretrained VLM already knows language-aligned features — the question is whether you can steer its dense outputs directly onto pixel labels, with no additional training.

the approaches

Skip training entirely: freeze a pretrained VLM and project its dense features onto text embeddings for pixel labels — fast, and it inherits the VLM’s open vocabulary directly.

Geometry alignment and attention fixes correct the VLM’s global bias.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Model: LLaVA-1.5 (13B), frozen weights; extract intermediate layer features at layer 24
  2. For query ‘laptop’: encode the text → 4096-d vector T
  3. Feature grid: last hidden states → reshape to 14×14×4096 spatial feature map F
  4. Cosine map: for each of the 196 positions, compute cos(F_ij, T) → 14×14 heatmap
  5. Procrustes alignment: rotate feature-space axes to maximize separability between ‘laptop’ and ‘background’
  6. Propagate: Laplacian smoothing on the 14×14 → upsample → final mask with IoU 0.73 on COCO val — zero training labels used
  • CVPRPEARL: Geometry Aligns Semantics for Training-Free Segmentation — Procrustes alignment + text-aware Laplacian propagation
  • CVPRThe Power of Prior: Training-Free Segmentation with LLaVA — frozen MLLM features + cosine matching for dense labels
  • CVPRDirect Segmentation without Logits Optimization — analytical distribution-discrepancy solve, no training
  • CVPRSPAR: Single-Pass Any-Resolution ViT for Segmentation — teacher-student distillation for resolution-agnostic features

Referring & visual grounding

both
the problem

Detection finds every mug; grounding must pick the exact one the words mean. 'The red mug behind the laptop' encodes color, identity, and a spatial relation — all three must be reasoned about jointly to reach a single region.

the approaches

Resolve a full phrase — ‘the red mug behind the laptop’ — to the exact region it means, reasoning over attributes and relations to pick among look-alikes.

Grounding spans images, video, and 3D, increasingly driven by MLLMs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Phrase: ‘the red mug behind the laptop’
  2. Parse: head noun = ‘mug’; attributes = {color: red}; relation = behind(laptop)
  3. Detect candidates: open-vocab detector finds 3 mugs at scores 0.82, 0.79, 0.71
  4. Filter by color: extract HSV histograms; mug #2 (H=0°, S=0.74) wins red filter; others eliminated
  5. Verify relation: laptop centroid at (310, 240); mug #2 centroid at (310, 190) — mug y < laptop y → ‘above/in-front’; mug #3 at (310, 280) → behind; pick mug #3
  6. Final box: [288, 258, 332, 302], score 0.71 — the right mug, chosen by reasoning over attributes + spatial geometry

  • CVPRVLM-Grounder: A VLM Agent for Zero-Shot 3D Visual Grounding — 2D-only VLM grounding reaches 51.6% on ScanRefer
  • CVPRDeRVOS: Decoupling Trajectory Generation for Referring Video Segmentation — temporal consistency + multimodal fusion for video referring
  • ROBOThinkGrasp: A Vision-Language System for Strategic Part Grasping in Clutter — reasons about occlusion to grasp heavily-occluded referents

Promptable segmentation (SAM-based)

both
the problem

Segmentation traditionally needs dense pixel-level supervision to produce accurate masks. SAM flips the model: given a prompt — a point, a box, or text — output a mask for anything, trained on 1B masks so it generalizes to any object without category labels.

the approaches

Segment Anything takes a prompt — a point, box, or text — and returns a mask for any object, no retraining. Robots use it to cut out arbitrary objects specified on the fly.

Adapters, LLM-driven prompting, and auto-labeling extend SAM to robot tasks.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 1024×1024 image; SAM image encoder (ViT-H) processes it once → 64×64×256 feature grid
  2. Prompt: a single click at pixel (450, 310) on a coffee mug
  3. Prompt encoder: point → 256-d positional embedding + learned ‘foreground’ token
  4. Mask decoder: 2-layer transformer attends over the 64×64 grid using the prompt tokens → 3 candidate masks (whole/part/subpart)
  5. Ambiguity resolution: select mask with highest IoU prediction score (0.91 for the ‘whole mug’ option)
  6. Output: 1024×1024 binary mask with 94.2% boundary IoU — any object, no category name needed
  • ROBOGraspSAM: When Segment Anything Meets Grasp Detection — light adapters turn SAM into a promptable grasp detector
  • ROBOSAM-Rectified Part Detection with Iterative Pose Refinement — fine-tuned SAM recognizes functional parts across objects
  • CVPRMovSAM: Single-Image Moving Object Segmentation via Deep Thinking — LLM reasoning generates prompts for SAM to infer motion
  • ROBOLow-Effort Iterative Dataset Generation for Instance Segmentation — change detection + SAM auto-labels novel objects

Open-vocabulary 3D scene understanding

both
the problem

3D scene understanding has always needed category-specific 3D labels — bounding-box annotations in point clouds for each class. Acquiring these is expensive, and a closed label set means the 3D understanding is as limited as the 2D one was.

the approaches

Bring open-vocabulary labels into 3D: parse a scene — points, voxels, or Gaussians — against arbitrary text, so a robot understands space by language without a closed 3D label set.

Reconstruction quality and 2D-to-3D fusion are the bottlenecks.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 60-frame RGB video of a living room; SfM gives camera poses + sparse point cloud (12,000 pts)
  2. Dense reconstruction: CLIP3R builds a dense 3D volume at 4cm voxel resolution → 38,000 occupied voxels
  3. Feature per voxel: project each voxel into all frames where it’s visible; average their CLIP-ViT-L/14 patch features → 768-d descriptor
  4. Query ‘tv stand’: encode text → 768-d T; for each voxel compute cosine score
  5. Top-k cluster: 847 voxels score > 0.42; DBSCAN (ε=8cm) → 1 cluster = the tv stand instance
  6. Result: 3D bounding box [0.8m × 0.4m × 0.6m] located at (2.3m, 0.3m, 1.1m) — no 3D label ever applied
  • CVPROv3R: Open-Vocabulary Semantic 3D Reconstruction from RGB Videos — CLIP3R fuses 2D-3D for instance-level open-vocab reconstruction
  • CVPRCoSMo3D: Promptable 3D Semantic Segmentation via LLM-Guided Canonical Space — LLM-guided canonical modeling for open-world 3D segmentation
  • CVPROpenVoxel: Training-Free Grouping and Captioning of Voxels — MLLM text reasoning over 3D voxels, training-free
  • ROBOOpenVox: Real-Time Instance-Level Open-Vocabulary Voxels — incremental voxel SLAM with VLM-grounded instances

Language-embedded feature fields

both
the problem

A 3D scene is continuous — you want to ask 'what is at position (x,y,z)?' and get a language-aligned answer. Discrete voxels waste memory; a continuous implicit field stores a feature at every point, but distilling 2D foundation-model knowledge into it is the hard part.

the approaches

Distill 2D foundation-model features (CLIP, DINO) into a continuous 3D field, so querying any 3D location returns a language-aligned feature — open-vocabulary 3D with no 3D annotation.

Efficiency (dense features are heavy) and view-consistency are the work.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scene: tabletop with 5 objects; 100 posed RGB-D frames → 3D Gaussians via 3DGS (50,000 splats)
  2. Feature distillation: for each Gaussian g, project its center into every visible frame; bilinearly sample Alpha-CLIP features → average → 512-d feature F_g stored on g
  3. Query ‘handle of the mug’: encode text → 512-d T; for each splat compute cos(F_g, T)
  4. Top-ranked splats: 312 splats score > 0.55, covering the mug handle region
  5. Grasp sampling: fit a grasp pose to the top-ranked splat cluster → 6-DOF grasp at the handle
  6. Execution: robot grasps at the handle pose with 89% success vs. 41% for whole-object grasping — the language embedding pays off in action
  • ROBOGraspSplats: Efficient Manipulation with 3D Feature Splatting — distills features into 3D Gaussians for grasp sampling in 60 s
  • ROBOLensDFF: Language-Enhanced Sparse Feature Distillation for Grasping — view-consistent 2D features onto point clouds for dexterous grasps
  • ROBODVEFormer: Efficient Dense Visual Embeddings via Distillation — distills Alpha-CLIP into an RGB-D transformer at 26–77 FPS
  • CVPRExtrinSplat: Decoupling Geometry and Semantics in 3D Scenes — separates geometry and semantics in Gaussian splatting

3D visual grounding & spatial localization

vision
the problem

In a 3D point cloud, 'the chair to the left of the desk' requires simultaneously understanding object identity in 3D geometry, spatial relations between objects, and language-to-3D correspondence — three hard problems at once, usually without 3D training data.

the approaches

Ground language directly in 3D — point to the region a phrase means in a point cloud or splat — often zero-shot, without 3D training data, by reasoning over geometry and relations.

Reasoning-chain distillation makes it fast enough to run.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scene: ScanNet point cloud, 41,000 points, 9 object instances detected
  2. Query: ‘the chair to the left of the desk’
  3. Zero-shot object recognition: project 3D to 5 viewpoints → CLIP scores for [‘chair’,‘desk’,‘table’,‘lamp’,...] per instance
  4. Candidate chairs: instances #3 (score 0.61) and #7 (score 0.58); desk = instance #5 (score 0.73)
  5. Spatial relation: compute signed angle from desk to each chair candidate in the horizontal plane; chair #7 is at +87° (left) vs chair #3 at -110° (right)
  6. Result: instance #7 selected; 3D box returned in 130ms at 7.69 FPS — zero 3D grounding labels used

Open-world discovery & novel categories

both
the problem

An open-world robot encounters objects that are not in any known category. Rather than force them onto the nearest known label, the system must detect the anomaly, flag it as unknown, and optionally cluster new instances into a new category — the basis for safe lifelong learning.

the approaches

Rather than force every object onto a known class, treat the unfamiliar as an anomaly in feature space — discover, flag, or learn genuinely new categories, the basis for lifelong, safe perception.

Anomaly framing and novel-category embedding are the recurring tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Deployment: agricultural robot trained on {crops, soil, sky}; encounters a weed species not in training set
  2. Feature extraction: backbone extracts 512-d feature for the unknown region; 0.41 cosine distance to nearest known class ‘soil’
  3. Anomaly score: threshold = 0.35; region scores 0.41 → flagged as anomaly (not forced to ‘soil’)
  4. Clustering: over 200 frames, 37 anomaly crops of this weed are collected; k-means with k=1 → one new cluster
  5. Prototype formed: mean feature = 512-d prototype for ‘weed-type-A’; added to known set
  6. Subsequent precision: recall for ‘weed-type-A’ rises to 0.82 on the next 100 frames — new category learned from anomaly detection alone
  • CVPRZoo3D: Zero-Shot 3D Object Detection at Scene Level — training-free 3D detection via SAM + graph clustering
  • CVPRNoOVD: Novel Category Discovery for Open-Vocabulary Detection — metric learning discovers and embeds novel categories
  • ROBOZero-Shot Semantic Segmentation for Robots in Agriculture — crops as hyperspheres in feature space; weeds are anomalies
  • ROBOOoDIS: Anomaly Instance Segmentation and Detection Benchmark — a benchmark for detecting genuinely unknown objects

Part segmentation & fine-grained understanding

vision
the problem

Object-level recognition tells you 'mug' but not 'handle of the mug' — the part a robot arm must actually grasp. Segmenting parts requires combining coarse global context (what is the object?) with fine local attention (where is the handle within it?), with no fixed part taxonomy.

the approaches

Go below the object to its parts — the handle, the blade, the wheel — combining global semantics with fine attention, without a predefined part taxonomy. Parts are what manipulation actually acts on.

The hard split is over-segmenting an object versus missing a small part.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Image: a ceramic mug on a table; CLIP global feature matches ‘mug’ at cosine 0.74
  2. Part query: ‘handle of the mug’ → 1024-d text embedding T_part
  3. HOPS hierarchical attention: layer 22 attention heads (spatial) filtered by cosine(head_output, T_part); 3 of 16 heads activate strongly on the right side of the mug
  4. Affinity propagation: from the 3 activated patch tokens, propagate similarity across the patch grid → 14×14 part heatmap
  5. Threshold + upsample: 11 patches above 0.40 → binary mask upsample to 448×448
  6. Result: part mask with 71.3% IoU vs. annotated handle on PartImageNet — enabling a successful pinch grasp at the correct point
  • CVPRHOPS: Hierarchical Open-Vocabulary Part Segmentation — attention filtering + affinity propagation for parts
  • CVPRPCA-Seg: Cost Aggregation for Semantic & Part Segmentation — unified semantic and part segmentation
  • CVPRSRA-Det: Detection Beyond Category Names — multi-facet decomposition reaches attributes and parts
  • CVPRPart-Aware Vision-Language Models for Fine-Grained Recognition — explicit part alignment inside a VLM

Language-driven affordance grounding

robotics
the problem

For a robot, the perception question is not just 'what is it?' but 'where do I interact with it, given my task?' A VLM that recognizes 'mug' doesn't automatically know the handle is the grasp point, and the pour spout is the pour point — affordances must be grounded from task descriptions.

the approaches

For a robot, the useful open-vocabulary question isn’t just ‘what is it?’ but ‘where do I act?’ — grounding a task in words to the object part and pose to grasp or push.

VLMs reason about task-relevant affordances zero-shot, from implicit instructions.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Task instruction: ‘pour the coffee into the cup’
  2. VLM reasoning: AffordGrasp queries the VLM: ‘given the instruction, which part of the mug affords pouring?’ → answer: ‘hold the handle and tilt toward the spout’
  3. Part grounding: open-vocab part segmenter locates ‘handle’ (IoU 0.68) and ‘spout’ (IoU 0.61) on the mug
  4. Grasp point: centroid of handle mask + grasp axis perpendicular to handle long axis → 6-DOF grasp pose
  5. Motion direction: spout centroid defines the tilt axis; plan 45° tilt toward cup
  6. Execution: robot completes pour in 4.2s with 91% success — vs 44% for whole-object random grasping without affordance grounding

  • ROBORoboPoint: A VLM for Spatial Affordance Prediction — predicts image keypoint affordances, zero-shot across scenes
  • ROBOMOKA: Open-World Manipulation through Mark-Based Visual Prompting — affordance reasoning as VLM question-answering
  • ROBOSemSegGrasp: Task-Oriented Grasping via Semantic Segmentation — open-vocab part segmentation drives task grasps

Open-vocab for manipulation & navigation

robotics
the problem

A mobile manipulator given 'fetch the green bottle from the counter' needs to navigate to the counter, find the green bottle among other objects, and grasp it — three steps that each require open-vocabulary perception, with no fixed class list covering every possible instruction.

the approaches

The payoff on real robots: open-vocabulary perception feeding action — grab or go to the object named in plain language, even if it was never a training class.

Open-vocab maps and grasping close the loop from words to motion.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Instruction: ‘fetch the green bottle from the counter’
  2. Navigation: open-vocab map queried for ‘counter’ → CLIP similarity peak in map at position (4.2m, 1.8m); robot navigates there
  3. Local detection: at the counter, open-vocab detector runs prompt=‘green bottle’; 3 candidate boxes returned, scores [0.79, 0.61, 0.38]
  4. Grounding: ‘green bottle’ grounding: verify color (HSV hue=130°, saturation=0.6 → green ✓) on top box
  5. Grasp: feature-field splats on the bottle queried for ‘body of bottle’ → grasp point at centroid (pixel 342, 218) → 6-DOF pose
  6. Task completion: 83% success across 48 trials on objects never in training — pure language-to-action via open vocab

Video temporal & 4D open-vocabulary

vision
the problem

Video adds a time axis to every open-vocabulary problem: a phrase like 'when the person picks up the mug' must be grounded to a temporal interval, not just a spatial box, and the object's identity must persist across frames as it moves.

the approaches

Extend open vocabulary through time: localize the moment or the moving object a phrase describes in a video, or query a dynamic 4D scene — adding identity tracking to the semantic problem.

Hierarchical embeddings and instance-aware 4D fields drive it.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Video: 3-minute kitchen clip at 30fps = 5,400 frames; query: ‘when the person picks up the mug’
  2. Temporal proposal: HERO generates 200 candidate temporal windows at multiple scales (2s, 5s, 10s)
  3. Hierarchical embed: for each window, extract 4-level features (frame → clip → scene → event); cross-modal filter with query embedding
  4. Top window: 27.4s–31.8s gets score 0.84; second best is 0.61; gap > 0.2 → confident prediction
  5. Within-window grounding: frame 28.1s: open-vocab detector finds ‘mug’ at [310, 180, 380, 260]; CLIP confirms ‘mug’ cosine 0.78
  6. 4D track: identity-adaptive Gaussian tracks the mug from frame 820 to 954 (4.5s); temporal IoU 0.73 vs. annotation

  • CVPRST4R-Splat: Spatio-Temporal Referring Segmentation in 4D Gaussians — instance-aware 4D fields with auto MLLM captions

Domain-specific open-vocabulary

vision
the problem

CLIP was trained on internet photos of everyday objects. Remote-sensing imagery (looking down, small-scale objects), underwater images (color shift, scattering), thermal images (no RGB, only heat), and medical images (very different statistics) all break off-the-shelf CLIP features in predictable ways.

the approaches

Take open vocabulary out of everyday photos and into remote sensing, underwater, thermal, and medical imagery — where distribution shift wrecks off-the-shelf VLM features and domain priors must be added.

Attention refinement and cross-modal fusion adapt the backbone.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Domain: thermal infrared image of a street scene (FLIR dataset, 640×480 single-channel 14-bit)
  2. Problem: CLIP-ViT-L/14 cosine similarity for ‘pedestrian’ on thermal patches: mean 0.18 ± 0.11 — barely above noise
  3. Cross-modal distillation: Thermal-Det trains a student thermal backbone to mimic the RGB CLIP teacher’s features on aligned RGB–thermal pairs
  4. Alignment: after 30 epochs, thermal student features for ‘pedestrian’ patches: cosine 0.61 ± 0.09 — 3.4× improvement
  5. Open-vocab detection: thermal-student features fed into Grounding DINO detector with prompt ‘pedestrian’; AP_50 = 71.3 vs. 38.6 for off-the-shelf CLIP
  6. Generalization: the same thermal backbone, prompted with ‘cyclist’ (never in thermal training), scores AP_50 = 58.7 — open vocab preserved across the domain gap

Part IV

Where it stands

Naming anything by language is a young superpower with rough edges. What is genuinely still hard:

Dense precision from a global model. VLMs are trained on whole-image alignment, which fights the pixel-level precision segmentation needs — background bias and geometric drift persist.
Rare words & fine parts. Open vocabulary is strong on common objects but weak on rare descriptions, fine-grained parts, and compositional phrases (‘three to the left of’).
Real-time at dense resolution. Per-pixel VLM inference is slow; sliding-window accuracy costs a large slowdown, and edge deployment forces hard trade-offs.
Feature drift & hallucination. VLM embeddings drift across domains and sessions and confidently mislabel; there is no principled uncertainty for a grounding failure.
Geometry the language ignores. Language names things well but reasons about exact distance and reachability poorly — robot use still needs separate geometric grounding.
Domain shift. Remote-sensing, underwater, thermal, and medical imagery break off-the-shelf features; generalizing across domains stays brittle.
