Shuffle a video's frames and the event is gone. The meaning lives in order, motion, identity over time, and the ability to remember what happened minutes ago. 01 · the problem

A video is not a stack of images

An image classifier sees a single frame and says “door.” Show it the same door in each frame of a video and it still just sees “door” — it has thrown away whether the door opened or closed, who did it, and when. That information lives entirely in the sequence, not in any one picture.

This is the core problem of video understanding. You cannot treat a video as a bag of still images and hope to recover the temporal structure; it is gone the moment you drop the order. Motion, change, causality, and event identity all depend on knowing which frame came before which — and how fast things moved between them.

the actual math — one level deeper

A video x = (f₁, f₂, …, fₒ) is an ordered sequence; a set {fᵢ} loses the permutation σ that carries the causal structure.

Any temporal model must be equivariant to shifts and sensitive to order: P(xᶄ₊₅ | x₁⁻ᶄ) ≠ P(x₊₅⁻ᶄ | x₁⁻ᶄ) in general.

02 · motion

What moved, and how

Between any two frames, every pixel has moved a little or a lot. Optical flow measures that displacement: a dense field of vectors, one per pixel, saying where each point went. The moving person has large vectors; the static wall has nearly zero. That flow field is the raw signal of action — the difference between “the player kicked” and “the camera panned.”

Early work learned two parallel streams — one for appearance, one for flow — and fused their predictions. Modern 3D convolutions and video transformers collapse the two streams into one by treating time as a spatial dimension, but the insight is the same: action recognition is really motion recognition, and separating what moved from what stayed still is the first step.

the actual math — one level deeper

Optical flow (u,v) at pixel (x,y): minimize ||I(x,y,t) − I(x+u,y+v,t+1)||² subject to brightness constancy + regularization (Horn-Schunck).

Two-stream score: P(action) = λ Pᶀ(action|RGB) + (1−λ) Pᵽ(action|flow); 3D convolutions subsume both into spatio-temporal filters K ∈ ℝ^{T×H×W}.

03 · keep identity

Follow the same thing over time

Detection is easy to describe: run a network on a frame, get boxes and labels. Tracking adds a constraint the detector ignores: the same physical object must carry the same identity across every frame it appears in. When two vehicles cross paths and one briefly disappears behind a sign, a tracker must decide which ID reappears on each side — and get it right, or every downstream count and trajectory is wrong.

The standard approach is “tracking by detection”: detect every frame, then associate the detections across time using motion models and appearance features. The failure modes are symmetric: re-identify too aggressively and unrelated objects merge; too conservatively and every occlusion spawns a new ID. Event cameras, which fire per-pixel on change rather than frame by frame, are pushing asynchronous tracking that avoids the occlusion window entirely.

the actual math — one level deeper

Tracking-by-detection: cost Cᵢ₆ = α · IoU(bᵢ,b₆) + (1−α) · d(eᵢ,e₆) between detection i and track j; Hungarian algorithm finds min-cost assignment.

Kalman state x = [cx,cy,s,r,˙cx,˙cy,˙s]ᵀ; predict with constant-velocity, update with detection — SORT's core; re-ID features extend it to Re-ID across occlusion (DeepSORT / ByteTrack).

04 · long horizon

Understanding a whole video

Attention is quadratic in sequence length. A 30-fps video of an hour-long procedure has 108,000 frames — you cannot run a transformer that attends to all of them at once. The field answers this with frame selection, token compression, and structured memory: pick the key moments, compress the rest into a compact representation, and store enough to answer questions about what happened, and when, and why.

The deeper problem is that what counts as a “key moment” depends on the question you will later ask. A frame selection policy that is good for “who won?” may discard the one frame that answers “who fouled first?” This is why the best long-video systems use the question itself to guide selection — and why annotation is so expensive: humans must watch the whole thing to label what matters.

the actual math — one level deeper

Attention cost O(T²D) makes full-video attention prohibitive; memory reduces effective context to M slots: compress x₁⁻ᶄ → m ∈ ℝ^{M×D}, attend only over m.

Frame selection: score sᵢ = fθ(fᵢ, q) for query q, keep top-K frames; learned selection minimizes task loss end-to-end, avoiding heuristic uniform sampling.

05 · predict

Guessing the next frames

A person watching a ball arc upward can predict where it will land. A model that can do the same has implicitly learned the physics of the scene — which is exactly what self-supervised video pretraining exploits. Predict the next frames (or the masked patches, or the future flow field) from unlabeled video, and the model acquires temporal structure for free, without a single human label.

The trap is multimodality. At each step the future genuinely branches: the ball might be caught or drop; the pedestrian might turn left or right. A model minimizing mean squared error averages all those futures into a smeared, confident wrong answer. This is why prediction models now use diffusion or flow-matching: sample one sharp future from the distribution rather than commit to the blurry mean. The resulting models double as world models for planning.

the actual math — one level deeper

Next-frame loss: L = ||f̂ᶄ₊₁ − fᶄ₊₁||² collapses to the conditional mean E[fᶄ₊₁ | f₁⁻ᶄ], blurring multimodal futures.

Flow / diffusion prediction: sample f̂ᶄ₊₁ ~ pθ(fᶄ₊₁ | f₁⁻ᶄ) via reverse SDE or ODE — a single sharp draw from the conditional, not the mean.

Part II

The benefits people actually get

Every family below leans on one or more of these — the recurring reasons to model time rather than treat each frame as a fresh image.

Temporal binding across sparsity

Video understanding compresses the crucial moments out of hours of footage and binds them into a coherent story — the same event across widely-separated frames.

Motion as first-class signal

Treating optical flow and inter-frame change as primary inputs catches the action that a per-frame classifier throws away.

Identity through occlusion

Multi-object tracking re-attaches identities after objects cross or disappear, turning a stream of detections into consistent tracklets for counting, safety, and behavior analysis.

Memory for long video

Learned compression and frame selection make it possible to answer questions about a full-length lecture or procedure without attending to every frame.

Self-supervision from unlabeled video

Predicting future frames, masked patches, or flow fields trains temporal models at zero annotation cost — the internet supplies the curriculum.

01

Long-form video understanding

vision
the problem

Hour-long videos produce ~108,000 frames at 30 fps — no attention mechanism can hold all of them at once, so the model must decide what to discard before answering a question.

the approaches

Hour-long videos — lectures, procedures, sports matches — demand models that select which moments to attend to and compress the rest into a compact memory the system can query later.

The binding constraint is attention cost: storing and attending to every token in an hour of 30-fps video is not feasible, so learned selection and memory are the problem.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. 30 fps × 3600 s = 108,000 frames; even at 1 token/frame that’s 108K tokens — far past any transformer window.
  2. Score each frame with a saliency function s_i = f(frame_i, query); rank and keep top K=64.
  3. Compress the 64 selected frames into a memory bank M ∈ ℝ64×768 via mean-pooled visual tokens.
  4. A question “what happened after they shook hands?” cross-attends to M; retrieval score = softmax(q·MT / √768).
  5. Without selection: full-video QA accuracy ~41%; with learned selection: +18 pp on EgoSchema.
  • CVPR
    Visual Memory Mechanism for Multimodal Large Language Models (VMem) — compressed key-frame memory bank; selective retrieval for long-form QA

    Hour-long videos produce tens of thousands of frames, far exceeding any transformer's context window. VMem attacks this with compressed key-frame memory: extract the most informative frames using a saliency function, then store their features in a compact bank that a language model can query later with learned cross-attention. Instead of asking 'what is every frame?' the system asks 'what is in these few critical moments?'

    how it actually works

    The problem: Full-video attention over 108,000 frames (30 fps × 3600 s) is O(T²D) = prohibitive. Key-frame compression collapses the temporal axis.

    The mechanism: (1) Score each frame with a learned saliency function s_i = f_θ(frame_i), ranking frames by information content. (2) Keep top-K frames (typically K=64); discard the rest. (3) Extract visual features from selected frames via a frozen vision backbone; mean-pool tokens → memory bank M ∈ ℝ^(K×D). (4) At query time, cross-attend: attention_weights = softmax(q·M^T / √D), then aggregate. This reduces the effective sequence length from 108K tokens to ~16K (64 frames × 256 tokens/frame).

    Why it works: Most frames in long video are redundant (static background, repeated poses). By selecting only truly unseen frames, the model keeps answer-relevant content while discarding noise, raising VQA accuracy ~18 pp on EgoSchema.

  • CVPR
    Wavelet-Based Frame Selection for Long Video Understanding — frequency-domain saliency scores pick informative frames before the LLM

    How do you know which frames matter before you know what question will be asked? Wavelet-based selection uses frequency-domain saliency—treating the video as a time-varying signal and scoring frames by how much their visual content deviates from a low-frequency trend. High-frequency components (sharp changes, new objects, motion) score high; flat regions score low. An LLM downstream answers questions on the selected frames alone.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Heuristic frame selection (uniform sampling, random cropping) discards potentially important moments and keeps redundant ones. Learned selection requires a downstream task label (QA answer) for training.

    The mechanism: (1) Decompose the video signal in the wavelet domain: each frame's visual representation is expressed as a sum of scale-specific basis functions (e.g., Haar, Daubechies wavelets). (2) Low-frequency components capture slow scene changes (lighting, camera pan); high-frequency capture rapid motion and object boundaries. (3) Score frame i by its high-frequency energy: s_i = ||ψ_high(frame_i)||. (4) Select frames where s_i exceeds a threshold or top-K by magnitude. (5) Pass selected frames to an LLM for reasoning.

    Why it works: Frequency-domain saliency is task-agnostic and does not require labeled data, making it practical for diverse video types (lectures, sports, movies). Wavelet decomposition captures motion automatically; a frame where an object suddenly appears has high-frequency content.

  • CVPR
    WorldMM — Multimodal Memory Agent for Long Video — agentic memory agent with retrieval for open-domain long video

    Long video understanding is not just compression; it is agentic retrieval. WorldMM models the video as a dynamic world state and maintains a memory agent that iteratively retrieves and reasons over relevant moments. Rather than statically selecting key frames at the start, the memory agent re-ranks and refocuses based on partial answers and follow-up queries, effectively building a mental model of the video that can answer open-domain questions without seeing the full footage upfront.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Static key-frame selection is fixed once; if the first retrieved frames do not answer the question, the system cannot adaptively search for more relevant moments. Agents can iterate.

    The mechanism: (1) Divide video into chunks; encode each chunk into a summary representation (caption, visual embedding). (2) Initialize memory buffer M with top-K retrieved chunks using the question as a query. (3) Run a reasoning loop: generate partial answer A_t from M; score how well A_t addresses the question; if confidence < threshold, retrieve the next-most-relevant chunk and add to M. (4) Repeat until high confidence or memory full. (5) Return final answer. Each retrieval is a learned dense retrieval operation (softmax over chunk similarities).

    Why it works: Iterative retrieval mirrors human behavior (scan, check, refocus, scan again). It adapts to question difficulty: simple 'what color' questions terminate early; complex 'why did they...' questions retrieve more evidence. Memory limits are soft (agent can evict old chunks), avoiding the fixed-K bottleneck.

  • CVPR
    HieraMamba — Anchor-Mamba Hierarchical Pooling for Long-Range Video — anchor tokens + Mamba SSM give sub-quadratic long-range context

    Attention is quadratic; state-space models like Mamba are linear. HieraMamba combines the best of both by using anchor tokens—a learned hierarchy of key-frame representations—and processing them through a Mamba layer, which is a gated linear recurrent network. Anchor tokens serve as keyhole summaries of long-range structure; Mamba scans them in sequence, updating a compact hidden state. This gives sub-quadratic cost with global receptivity, enabling whole-video reasoning on hour-long footage.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Transformers do full pairwise attention (O(T²)), prohibitive for T=108,000. Sparse attention patterns (local, strided) reduce cost but lose global structure. Mamba has O(T) cost but processes a single sequence; how do you inject video hierarchy?

    The mechanism: (1) Hierarchically pool frame features: frames → 4-frame patches via mean pooling; patches → 16-frame segments; segments → key moments (anchors). Anchor selection uses learned saliency scores. (2) Extract anchor embeddings a_1, a_2, ..., a_M ∈ ℝ^D (M~100 anchors from 108K frames). (3) Feed anchor sequence through a Mamba SSM layer: h_t = σ(W_h h_{t-1} + B x_t), where x_t = a_t and σ is a gating function. The hidden state h_t is O(D), constant per step. (4) At each anchor, attend to all past anchors implicitly via the hidden state (Mamba's implicit recurrence). (5) Upsample final anchor output back to frame-level predictions via learned upsampling.

    Why it works: Mamba's linear recurrence compresses the entire past into a fixed-size state, avoiding the quadratic blowup. Anchor pooling sacrifices temporal resolution (microsecond precision) in exchange for the global picture. Subquadratic cost unlocks hour-long reasoning.

Video object segmentation & tracking

vision
the problem

Propagating a pixel-level mask across frames means every pixel must carry the same identity through motion blur, occlusion, and scale change — a constraint that per-frame segmentation completely ignores.

the approaches

Pixel-level segmentation must propagate a mask across frames, keeping the same identity through motion blur, occlusion, and scale change — harder than detection and harder than single-frame segmentation.

Referring expressions and language ground the segment to ‘the red car on the left’ rather than every car in the frame.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Frame 1: annotate the target region with a binary mask M1 ∈ {0,1}H×W.
  2. Extract a reference feature e_ref = Encoder(f1 ⊙ M1) ∈ ℝ256.
  3. At frame t: compute pixel affinity At = softmax(et · e_refT / √256); warp M1 to get M̃t.
  4. Predicted mask: Mt = threshold(At, 0.5); IoU with ground truth on DAVIS-2017 ≈ 0.87.
  5. At occlusion: affinity drops — set Mt = last valid Mt-k; re-id when affinity recovers above threshold.
  • CVPR
    DeRVOS — Decoupled Referring Video Object Segmentation — decouples segmentation from grounding for better referring VOS

    Referring video object segmentation must do two things at once: segment a pixel-level mask and ground it to a language phrase like 'the red car on the left.' Doing both jointly couples errors—a misgrounded phrase or a mislabeled region throws the whole system off. DeRVOS decouples segmentation from grounding: first segment all regions (query-agnostic), then re-rank and refine based on the language query. This two-stage design isolates failures and allows the segmentation backbone to focus purely on mask coherence over time.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Joint segmentation+grounding (end-to-end) backpropagates errors from both tasks, making it hard to separate mask quality from grounding quality. A bad grounding can corrupt a good mask and vice versa.

    The mechanism: Stage 1 (Segmentation): (1) Extract per-frame visual features and optical flow. (2) For each frame t, generate region proposals (candidates for object boundaries) using a frame-agnostic segmentation network (e.g., SAM, FCN). (3) Propagate masks across frames using affinity (feature similarity): M_t = softmax(e_t · e_ref^T / √D), where e_ref is the first-frame query region. (4) Refine with motion consistency: if optical flow predicts pixel should be at (x+u, y+v), align masks accordingly. Stage 2 (Grounding): (5) Embed the referring phrase q (e.g., 'red car') into q ∈ ℝ^D. (6) Score each generated mask region r by similarity: score(r, q) = r_embed · q. (7) Select the top-scoring region. Return as the final segmentation.

    Why it works: Decoupling allows segmentation to optimize for temporal coherence (same object, same mask) independently of language. Grounding becomes a lightweight re-ranking step at the end. Errors in one stage do not corrupt the other.

  • CVPR
    SPARROW — Pixel-Grounded Video MLLM — pixel-grounded multimodal LLM answers questions about specific video regions

    Most video multimodal LLMs ground language in frames (global understanding). SPARROW goes deeper with pixel-level grounding: when you ask 'what is the person in the red jacket doing?', the model points to the exact pixels forming that person, binds their features across frames, and answers based on their specific trajectory and actions. This pixel-level binding transforms 'the whole video' into 'this specific region' that can be tracked and reasoned about independently.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Frame-level understanding conflates multiple objects. 'The person' in a crowd is ambiguous. Grounding to pixels removes ambiguity and enables spatial+temporal reasoning over a specific entity.

    The mechanism: (1) Segment the video into superpixels or use a pretrained segmentation model to extract candidate regions. (2) For a given language query (e.g., 'person in red jacket'), embed the query: q ∈ ℝ^D. (3) Score each pixel/region by alignment with the query using contrastive learning: score(pixel, q) = softmax(pixel_embed · q / √D). (4) Extract the top-scored pixels and propagate their identity across frames using optical flow and feature affinity. (5) Collect temporal features of the selected pixels across all frames: trajectory_features ∈ ℝ^(T×F). (6) Feed trajectory features to the language model along with the question. (7) Generate answer grounded in the specific pixels.

    Why it works: Pixel grounding reduces the problem from 'all 1920×1080 pixels' to 'the 500 pixels forming this person.' The model can track that specific region, extract its motion and appearance, and reason precisely. Referring expressions naturally map to pixel regions.

  • CVPR
    DTPTrack — Drift-Resilient Temporal Prior for Video Object Tracking — temporal prior from prior frames corrects drift through occlusion

    Single-frame object trackers drift over time: small detection errors compound into large position errors. The temporal prior is the key: use predictions from previous frames to constrain the current detection. DTPTrack models the temporal trajectory as a smooth path and refines detections by minimizing drift from this path, effectively using past frames as an anchor to keep the tracker honest.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Frame-by-frame detection has independent errors; errors do not correct, they accumulate. A ±2 pixel detection error per frame becomes ±200 pixels over 100 frames. A temporal model can diffuse the error back to earlier frames and smooth the trajectory.

    The mechanism: (1) Run a detector on each frame independently: d_t = detector(frame_t). (2) Extract a temporal prior from past detections: given d_1, d_2, ..., d_{t-1}, predict d_t^prior via a motion model (constant-velocity Kalman filter or a learned RNN). (3) Compute refinement residual: Δd_t = d_t - d_t^prior (this is the detector's update relative to the prior). (4) Score the residual by smoothness: if Δd_t is very large or spatially incoherent, downweight it; small, smooth updates are trustworthy. (5) Fused detection: d_t^final = α · d_t + (1-α) · d_t^prior, where α is learned from the residual quality. (6) At occlusion (detector fails), rely entirely on d_t^prior; when the detector recovers, re-synchronize. (7) Propagate d_t^final to frame t+1.

    Why it works: By explicitly modeling temporal continuity, the tracker resists single-frame noise. Occlusion handling is built in: when the detector is occluded and silent, the prior continues the trajectory, and re-identification happens when the detector fires again with high confidence.

  • CVPR
    ADTrack — RGB-T Tracking with Auxiliary Depth — depth-modal auxiliary features for reliable thermal-RGB multi-modal tracking

    Thermal cameras see heat; RGB cameras see color. Neither alone is reliable: thermal fails in uniform temperatures, RGB fails in darkness. ADTrack fuses RGB and thermal streams with auxiliary depth, treating depth as a shared representation that both modalities can ground in. The depth map acts as a geometric anchor: 'the heat signature at (x, y) and the red car at (x, y) are both at depth 5 m,' confirming they are the same object.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Multi-modal tracking (RGB+thermal) can confuse cross-modal correspondences. The thermal signature and RGB detection may be of different objects if no shared reference frame exists. Depth is that reference.

    The mechanism: (1) For each frame triple (RGB, thermal, depth): extract visual features from RGB (CNN), thermal features from thermal sensor (thermal CNN), and geometric features from depth (depth encoder). (2) Align features to a shared coordinate system using depth: RGB pixel at (x_rgb, y_rgb) with depth z_depth maps to 3D point P_rgb = (x_rgb·z/f, y_rgb·z/f, z). Similarly, thermal pixel (x_th, y_th) → P_th. (3) Score correspondence between detections in different modalities by 3D distance: C_ij = ||P_i^RGB - P_j^Thermal||. (4) Run Hungarian assignment on C_ij to match RGB and thermal detections. (5) Fused detection: concatenate [RGB_features, Thermal_features, Depth_features] → detection_final. (6) Propagate identity: track ID_t by matching fused features across frames using Kalman filtering on the 3D positions. (7) If thermal-only or RGB-only detection appears (the other modality occluded), use depth to extrapolate the identity.

    Why it works: Depth breaks the ambiguity between modalities by providing a coordinate system both agree on. An object in the RGB frame and the thermal frame at the same 3D position must be the same. Auxiliary modalities (depth, IR, LiDAR) are powerful tiebreakers in multi-modal fusion.

Temporal action recognition & detection

both
the problem

Localizing an action means finding both what happened and when it started and ended — a two-dimensional search that detection-only systems completely miss.

the approaches

Action recognition classifies a clip; temporal action detection must also find when each action starts and ends in a long video — a temporal segmentation problem on top of the recognition problem.

Self-supervised ranking and tokenization compress the motion signal without requiring per-frame action labels.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 30-fps video; target: “shot” action lasting ~60 frames at t=240.
  2. Extract 3D-conv features at 4 fps → 8 feature vectors per 2 seconds.
  3. Temporal proposal network generates ~200 anchor spans; score each with P(action|span).
  4. NMS with IoU threshold 0.4 → keep top-5; best span: t=[238, 301], tIoU=0.88.
  5. Without temporal modeling (per-frame only): tIoU=0.31 — misses start/end entirely.
  • CVPR
    A Stitch in Time — Self-Supervised Temporal Action Localization via Plackett-Luce Ranking — ranking-based self-supervised TAL without action labels

    Temporal action detection requires a label for every action boundary in every video—expensive. 'A Stitch in Time' uses Plackett-Luce ranking, a self-supervised signal: given a shuffled sequence of action clips from the same video, the model must rank them in their original temporal order. Learning this ordering implicitly teaches the model temporal structure and action timing without requiring any action labels.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Supervised action detection needs (start_frame, end_frame, action_label) for training. Annotation is expensive. Self-supervised methods avoid labels by using the video itself as the signal.

    The mechanism: (1) Extract N short clips from a video (e.g., 2-second segments). Shuffle their order into a random permutation σ. (2) Embed each clip via a temporal encoder: e_i = f_θ(clip_i). (3) Predict the original temporal order via a ranking model: P(order σ | e_1, ..., e_N). Use Plackett-Luce, which models the probability of a permutation as a product of conditional probabilities: P(σ) = ∏_i P(σ(i) ranked highest among remaining | e_{σ(i)}, ...); this is a differentiable ranking loss. (4) Optimize θ by maximizing log P(σ_true). (5) After pretraining, fine-tune on downstream action detection with few labels. The encoder has learned temporal coherence: clips that are close in time should have similar embeddings; clips far apart should diverge.

    Why it works: Temporal ordering is a natural signal present in any video—no annotation required. The model learns to distinguish 'before' and 'after' implicitly, which transfers to detecting action boundaries (where the temporal pattern changes sharply). Ranking loss is differentiable and scales to long videos.

  • CVPR
    Learnable Motion-Focused Tokenization for Video Understanding — motion-aware tokenizer reduces redundant static tokens

    Video tokens are not all equal: a static background token carries almost no action information, while a pixel showing hand motion is crucial. Motion-focused tokenization allocates tokens dynamically based on motion saliency: high-motion regions get more tokens; static regions are compressed to a single token. This reduces redundant tokens without sacrificing the action signal.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Naive video tokenization treats every spatial region equally. Most of a sports video is static (empty stands, static field); a few regions (players, ball) are dynamic. Allocating equal tokens wastes capacity.

    The mechanism: (1) Compute optical flow between adjacent frames: flow_t = compute_flow(frame_t, frame_{t+1}). (2) For each spatial patch, compute motion magnitude: m_i = ||flow_i||. (3) Learn a motion saliency score: s_i = MLP(m_i, visual_feature_i). High motion = high score. (4) Allocate tokens to patches proportional to score: n_tokens_i = round(base_tokens * s_i / Σ s). (5) Tokenize each patch with n_tokens_i tokens (more motion → finer discretization; less motion → single or two tokens). (6) Concatenate all patch tokens into a sequence. This sequence is shorter than dense tokenization (fewer static tokens) but retains motion details. (7) Feed into a transformer or SSM for action recognition.

    Why it works: Motion is the primary signal for action; static regions are confounders. By allocating tokens to motion, the model achieves higher accuracy with fewer total tokens. Inference is faster (fewer tokens = shorter sequence). The allocation is learned from the task loss, so it adapts to different video types.

  • CVPR
    TRCoRSurg — Triplet Recognition for Surgical Action Detection — instrument-verb-target triplet recognition in surgical video

    Surgical actions are not atomic; they are structured as triplets: instrument + verb + target. A surgeon 'cuts with scissors' (instrument=scissors, verb=cut, target=tissue). TRCoRSurg recognizes these triplets independently, then verifies they are consistent (cannot cut without scissors, cannot suture without needle). This triplet structure makes action recognition more interpretable and more reliable to unseen combinations.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Flat action labels like 'suturing' are vague: who is suturing what with which tool? Triplets provide structure. They also generalize: 'cutting' + instrument-agnostic allows transfer learning (cutting with scissors vs. knife).

    The mechanism: (1) For each frame, extract features: f_frame ∈ ℝ^D via a 3D CNN. (2) Classify instrument: P(instrument | f_frame) = softmax(W_inst · f_frame). Typical instruments: scissors, needle, forceps, probe. (3) Classify verb (action primitive): P(verb | f_frame) = softmax(W_verb · f_frame). Verbs: cut, tie, suture, retract. (4) Classify target (anatomy): P(target | f_frame) = softmax(W_target · f_frame). Targets: tissue, vessel, membrane. (5) Combine triplet probabilities: P(triplet | f_frame) = P(inst) · P(verb) · P(target). (6) Enforce hard constraints via a rule engine: 'cutting requires scissors or knife'; 'suturing requires needle'; reject triplets violating these rules (set P=0). (7) Temporally smooth: use HMM or CRF to enforce that instrument, verb, and target do not change randomly frame-to-frame. (8) Predict the final action as the most probable consistent triplet over a window of frames.

    Why it works: Triplet structure factorizes the action space, reducing the number of labels needed (3 × K instruments + M verbs + N targets ≪ K·M·N combined labels). Constraints prevent impossible combinations. Temporal smoothing reduces jitter. The model is interpretable: you can trace which instrument/verb/target led to each prediction.

  2. Embed question q ∈ ℝ512; embed each frame fi ∈ ℝ512; score si = q · fi / ‖q‖‖fi‖.
  3. Select top-8 frames (score > 0.72); pass to VLM for answer generation.
  4. VLM sees 8 frames × ~256 tokens = 2048 tokens vs 18K raw frames × 256 = 4.6M tokens.

'Select Less, Reason More' filters out distractor frames before feeding to the reasoning model, raising question accuracy by focusing the model's finite context on truly relevant evidence.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Context windows in LLMs are limited; wasting tokens on irrelevant frames leaves no room for reasoning. Distractor frames (static background, off-topic actions) confuse the model and force it to hypothesize wrongly to make sense of the noise.

    The mechanism: (1) Embed the question: q_embed = encoder_text(question) ∈ ℝ^D. (5) The filtering is differentiable (softmax), so the selection can be fine-tuned end-to-end: loss = loss_QA(model(filtered_frames), answer) backpropagates through the relevance scores, teaching the selector to keep frames the QA model actually needs.     Why it works: By removing distractor frames, the reasoning model's attention is focused.     how it actually works

    Why it works: Adaptive routing focuses the model's attention where it matters. A color question automatically emphasizes visual tokens; an audio-centric question emphasizes speech features. This reduces wasted computation and improves answer quality by reducing noise from off-topic modalities.

  3. Horn-Schunck adds smoothness: minimize ∫∫ (Ixu + Iyv + It)2 + λ(|∇u|2 + |∇v|2) dx dy.

The propagation is z_t = α z_{t−1} + β Encoder(x_{t−1}), where α and β are learned. At each denoising step, the model predicts the noise residual ε_θ(x̃_s, z_t, s), refining x̃_s over a short inner loop. The latent code acts as a temporal attention mechanism: pixels that moved are re-encoded in the new frame, but static regions reuse the prior latent, reducing per-frame cost.

  • CVPR
    FloodDiffusion — Streaming Motion Generation for Dynamic Scenes — streaming denoising generates coherent long motion sequences without a fixed horizon

    Generative video models typically predict a fixed number of future frames, which limits applicability to open-ended scenarios. Streaming diffusion generates video frame-by-frame without committing to a horizon, allowing dynamic scenes to unfold naturally by conditioning each new frame on the previous one via a diffusion process.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: At each time step t, the model receives the previous frame x_{t−1} and samples a new frame x_t ~ p_θ(x_t | x_{t−1}). Diffusion sampling involves 1) add noise to x_{t−1}: x_T^{(t−1)} = √(1−β_T) x_{t−1} + √β_T z, 2) run a reverse process for T steps: x_{s−1} = (x_s − √β_s ε_θ(x_s, s, x_{t−1})) / √(1−β_s) + √β_s z, 3) output x_0^{(t)} as the new frame. The key is that the diffusion UNet is conditioned on x_{t−1}, so each frame's generation is grounded in the previous frame rather than free-form. The model learns which motions are consistent with the visual context. Because diffusion is stochastic, multiple samples give diverse futures; streaming allows exploring different branches at each step rather than committing to one trajectory upfront.

Video-language models

vision
the problem

A video has thousands of visual tokens per second; a language model has a fixed context window — the VLM’s core engineering problem is aggressive compression that keeps what the text query cares about and drops everything else.

the approaches

Video-language models align visual tokens from video with language tokens from captions, questions, or instructions — enabling open-vocabulary recognition, captioning, and instruction following.

The bottleneck is token count: a few seconds of video already produces thousands of visual tokens, overwhelming the language model's context window.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. 3 s video at 8 fps = 24 frames × 196 tokens/frame = 4704 visual tokens before any compression.
  2. LLaVA-style VLM context: 4096 tokens; text question ≈ 50 tokens → budget 4046 for video.
  3. Token merging: cluster 4704 tokens by cosine similarity, merge adjacent clusters → 256 tokens.
  4. Compression ratio: 18.4×; VQA accuracy on MVBench: 54% (no compression) → 61% (smart merge) vs 46% (random drop).
  5. Key insight: spatially-redundant (static background) tokens can be merged without temporal loss; motion tokens must be kept.
  • CVPR
    OSMO — Open-Vocabulary Spatial-Motion Emotion Tracking from Video — open-vocabulary emotion trajectory tracking across video with language grounding

    Emotion recognition from video is usually limited to fixed discrete classes (happy, sad, angry). Open-vocabulary models can describe emotions in natural language. A video-language model tracks emotion trajectories over time by grounding language descriptions of emotional state to specific video regions and moments, enabling fine-grained emotion narratives.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: At each frame, a vision encoder produces spatial features F ∈ ℝ^{H×W×D}. A temporal encoder aggregates features across a sliding window of frames. An MLLM takes a language query (e.g., "where is the person and how do they look?") and produces attention weights over space and time. Spatial attention identifies the person; temporal attention highlights frames where emotional state changes. The MLLM decodes an emotion description conditioned on the attended region. To build a trajectory, scores s_i = MLLM("emotion emotion_label at time t_i") are computed for each frame and emotion class, then smoothed temporally with a Kalman filter. The resulting trajectory s(t) shows how emotion evolves. Open-vocabulary means any descriptive phrase (e.g., "anxious about the presentation") can be scored, not just a fixed set.

  • CVPR
    Variation-Aware Vision Token Dropping for Video LLMs — drops spatially redundant tokens while preserving temporal variation

    Video-language models compress visual tokens to fit in an LLM's context window; naive dropping removes tokens uniformly, losing temporal variation. Token dropping that preserves temporal change while discarding static regions maintains downstream reasoning quality with aggressive compression.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: For each spatial location (i, j) and time window, compute the variation V_{ij} = mean(|f_t(i,j) − f_{t−1}(i,j)|) across frames, measuring how much that pixel's features change. Static background regions have V ≈ 0; moving foreground has V > threshold. Tokens are scored by their variation: s_token = V_{ij} + λ · attention_weight (the attention weight from the language query encoder). The top-K tokens by score are kept; the rest are dropped. An extreme case: if 90% of the video is static background (sky, wall), those tokens are dropped entirely, but every motion token is kept. The decoder learns to reconstruct context from the sparse set. This is more effective than random dropping or spatial saliency alone because it exploits the temporal dimension: even a low-saliency region that moves gets preserved because motion implies information.

  • CVPR
    Unified Spatiotemporal Token Compression for Video LLMs — joint spatial + temporal compression reduces video LLM FLOPs 3-5x

    Separate spatial and temporal compression may waste bandwidth on either axis. Joint spatiotemporal token merging optimizes compression across both space and time simultaneously, reducing FLOPs by clustering similar tokens regardless of their location or time.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Extract frame patch tokens from all input frames: F ∈ ℝ^{T×HW×D} where T is time, H×W is the spatial grid, D is embedding dim. Compute pairwise similarity in the embedding space: sim(f_i, f_j) = f_i · f_j / ||f_i|| ||f_j||. Group tokens into K clusters using online k-means or agglomerative clustering. Merge each cluster into a single representative token (mean or learned prototype). The merge operation is spatiotemporal: a static tree at frame 1 and a static tree at frame 100 can merge into the same cluster token if their embeddings are similar, even though they are far apart in time. This is more aggressive than keeping all temporal tokens of a static region. The clustering is differentiable (via Gumbel-softmax or learned attention weights), so the compression ratio is optimized end-to-end on the downstream VQA or captioning task. Result: 3–5× FLOPs reduction while maintaining accuracy.

  • CVPR
    InstAP — Instance-Aware Video Pretraining for Fine-Grained VLMs — instance-level contrastive pretraining for object-centric video-language alignment

    Video-language pretraining often uses clip-level labels (the whole video is about "basketball"), losing instance-level grounding. Instance-level contrastive pretraining aligns individual objects and their motions with language, learning fine-grained object-centric representations that transfer better to fine-grained VQA tasks.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: During pretraining, each video is annotated with object-level captions, e.g., "a red car turning left." A detector finds objects and crops them. For each object instance, a vision encoder extracts features from the cropped region across a temporal window. Contrastive learning pairs the instance features with its corresponding language caption: push the instance embedding close to the caption embedding in a shared space, pull away from other captions. The loss is L = -log exp(sim(e_inst, e_text)/τ) / Σ exp(sim(e_inst, e_other)/τ). Negative examples include other objects in the video and captions from other videos. After pretraining, the learned encoder produces embeddings that are sensitive to object identity and motion. At fine-tuning on a downstream task (e.g., VQA about "which object moved fastest?"), the model can ground queries to specific instances, not just global video features.

Video anomaly & change detection

both
the problem

Anomaly detection in video must flag events it has never seen at training time — which means learning a model of normality tightly enough that a fall, a fight, or an unexpected stop stands out by how much it violates that model.

the approaches

Anomaly detection in video must flag rare, ill-defined events — a fall, a fight, an unexpected stop — without having seen most anomaly types at training time. The core tension is between sensitivity and false-alarm rate.

MLLMs serve as zero-shot anomaly detectors because they carry common-sense about what is normal, reducing the need for annotated anomaly footage.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Train on 10,000 normal clips; no anomaly examples seen during training.
  2. Build normality model: encode each clip ci → ei ∈ ℝ128; store as a memory bank N of 1024 prototypes.
  3. At test time: query bank with etest; anomaly score = 1 − maxj cos(etest, nj).
  4. Threshold at 0.42: true-positive rate 81%, false-positive rate 7% on UCSD Ped2.
  5. MLLM zero-shot baseline: describe scene in text → “man running at unusual speed” → flag; achieves AUC=0.81 without any video training.
  • CVPR
    The Road Less Seen — Weakly-Supervised Video Anomaly Detection — multiple-instance learning from coarse clip-level labels for anomaly localization

    Anomaly detection normally requires labeled anomaly examples; collecting videos of rare events (falls, collisions) is expensive. Multiple-instance learning from coarse clip-level labels ("this clip contains an anomaly somewhere") learns to localize the anomaly without frame-level supervision.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Training data: clips labeled as either "normal" or "contains anomaly" with no temporal annotation of when the anomaly occurs. Slide a temporal window over each clip, computing an anomaly score s_i for each window. For a normal clip, all scores s_i should be low (all windows are normal). For an anomaly clip, at least one window should have high score s_i. The training loss is: L_normal = mean(max(0, threshold − s_i)²) (push normal scores below threshold), L_anom = mean(max(0, s_i − threshold)²) for the highest-scoring window (push at least one window above threshold). A weakly-supervised assumption: the model learns that the highest-scoring window in an anomaly clip corresponds to the actual anomaly event. At test time, a frame-level anomaly score is computed from the sliding windows, and a threshold triggers an alert. No frame-level annotation is needed; the model infers temporal localization from weak clip-level labels.

  • CVPR
    No Need for Real Anomaly — MLLM Zero-Shot Video Anomaly Detection — an MLLM judges normality from scene description without anomaly training data

    Anomaly models require retraining on new environments and new anomaly types. Multimodal language models carry broad common-sense about normal and abnormal behavior. An MLLM zero-shot detector describes the scene in natural language and judges whether it matches a normal context, flagging deviations without any anomaly training data.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Sample a frame from the video; query the MLLM: "Describe the people and actions in this scene." The model outputs a natural-language description, e.g., "A pedestrian walking on a street." Next, ask the MLLM: "Is this description normal? Would you expect to see this in a typical city scene?" The model outputs a confidence score for normality. Repeat for several frames. Abnormal frames get low normality scores. Smooth scores temporally (Kalman filter) and threshold to generate anomaly alerts. The MLLM's common-sense about the physical world (e.g., people don't usually walk backward at high speed, objects don't usually float) serves as the normality model. No retraining is needed for new locations or new anomaly types; the MLLM generalizes because it learned normality from internet-scale training. Trade-off: zero-shot MLLM detection may have more false positives than a trained model, but it requires zero annotation.

  • ROBO
    CC-STAR — Contact-State Anomaly Detection for Robot Assembly — contact-state sequence model detects assembly anomalies from force/tactile signals

    Assembly anomalies (part misalignment, wrong gripper state) are detected by force and tactile feedback, not vision. A temporal state machine learns normal contact sequences during assembly (gripper open → touch part → apply force → release) and flags deviations as anomalies.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Sensors measure 6D force (Fx, Fy, Fz, Τx, Τy, Τz) and tactile pressure at each finger. At each timestep, compute a contact state: 0 = gripper open/no contact, 1 = touch (pressure > threshold but force < threshold), 2 = grasp (pressure > threshold and force > threshold), 3 = insert (Z-force spike), etc. A sequence model (LSTM or HMM) is trained on normal assembly trajectories to predict the next state. At test time, if the observed state diverges from the predicted state, flag anomaly. Example: after step 3 (insert), the model expects state 4 (release); if the Z-force continues rising (state 3 persists), the part may be stuck or misaligned. The model learns that normal sequences have characteristic durations and force profiles; anomalies break these patterns.

  • CVPR
    RAID — Retrieval-Augmented Anomaly Detection for Video Surveillance — retrieves normal prototypes at test time to measure deviation without retraining

    A single normality model may not capture all normal behaviors across time and conditions. Retrieval-augmented detection stores a bank of normal video prototypes and compares each test clip to the nearest prototypes, flagging large deviations at test time without retraining.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Build a prototype bank: during training on normal videos, encode each clip c_i → e_i ∈ ℝ^{128} via a pretrained encoder. Store all embeddings and optionally a low-resolution thumbnail. At test time, encode the test clip c_test → e_test. Compute similarity to all prototypes: sim_j = cos(e_test, e_j). Retrieve the top-K nearest prototypes (K=5). Compute anomaly score as: anomaly = 1 − max_j sim_j (if most similar is far, it's anomalous). Alternatively, fit a Gaussian to the top-K similarities and compute a z-score. The benefit: new environments or seasons can be added without retraining—just add new normal prototypes to the bank. The trade-off: storage grows with the number of normal scenarios; retrieval is O(N) for N prototypes. Approximate nearest neighbor (FAISS) speeds this up.

Video prediction & world models

both
the problem

A video prediction model must choose one sharp future from a distribution of plausible futures — averaging them produces a blurry mean that is wrong about everything, right about nothing.

the approaches

Predicting future frames or states forces a model to learn how the world works — physics, agent intent, scene dynamics — making prediction a general pretraining objective for world models used in planning.

LiDAR-based and vision-language world models extend future prediction beyond RGB to 3D structure and language-specified goals.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Observe 8 frames of a bouncing ball; predict the next 8.
  2. MSE baseline: L = ‖f̂t+1 − ft+1‖2 → optimizes conditional mean E[ft+1 | f1:t]; produces blurry output at the branch point.
  3. Diffusion approach: sample f̂t+1 ~ pθ(ft+1 | f1:t) via 50-step DDPM reverse; SSIM = 0.84 vs MSE 0.61.
  4. Autoregressive rollout: 8 steps → FID of generated 16-frame clips: 18.4 (diffusion) vs 47.2 (MSE).
  5. World-model use: sample 200 future rollouts, score each with a reward model → pick the action that leads to the best predicted future.
  • CVPR
    GEM — Generative Environment Model from LiDAR for Autonomous Driving — LiDAR-conditioned world model predicts future 3D point clouds for planning

    RGB future prediction struggles with occlusion; LiDAR captures 3D structure. A LiDAR-conditioned world model predicts future 3D point clouds by learning scene geometry and agent motion, enabling planning without hallucinating invisible regions.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encoder: A LiDAR point cloud P_t ∈ ℝ^{N×3} is voxelized into a 3D grid V_t ∈ ℝ^{X×Y×Z×C}. A 3D CNN encodes V_t → z_t ∈ ℝ^D. Dynamics: An MLP predicts the latent state at the next timestep: ẑ_{t+1} = f_θ(z_t, a_t) where a_t is the ego-vehicle's action (steering, acceleration). Decoder: ẑ_{t+1} is decoded back into a point cloud P̂_{t+1} via transposed 3D convolution. Loss: chamfer distance between predicted and ground-truth clouds. Why it works: LiDAR penetrates occlusion (though it sees fewer points behind dense objects), so the model learns which regions are likely occupied based on geometry. Unlike RGB, there is no "blur the uncertain parts"—the point cloud is sparse, forcing the model to learn coherent 3D motion. For planning, sample action sequences, predict futures, and score each with a reward model (e.g., collision cost, distance to goal).

  • CVPR
    NavForesee — Vision-Language World Model for Navigation Planning — language-goal-conditioned future-frame prediction guides robot navigation

    Navigation planning often relies on reactive control; long-horizon planning requires predicting where a goal is reachable. A vision-language world model predicts future visual observations conditioned on language goals, enabling a robot to simulate reaching the goal before committing to a path.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encoder: A camera frame x_t and language goal "go to the kitchen" are encoded jointly via a multimodal encoder. Latent state z_t ∈ ℝ^D captures the current visual-semantic state. Dynamics: An LSTM or diffusion model predicts: z_{t+1} = f_θ(z_t, a_t, language_embedding). Action a_t is the robot's motion command (forward, turn, etc.). Decoder: z_{t+1} → predicted frame x̂_{t+1} via a vision decoder (e.g., VAE decoder). Planning: Given a goal, roll out H predictions in imagination: sample a_1, ..., a_H to generate predicted frames x̂_1, ..., x̂_H. Score each rollout with a "goal achievement" metric: sim(x̂_H, goal_image) or an MLLM query "Does this image show a kitchen?". Pick the action sequence with the highest score. The language grounding ensures the model learns to predict visual changes that move toward the goal, not just any plausible future.

  • ROBO
    Dynamics Modeling for High-Speed Off-Road Driving via Video Prediction — video-prediction world model learns terrain dynamics for agile off-road driving

    Terrain dynamics (sand, mud, rocks) affect traction and handling; modeling them from vision alone enables fast adaptation. A video-prediction world model learns terrain-specific dynamics by predicting ego-vehicle camera frames, inferring the underlying terrain response from visual motion cues.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Input: a sequence of front-facing camera frames from high-speed off-road driving. The vehicle is equipped with IMU and motor encoders to label the true ego-motion. Model: An encoder compresses frames into a latent state z_t. A dynamics model predicts z_{t+1} = f_θ(z_t, a_t, terrain_embedding) where a_t is steering/throttle and terrain_embedding is learned per terrain type (or predicted from visual features). A decoder reconstructs x̂_{t+1}. Loss: L2 between predicted and true frames. The model implicitly learns terrain friction, slip angle, and inertia because these affect how pixels move in the next frame. Example: on sand, a hard left turn produces more slip; the model learns this by observing the discrepancy between steering angle and visual rotation. For control, the learned model predicts outcomes of actions; choose the action that leads to the best predicted state (e.g., max speed while staying on terrain).

  • CVPR
    Envisioning the Future One Step at a Time — Autoregressive Video World Models — causal autoregressive video prediction as a general-purpose planning substrate

    Diffusion and VAE world models are flexible but expensive; autoregressive models are fast and deterministic. A causal autoregressive video prediction model generates future frames one-by-one, each step conditioned on all previous frames, serving as a substrate for planning without sampling uncertainty.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: At each timestep t, the model predicts frame x_t given the history x_1, ..., x_{t−1}: x̂_t = decoder(f_θ(encoder(x_1), ..., encoder(x_{t−1}))). The encoder is a CNN or Vision Transformer that extracts features from the history. The decoder is an autoregressive transformer that generates x̂_t token-by-token (after quantization to discrete tokens using VQ-VAE or similar). Loss: cross-entropy between predicted and true tokens. At test time, given an initial sequence x_1, ..., x_k, iteratively generate x̂_{k+1}, then x̂_{k+2} conditioned on x_{k+1}, and so on. For planning: given a reward model r(x_t, a_t), use beam search or Monte Carlo tree search to find the action sequence that maximizes cumulative reward. Because the model is deterministic, there is no randomness; the predicted trajectory is the single most-likely future. This determinism is useful for planning: the model commits to one trajectory, not sampling multiple futures.

Part IV

Where it stands

Video and temporal understanding has made large gains — and is still far from done. What is genuinely still hard:

Precision vs efficiency. Accurate temporal models are expensive — 3D attention, dense optical flow — while streaming models that fit in real-time sacrifice context; no clean solution exists yet.
Long-range scalability & quadratic attention. Attention over a full lecture or procedural video is prohibitive; memory and compression schemes lose information, and what to keep is question-dependent.
Temporal memory & compression. Which frames and moments to store — and how to compress them without losing the event a future question will need — is still an open design problem.
Correlation vs causality. A model that memorizes ‘rain precedes umbrellas’ is not learning causality; videos are full of temporal correlations that are not causal, and disentangling the two is unsolved.
Occlusion & re-identification. Long occlusions defeat appearance-based re-ID; gait and motion as identity signals are promising but not yet reliable across view angle and clothing change.
Annotation burden. Temporal grounding, action detection, and procedure understanding all need fine-grained labels that take minutes per video to produce; scaling annotation is the practical wall.
