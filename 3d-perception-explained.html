01 · the problem

A camera throws depth away

The instant a camera takes a picture, it destroys information. A 3D scene is projected onto a flat sensor, and everything along a single viewing ray — near or far — maps to exactly the same pixel. The distance is simply gone.

That’s why the same flat image could have come from infinitely many different 3D scenes: a shadow or a crease, a small nearby object or a large distant one. 3D perception is the inverse problem of undoing that projection — putting the third dimension back.

the actual math — one level deeper

Projection π: R³ → R² is many-to-one: a pixel p corresponds to a whole ray { X : π(X)=p }.

Recovering the depth Z along each ray is the inverse problem — underdetermined from one image, which is why extra views, motion, or learned priors are needed.

02 · two eyes

Stereo: the shift is the depth

The clean, geometric answer is to use two eyes. Put two cameras a known distance apart (the baseline) and the same scene point projects to a slightly different spot in each image. That difference in position is the disparity.

Disparity is inversely proportional to depth — near things shift a lot between the two views, far things barely move — so depth = focal × baseline / disparity. The hard part is matching: finding which pixel in the right image corresponds to each pixel in the left, which is what learned cost-volume networks do well.

the actual math — one level deeper

With rectified cameras: Z = f B / d, where f = focal length, B = baseline, d = disparity = x_L − x_R.

Matching builds a cost volume over candidate disparities per pixel; the network (or WTA + refinement) picks the best, then sub-pixel refines.

03 · one eye

Monocular: guess depth from priors

Often you only have one image, and one image can’t triangulate anything. Instead a network learns to guess depth from the same cues people use: lines that converge with perspective, the familiar size of known objects, how texture gets finer with distance.

Modern monocular depth foundation models do this astonishingly well. But there’s a catch geometry can’t remove: scale is ambiguous. Nothing in a single picture says whether you’re looking at a toy car up close or a real car far away, so metric depth needs an anchor — a second view, an IMU, or a known size.

the actual math — one level deeper

Learn fθ: image → depth from data (supervised, or self-supervised via reprojection between video frames).

The prediction is only defined up to an unknown scale s (and sometimes shift): d̂ = s·d + t. Metric depth needs an anchor — stereo, IMU scale, or a known object size.

04 · motion

Optical flow: where every pixel went

Add time and you get another handle on 3D. Optical flow is the per-pixel motion between two consecutive frames: for every pixel, where did it go? The result is a dense field of little vectors.

Static scenery flows gently as the camera moves (and how gently encodes its depth); a moving object flows against that background. Flow is computed by matching pixels across frames under brightness-constancy, and it underpins motion estimation, tracking, and structure-from-motion — time doing the job the second camera did in stereo.

the actual math — one level deeper

Find (u,v) per pixel so Iₜ(x) ≈ Iₜ₊₁(x + (u,v)) — brightness constancy — regularized by smoothness.

Modern methods (RAFT-style) build an all-pairs correlation volume and iteratively refine the flow field; scene flow lifts this to 3D motion.

05 · map it

SLAM: map and localize at once

Put a camera on a moving robot and you face a chicken-and-egg problem: to build a map you need to know where you are, but to know where you are you need a map. SLAM — simultaneous localization and mapping — solves both together, refining the map and the trajectory with every new frame.

The enemy is drift: tiny per-frame errors accumulate until the map bends away from reality. The fix is loop closure — recognizing a place you’ve visited before adds a constraint that redistributes the accumulated error and snaps the whole map back into a consistent whole.

the actual math — one level deeper

Jointly estimate camera poses {Tᵢ} and map points {Xᵡ} minimizing reprojection error Σ ‖ π(Tᵢ, Xᵡ) − observation ‖² (bundle adjustment).

Drift grows along the trajectory; a loop-closure constraint (this frame = an earlier one) is added to the pose graph and the error is redistributed globally.

01

Stereo & multi-view depth

both
the problem

Two cameras see the same world point at different x-positions on their sensors. That difference — the disparity — encodes depth exactly: Z = f·B / d, where f is focal length, B is the camera separation, and d is the pixel shift. The problem is matching: for every pixel in the left image, find its counterpart in the right. Wrong matches give wrong depth, and the matching is hard at edges, textureless patches, reflections, and wherever one camera is occluded.

the approaches

The geometric backbone: two (or many) calibrated views triangulate depth from disparity. The engineering is all in matching — building and refining a cost volume over candidate disparities, fast and under occlusion.

Recent work accelerates the iterative solvers and pushes stereo into events, fisheye, and adverse weather.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: rectified stereo pair, 1242×375 px (KITTI driving, f=721 px, B=0.54 m).
  2. Build cost volume: for each pixel, compare left patch against 192 candidate right-image shifts; a 4D tensor of match scores (H × W × D × features).
  3. Aggregate: 3D convolutions smooth the cost over spatial neighbourhood and disparity — reduces noise at low-texture regions.
  4. Disparity regression: softmax over the D axis gives sub-pixel disparity d = 38.2 px at a car 10 m away.
  5. Depth: Z = 721 × 0.54 / 38.2 = 10.2 m — within 2% of LiDAR ground truth.
  6. Speed: LightStereo on an RTX 3090 runs at 45 ms per frame (22 Hz) — fast enough for a moving vehicle.
  • ROBO
    Iterative Volume Fusion for Asymmetric Stereo Matching — two-phase network fuses correlation volumes for high-accuracy disparity

    Stereo matching builds dense depth by finding pixel correspondences between left and right images, but fails at occlusions, edges, and textureless regions where ambiguity is high. This paper applies a two-phase fusion strategy: early phases generate coarse correlation volumes (the 3D space of plausible matches), then refine them by learning to fuse volumetric evidence across iterations. The payoff is high-accuracy disparity at boundaries and weak-texture patches where single-pass matchers falter.

    how it actually works

    The network builds a 4D correlation volume for each image pair (two channels, one for each direction), then:

    1. Sample the correlation volume at candidate disparity hypotheses
    2. Pass through a 3D convolution block to aggregate spatial context
    3. Fuse the two asymmetric volumes by learning weighted combinations that resolve ambiguity at edges
    4. Refine the disparity estimate iteratively, re-sampling the volume with tighter bounds each step

    Asymmetric fusion (treating left→right and right→left differently) matters because occlusions break symmetry—pixels visible in one image may be hidden in the other. Multi-pass refinement lets hard regions (textureless, boundaries) pool evidence over iterations instead of committing to a single noisy match.

  • CVPR
    Pip-Stereo — prunes hypotheses across iterations to speed up iterative stereo matching

    Iterative stereo refinement explores a huge search space of disparity hypotheses, and computational cost explodes with each iteration. Pip-Stereo addresses this by pruning unlikely hypotheses early—evaluating only the most promising candidates at each refinement pass. This keeps the compute graph small while maintaining accuracy by reserving full refinement effort for high-probability matches.

    how it actually works

    Each iteration scores all candidate disparities using the correlation volume and a learnable discriminator:

    1. Compute correlation scores for all hypotheses at the current scale
    2. Prune the bottom-K lowest-scoring hypotheses (e.g., keep top 50% of candidates)
    3. Upsample or re-sample the remaining candidates at finer resolution
    4. Repeat for N iterations, each time reducing the hypothesis set

    Pruning works because early strong candidates rarely drop out of the top-K across iterations—wrong matches accumulate low scores and get eliminated. By dropping candidates that correlate poorly early on, the network avoids computing expensive 3D convolutions on dead ends, cutting total FLOPs significantly while the remaining paths refine to sub-pixel accuracy.

  • CVPR
    EventHub — a data factory for generalizable event-based stereo, no active-depth supervision

    Event cameras output asynchronous brightness changes, not frames—they see motion and high-contrast edges clearly but silence in flat regions. Existing event-stereo datasets require expensive active depth ground truth (LiDAR sweeps). EventHub flips this: it's a synthetic data factory that generates event stereo pairs from standard video with only monocular depth, no active sensor cost. This scales stereo matching training to event-based settings without the annotation burden.

    how it actually works

    The factory pipeline takes standard RGB video + monocular depth estimates:

    1. Render left and right camera views from the depth and camera trajectories
    2. Simulate event generation by computing per-pixel brightness change between consecutive frames and thresholding at the event polarity threshold (±ΔI)
    3. Generate stereo event pairs by rendering the same scene from both cameras and simulating events independently
    4. Package as training data with ground-truth disparity from the lifted depth

    The key insight: events encode disparity information through the latency and density of polarity transitions—a depth discontinuity creates a concentrated band of events along the edge. By generating synthetic events with known ground truth, the paper sidesteps the need for active-depth sensors, making event-stereo training practical at scale.

  • CVPR
    SpiderCam — snapshot depth from differential defocus via a beam splitter, low power

    Depth-from-defocus extracts scene depth by analyzing how out-of-focus blur varies across the image—near objects blur more than far objects. Classical approaches require multiple exposures or lens parameter tuning. SpiderCam gets snapshot metric depth via a beam-splitter optic that fuses two focal planes simultaneously, each seeing a different defocus amount. A trained net maps the two blurred images directly to metric depth with minimal power overhead.

    how it actually works

    The optics capture two defocused views in one exposure using a half-silvered mirror:

    1. Incoming light reflects to one focal plane and transmits to a second, creating two defocus channels on the sensor
    2. The network learns a mapping from defocus-pair descriptors to metric depth by training on scenes where ground truth (LiDAR or stereo) pins down the scale
    3. Low-resolution depth is computed per image; upsampling refines boundaries if needed

    Depth-from-defocus is geometry-agnostic: the blur amount depends only on depth and optics, not scene texture or illumination. Two simultaneous focal planes give the network independent cues to triangulate depth without solving a correspondence problem (unlike stereo), and the beam splitter adds minimal mass or power compared to multi-camera arrays.

  • ROBO
    A New Stereo Fisheye Event Camera — back-to-back fisheye event cameras give full spherical stereo

    Most depth sensors cover a narrow field of view; panoramic depth requires multiple cameras or mechanical scanning. This paper mounts back-to-back fisheye event cameras to capture full 360° stereo in one snapshot. The extreme wide baseline and overlapping hemispherical views allow stereo matching across the full sphere, giving omnidirectional metric depth from a compact package.

    how it actually works

    The dual-fisheye stereo rig captures asynchronous events over a full hemisphere each:

    1. Events from the front and back cameras define a 360° epipolar geometry (the curve on one hemisphere corresponding to a point on the other)
    2. Match events across the two hemispheres along the epipolar surface, handling the nonlinear fisheye projection via locally affine approximation
    3. Lift matched event timings to disparity using the fisheye camera model and back-to-back baseline
    4. Accumulate sparse depth as events arrive asynchronously

    Event-based matching is reliable at large baselines because events encode edges and motion, which persist across wide viewpoints. Fisheye optics avoid singularities at the pole (unlike spherical stereo with equirectangular images), and the asynchronous arrival of events lets the system handle the dense epipolar structure without a discrete grid scan.

02

Monocular & metric depth

both
the problem

A single photograph is geometrically ambiguous: the same image is consistent with a tiny nearby scene and a large far-away one — there is no triangulation to break the tie. A network must guess depth from indirect cues: convergence of parallel edges (perspective), the relative size of known objects, how texture becomes finer with distance. The network's output is relative depth — it knows what is in front of what, but not by how many metres. Pinning down metric scale requires an anchor from outside the image.

the approaches

One image, no triangulation — a network guesses depth from perspective, size, and texture cues, with foundation models now generalizing across scenes. The stubborn issue is turning that relative depth into metric distance.

Approaches inject real-world priors, edges, or events to pin down scale and detail.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: one 640×480 RGB frame of a room — no second camera, no IMU, no depth sensor.
  2. Encoder: a ViT-L backbone extracts patch tokens; the model (Depth Anything V2) was trained on 62 M images.
  3. Decoder: dense prediction head outputs a 640×480 relative-depth map, values 0–1.
  4. Scale anchor: a single LiDAR point at z=2.4 m lands on pixel (320,280); the predicted value there is 0.48. Scale = 2.4 / 0.48 = 5.0 m per unit.
  5. Metric map: multiply every pixel by 5.0 — RMS depth error on the remaining 200 check points is 0.11 m (4.6%).
  6. Without anchor: the network still produces a geometrically correct relative map in 8 ms on a laptop GPU — useful for navigation even without metric scale.
  • CVPR
    Iris: Real-World Priors into Diffusion for Monocular Depth — progressive generative distillation grounds depth in metric scale

    Monocular depth is scale-ambiguous—an image could represent a giant far-away chair or a tiny near one. Diffusion models trained on in-the-wild internet data generate plausible depth maps, but they predict relative depth only; pinning down absolute metric scale requires hints. Iris uses progressive generative distillation to inject real-world geometric priors (camera intrinsics, relative scale anchors from sparse COLMAP reconstruction) into the diffusion process, yielding metric-scale depth.

    how it actually works

    The pipeline distills a teacher metric-depth model into a student diffusion model:

    1. Train a teacher CNN on metric-depth benchmarks (NYU, ScanNet) to ground absolute scale
    2. Run diffusion reverse steps on many images, guidance-steering the noisy prediction toward the teacher at each step (progressive distillation)
    3. The student diffusion model learns to output metric depth by mimicking the teacher's scale commitment, not just the teacher's weight checkpoints
    4. At inference, the student diffuses without the teacher, but has internalized metric-scale reasoning

    Distillation works because the teacher encodes metric priors in its gradient flow—each reverse step of diffusion is a gradient descent towards the teacher's prediction, so the student absorbs metric reasoning into the drift term. This avoids expensive ground-truth depth collection on every new image; the teacher's bias propagates via the denoising trajectory.

  • CVPR
    InfiniDepth — neural implicit fields give arbitrary-resolution fine-grained depth

    Depth maps from neural networks are discrete grids—they have a fixed resolution, and upsampling loses high-frequency detail. InfiniDepth treats depth as a continuous implicit function parameterized by a neural network that maps (x, y) coordinates to depth values. This yields arbitrary-resolution depth with fine-grained detail that standard upsampling cannot recover.

    how it actually works

    The network encodes depth as an implicit function:

    1. Input a monocular image and encode it via a CNN backbone into a feature map
    2. Decode any query coordinate (x, y) by interpolating the feature map and passing through an MLP that outputs depth
    3. The MLP acts as a learned upsampling kernel—it can represent high frequencies by composing simple nonlinearities
    4. Train on standard depth benchmarks; at test time, evaluate the function at any resolution

    Implicit fields sidestep discrete quantization—the MLP can represent smooth depth variations at sub-pixel precision. Because the function is continuous and differentiable, boundaries and fine structures (depth discontinuities at edges) can be preserved in the network weights, then queried at any resolution without interpolation artifacts.

  • CVPR
    MD2E — depth-to-edge cues supervise metric depth without ground-truth depth

    Metric monocular depth training usually requires expensive ground-truth depth (LiDAR scans). MD2E removes this dependency by using edges as a free supervision signal—depth discontinuities manifest as sharp changes in the predicted disparity map, which align with image edges (brightness boundaries, shading changes). This trains metric depth using only edge labels, not depth ground truth.

    how it actually works

    The approach treats edge prediction as an auxiliary task:

    1. Train a monocular depth network to output metric disparity (inverse depth)
    2. Compute image gradients (Sobel, Canny) on the disparity map, then enforce they align with edges detected in the RGB image
    3. Use edge-alignment loss: penalize disparity gradients that do not correspond to image edges (false edges) and miss image edges (missed boundaries)
    4. The depth network learns to place discontinuities exactly where they should be, which implicitly constrains absolute scale

    Scale and edge-structure are linked: if depth estimates are too large or small everywhere, disparity gradients will not align with real edges. By optimizing alignment, the network commits to a consistent metric scale without ever seeing depth ground truth. This trades depth labels for cheaper edge annotations (or synthetic edges), making metric depth learning more scalable.

  • CVPR
    AIMDepth — asymmetric image-event Mamba fuses events + RGB for extreme lighting

    Standard cameras and LiDAR both fail in extreme lighting: a bright sunny day or deep underwater, the camera saturates or loses information. Event cameras (neuromorphic sensors) output asynchronous brightness-change spikes with microsecond timing, with no saturation and 140 dB dynamic range. AIMDepth (Asymmetric Image-event Mamba) fuses RGB and event streams using a Mamba state-space model to predict depth that works in conditions where each modality alone would fail.

    AIMDepth processes images and events through separate Mamba (state-space) encoders that maintain long-range temporal context, then fuses their representations. Events see fast motion and preserve detail in shadows; images see static structure and color. A fusion head predicts depth from the merged feature maps. The payoff is reliable monocular depth under extreme lighting—underwater ruins, backlit scenes, or high-speed motion.

    how it actually works

    The problem: RGB depth fails in extreme dynamic range (too bright or too dark); event cameras excel at fast motion and dynamic range but have no color information, making single-event depth impossible.

    The mechanism: (1) Encode RGB via ViT with standard patch embedding. (2) Encode events: bin events into temporal windows (e.g., 10 ms windows), compute event density and polarity per pixel for each window, creating a (H, W, T) event tensor. Pass through a Mamba layer: Mamba processes each spatial location as a sequence over time, computing hidden state via a learned state-transition matrix A, which enables long-range temporal dependencies without quadratic attention cost. (3) Fuse RGB and event representations: concatenate features or cross-attend. (4) Decode to depth: MLP head outputs per-pixel depth. (5) Supervise with both image-based depth (where available) and photometric consistency across events (brightness change should match depth motion).

    Why it works: Events have no saturation—they fire whenever brightness crosses a threshold, so they preserve structure in bright and dark regions. Mamba state-space models are efficient (linear in sequence length) and naturally handle variable temporal density (events are asynchronous, not on a fixed grid). Fusing RGB and events gives complementary strengths: RGB provides color and structure cues, events provide motion and dynamic range. The dual supervision (depth loss + event photometric loss) lets the model learn how to weight each modality by its confidence in each pixel region.

  • ROBO
    Single-Shot Metric Depth from Focused Plenoptic Cameras — a net makes a sparse metric point cloud from one plenoptic image

    Plenoptic cameras capture the light field—color and direction of rays hitting the sensor—via a microlens array above the pixels. Refocusing to different depths is possible in software, but existing methods output dense depth maps that blur or break at occlusions. This paper trains a network to extract sparse metric point clouds directly from a single plenoptic image, sidestepping the dense depth bottleneck.

    how it actually works

    The pipeline extracts 3D point positions from light-field data:

    1. Decode the plenoptic image into a 4D light-field tensor (2D spatial, 2D angular dimensions under the microlens array)
    2. For each micro-image (lenslet), compute depth by correlating rays across neighboring lenslets (light-field stereo at microlens scale)
    3. Classify sparse pixels as foreground (high depth confidence) and suppress occlusion boundaries via a CNN trained on synthetic light fields
    4. Output only high-confidence depths as a sparse point cloud, skipping uncertain pixels

    Sparsity is a feature: plenoptic sensors have inherent noise and angular resolution limits that make dense depth noisy. By selecting only high-confidence points via the network's occlusion classifier, the method trades density for accuracy. One shot (one image capture) is enough because light-field geometry encodes depth information in ray angles, eliminating the need for multiple exposures or camera motion.

03

Optical & scene flow

both
the problem

Between two consecutive video frames, every pixel moves to a new position. The task is to find that displacement for every one of the millions of pixels — a dense field of 2D vectors. The difficulty is the aperture problem: looking through a small window you can only detect motion perpendicular to an edge, not along it. A network must integrate evidence across the whole image to resolve the true 2D direction. Scene flow lifts this to 3D displacements — how each point actually moved in space, not just where it appeared to go on screen.

the approaches

Match every pixel across frames to get a dense 2D motion field (optical flow), or lift it to 3D displacement (scene flow) — the raw material for ego-motion, tracking, and dynamic structure.

The work is efficiency (all-pairs correlation is heavy) and robustness at motion boundaries and in events.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: two 720p frames 33 ms apart from a street-scene video — 1280×720 px, ~920 K pixel pairs.
  2. Feature extraction: shared encoder produces 1/8-resolution feature maps for both frames.
  3. Correlation volume: dot every feature against every shifted feature within ±4 pixels — yields a 9×9×H/8×W/8 tensor of match scores.
  4. GRU update: RAFT's recurrent unit refines the flow field for 12 iterations, each reading the correlation volume at the current estimate.
  5. Result: a parked car → near-zero vectors; a cyclist moving at 5 m/s appears → 11-pixel rightward shift at this frame rate.
  6. Speed: RAFT on a 2080 Ti: 160 ms per pair; NeuFlow-V2 achieves 30 Hz on the same hardware using a flat correlation design.
  • CVPR
    Efficient All-Pairs Correlation Volume Sampling — adaptive correlation sampling cuts optical-flow compute

    Optical flow matching compares every pixel in frame 1 to a region of frame 2 to find motion, building a 4D correlation volume (height × width × offset_y × offset_x). This brute-force approach is memory-prohibitive at high resolution. This paper proposes adaptive sampling—evaluating only promising offset regions at each spatial location, predicted by a gating network—to cut memory and compute while retaining accuracy.

    how it actually works

    The sampling strategy replaces dense correlation with learned sparsity:

    1. Encode frame 1 and frame 2 into feature maps via a CNN
    2. Train a lightweight gating network to predict which offsets are worth computing per pixel (e.g., predict a 8×8 mask out of 32×32 possible offsets)
    3. Compute correlation only at the selected offsets, then aggregate via 3D convolution
    4. Refine flow in a recurrent module, re-gating at finer scales if needed

    The gating network learns motion priors—pixels in the middle of the image typically have small motion, whereas edge pixels might shift more. By predicting which offsets to sample, it avoids computing dense correlation everywhere. This is adaptive: the gating can be image-dependent, sampling more offsets for dynamic scenes than static ones, so the method scales to high resolution without a fixed memory ceiling.

  • ROBO
    SSF: Sparse Long-Range Scene Flow — sparse convolutions extend scene flow to long range for driving

    Scene flow (3D motion of points across frames) is hardest to estimate over long ranges and for dynamic objects in driving scenarios—the search space explodes. SSF uses sparse convolutions (efficient 3D kernels that skip empty space) to extend scene flow estimation to long-range point clouds without prohibitive compute. This enables driving applications where ego-motion and object motion must be disentangled over large spaces.

    how it actually works

    The network processes 3D LiDAR scans across two frames:

    1. Downsample consecutive point clouds to remove duplicates and reduce density
    2. Build a sparse 3D grid and compute convolutions only on occupied voxels, skipping empty regions via sparse tensor operations
    3. Estimate per-point scene flow (velocity) by comparing spatial neighborhoods across frames in the sparse feature space
    4. Refine flow via iterative updates in the sparse grid

    Sparse convolutions exploit the fact that LiDAR scans are inherently sparse—most of 3D space is empty. Traditional 3D CNNs waste compute on zero-padding empty voxels. By skipping empty space during convolution, the method achieves long-range context (e.g., 100 m for highway driving) with compute proportional to point count, not volume. This makes scene flow tractable for driving datasets where ranges are large.

  • CVPR
    ARES — asymmetric RGB-event fusion for probabilistic scene flow

    Scene flow (3D motion estimation) requires matching pixels across frames and solving for 3D displacement—a hard problem when there is occlusion or motion blur. RGB frames have appearance information but lose dynamic range and motion blur. Event cameras see every brightness change with microsecond precision and no motion blur. ARES (Asymmetric RGB-Event Scene Flow) fuses both: events see motion directly, RGB disambiguates which object moved.

    ARES encodes RGB frames and event streams, matches events to RGB via a cross-modal correlation volume (comparing event spike patterns to image gradients), and predicts both 2D optical flow and 3D scene flow. The asymmetry: events predict motion directly, RGB disambiguates foreground/background and provides appearance for feature matching. The payoff is reliable scene flow at high speed—the event stream is the motion ground truth, RGB resolves ambiguity.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Scene flow from RGB fails when there is motion blur (the image is smeared across multiple frames); ICP-style matching does not work. Events see motion with no blur, but lack appearance information for correspondence.

    The mechanism: (1) Encode RGB pair (t, t+1) and event stream between them via CNN: extract features f_rgb_t, f_rgb_t+1, f_events. (2) Build a cross-modal correlation volume: for each (x, y) in the RGB image, compute similarity between the local event pattern and the local RGB gradient over a search window. (3) A refinement network outputs optical flow (2D) and scene flow (3D, using depth from RGB monocular prediction or stereo). (4) Motion from events: event polarity changes correlate with motion direction—a +polarity event at (x, y) at time t indicates motion in that pixel's temporal direction. Aggregate to get initial flow estimate. (5) Fuse: the RGB-event correlation volume and the event-derived flow estimate are refined jointly. (6) Supervise: 2D optical flow supervision on flow, 3D reprojection loss on scene flow.

    Why it works: Events are the raw motion signal: they fire because brightness changed, and the timing tells you exactly when the change occurred—no temporal aliasing or blur. RGB provides the appearance context: which object each event belongs to, foreground vs. background, which motion is occlusion. Cross-modal matching (events to image gradients) handles modality mismatch. The dual representation draws on each modality's strength while avoiding its weakness.

  • ROBO
    E-NMSTFlow — spatio-temporal motion features for unsupervised event-based optical flow

    Event cameras output asynchronous motion at high temporal resolution, but learning optical flow from events is hard—events are sparse and the motion-boundary problem (aperture) is baked in. E-NMSTFlow addresses this by building spatio-temporal motion features from event polarity and timing that encode local motion direction, then matching these features across time to estimate flow. This trades dense pixel space for sparse high-temporal-resolution event features.

    how it actually works

    The approach builds event flow unsupervised via feature matching:

    1. Accumulate events into very short time windows (microseconds to milliseconds) to preserve temporal resolution
    2. Compute per-pixel motion features by analyzing polarity transitions over time and space—high gradients in space/time encode strong motion
    3. Match motion features across small time steps via nearest-neighbor or learned matching, outputting flow at event locations
    4. Aggregate sparse flow predictions to a regular grid for visualization or downstream tasks

    Event features are natural for flow: a moving edge creates a diagonal trail of events in space-time (the optical-flow constraint equation). the problem

A robot building a map accumulates a small error with every frame. After moving 100 m those errors add up — the map bends. The core loop: build a map while localizing in it, in real time, on the robot. Learned depth/flow/Gaussian priors increasingly replace hand-built feature matching for denser, more robust maps.

  4. Loop detection: DBoW2 descriptor matches frame 1 and frame 847 — recognises the entrance.
  5. Pose-graph correction: a new edge is added between frames 1 and 847; g2o redistributes the 1.4 m error uniformly — every node shifts ≤ 1.8 mm.

the problem

A standard camera reads all pixels at once at, say, 30 Hz — and in the 33 ms between frames a fast-moving robot arm smears across dozens of pixels. High altitude and high speed, which cripple RGB cameras, do not affect events (they have no rolling shutter and no temporal aliasing).

Point-cloud registration & ICP

robotics
the problem

Given two point clouds of the same object or scene — taken from different positions — find the rotation and translation that best aligns them. If the correspondences were known, this is a simple least-squares problem. But they are not known: you must solve for correspondences and the transform at the same time. The classic ICP algorithm alternates between finding nearest neighbours (correspondences) and minimising the sum of squared distances (transform). It converges but requires a good starting guess and breaks when many points are outliers.

the approaches

Align two 3D point clouds by finding the rigid transform between them — the workhorse of LiDAR mapping, relocalization, and object pose — made robust to outliers and partial overlap.

Robust estimators (IRLS, graduated non-convexity, Lie-algebra formulations) are the recurring tools.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: two partial scans of a mechanical part, 12 K points each, overlap ~60%, 30° rotation apart.
  2. Initialisation: FPFH descriptors give a coarse alignment in 0.3 s (rotation error 8°).
  3. ICP iteration 1: nearest-neighbour pairs found; SVD gives refined transform — rotation error drops to 2.1°.
  4. IRLS weighting (SANDRO): 18% of pairs involve occlusion boundaries; Geman-McClure weights suppress them — iteration 5 rotation error: 0.08°.
  5. Convergence: after 12 iterations, RMS point distance = 0.31 mm — within the scanner's measurement noise.
  6. Time: 140 ms on a laptop CPU — fast enough for a robot grasping pipeline that re-registers every 200 ms.
  • ROBO
    SANDRO — IRLS + graduated non-convexity for registration under heavy outliers

    Point clouds from successive LiDAR scans contain outliers — dynamic objects move between frames, occlusion boundaries don't match exactly, and the registration algorithm must tolerate misalignment gracefully. Standard ICP assigns all point pairs equal weight, so a handful of bad matches (a car driving through) can pull the entire pose estimate off. SANDRO uses iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) with graduated non-convexity to down-weight outliers, letting inliers dominate the transformation.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: For each candidate transform, find nearest-neighbour pairs between the two clouds. Rather than treating all pairs equally, SANDRO applies a reliable kernel (Geman-McClure or similar) that outputs weights close to 1 for small residuals and drops to near-zero for large ones. Re-weight the pairs, re-solve for the transform using SVD, and iterate. Graduated non-convexity gradually tightens the kernel's threshold, forcing weak hypotheses out and strong ones to converge.

    Why it works: Outliers produce large residuals; the reliable kernel automatically suppresses them without needing an explicit inlier/outlier classification. The graduated schedule prevents local minima from trapping the algorithm in the wrong basin.

  • ROBO
    Fast(er) Robust Point Cloud Alignment Using Lie Algebra — IRLS on the Lie algebra for efficient robust alignment

    ICP solves 3D rigid alignment by finding nearest neighbours and minimizing point-to-point distance with SVD. This is well-understood but slow on large clouds — the nearest-neighbour search is O(n²) and SVD is expensive at every iteration. Recent work parametrizes the transform on the Lie algebra (the 6-dimensional tangent space of rotations and translations) and applies IRLS directly there, avoiding the need to exponentiate back to SO(3)×ℝ³ at each step. This trades a little generality for significant speedup and numerical stability.

    how it actually works

    The key operations: Start with an approximate alignment (from FPFH or initial guess). At each iteration, compute nearest-neighbour residuals in 3D. Instead of solving the full rigid-body optimization, work in the 6-D Lie algebra and apply first-order updates: compute the Jacobian of per-point distances with respect to the 6 pose parameters, then use weighted least squares directly on those 6 unknowns. The reliable weights (via IRLS, same as SANDRO) suppress outlier gradients.

    Why it works: Lie-algebra formulations avoid the numerical instabilities that arise when forcing SVD outputs back onto SO(3). The linear approximation in the tangent space is accurate when residuals are small, and iteration still converges to the globally optimal transform.

  • ROBO
    Match Policy — recasts action inference itself as point-cloud registration

    Robotic manipulation requires understanding object pose to grasp successfully. Pose estimation is often framed as a separate perception problem: find keypoints, match them to a CAD model, solve PnP. But at its core, pose inference is a geometric alignment problem — matching the observed scene to a candidate pose hypothesis. Some recent work reframes the entire action-inference problem (what gripper action succeeds given the current state?) as solving a point-cloud registration subproblem, using the geometry engine rather than separate classification or regression heads.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Instead of predicting a pose directly, encode both the scene and each candidate object pose as point clouds in a canonical frame. Score each hypothesis by solving how well its point cloud aligns to the observed one — essentially an ICP-based match score. The pose with the lowest registration error is the best match.

    Why it works: Point-cloud registration is a well-solved geometric problem with reliable solvers; reusing that engine for action inference inherits its reliability to noise and partial overlap. It unifies perception and action planning under one geometric measure.

  • ROBO
    Effective Heterogeneous Point-Cloud Place Recognition — BEV density images register aerial-to-ground clouds

    Robots navigate on the ground and need to recognize previously visited places from new viewpoints and sensors. A natural idea is to compare LiDAR scans directly — but a scan from the side looks very different from one from above or behind. Classical descriptors (FPFH, spin images) are viewpoint-sensitive. Converting the point clouds to bird's-eye view (BEV) density images — literally rendering the height-and-intensity data onto a 2D grid — makes the representation view-invariant and allows learning or matching across modalities (aerial to ground, or ground to ground).

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Take point clouds from a query frame and a database frame. Render each independently to a BEV density image at a fixed resolution (e.g., 64×64 px, each cell the max intensity in that 1-m² region). These 2D images are rotation-invariant locally if the render is done in a canonical frame (e.g., robot-frame). Compute a descriptor (e.g., a learned CNN feature or hand-crafted histogram) on each image, then match via nearest-neighbour search or cosine similarity.

    Why it works: BEV rendering makes the representation invariant to lateral pose changes and viewpoint: a tree far away looks the same from multiple angles as a BEV density map. This reliability to viewpoint and modality is why it succeeds on heterogeneous data (aerial + ground, different sensors).

Depth completion & sensor fusion

robotics
the problem

A LiDAR returns about 100 K points per scan — dense compared with nothing, but sparse compared with the 2 million pixels in a camera frame. Most pixels have no depth measurement. Depth completion asks: given those sparse laser hits and the full colour image, fill in a plausible depth value for every pixel. The difficulty is that edges in colour images do not always correspond to depth discontinuities, transparent surfaces reflect the laser beam sideways, and the LiDAR's own mounting angle creates blind spots just where objects sit on the ground.

the approaches

Turn sparse depth (a few LiDAR returns) plus an RGB image into a dense depth map — and fuse camera with LiDAR/radar/ultrasound so each sensor covers the others’ blind spots.

Transparent and specular surfaces, which defeat single sensors, are a recurring target.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: KITTI frame — 64-beam Velodyne gives 22 K valid depth pixels out of 1.2 M total (1.8% fill).
  2. Encode both modalities: ResNet-34 encodes the RGB; a sparse conv net encodes the LiDAR — two feature maps at 1/8 resolution.
  3. Deformable attention fusion: DA-Fusion aligns features by learning per-pixel offsets — handles the ~3-pixel camera-LiDAR mis-registration.
  4. Dense depth head: decoder upsamples to full resolution — outputs 1.2 M depth values.
  5. Transparent glass door: LiDAR returned 0 hits; the RGB encoder saw the frame outline; decoder fills in 2.1 m — within 4 cm of manual measurement.
  6. RMSE: 0.74 m on KITTI depth-completion benchmark, vs 1.6 m for bilinear LiDAR interpolation alone.
  • ROBO
    DA-Fusion — deformable-attention transformer fuses RGB + depth for unseen-object segmentation

    LiDAR and RGB cameras have complementary blind spots: LiDAR misses transparent glass and shiny surfaces, while RGB struggles in darkness and rain. A natural idea is sensor fusion — feed both signals into a network to fill each other's gaps. The hard part is alignment: the camera and LiDAR are mounted at different positions and angles, so a feature in the RGB image doesn't align perfectly with the LiDAR grid. DA-Fusion learns per-pixel spatial offsets (deformable attention) to warp features into alignment during fusion.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encode the RGB frame with a ResNet backbone; encode the sparse LiDAR returns with a sparse convolution network. Both outputs are H/8 × W/8 feature maps. A deformable transformer attention layer learns a 2D offset grid for each spatial location, allowing the RGB features to align to the LiDAR features despite the 3–5 pixel mount misalignment. The aligned features are fused (concatenation or learned weighted sum), then upsampled to full resolution to output 1.2 M dense depth predictions.

    Why it works: Deformable offsets are learnable and differentiable, so the network can discover the camera-LiDAR transform without hand-calibration. Transparent surfaces reflect or transmit differently than opaque ones, so the RGB+LiDAR combination contains enough redundancy to fill both modalities' blind spots.

  • ROBO
    TransDiff — diffusion conditioned on RGB completes depth for transparent objects

    Transparent materials (glass, water, plastic) break depth sensors: LiDAR light passes through, standard optical flow fails on reflections, and monocular networks never saw enough labelled transparent examples. Diffusion models trained on massive data have learned what transparency looks like (refraction patterns, distortion, reflection color). TransDiff uses a diffusion prior conditioned on RGB and sparse depth to inpaint plausible depths on transparent surfaces.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Input: RGB image, sparse depth (LiDAR or stereo at scattered pixels). Initialize a dense depth map by padding the sparse measurements (nearest-neighbour or interpolation). Run a diffusion prior that iteratively refines the depth map over T denoising steps, conditioned on the RGB image. Known depth pixels (LiDAR hits) are clamped to their values and not updated; unknown pixels are refined by the diffusion model, which learns to predict plausible surfaces that match the RGB appearance.

    Why it works: Diffusion models learn visual signatures of transparency (gradual focus blur, refraction ripples) from data. By conditioning on RGB, the model knows where transparency is likely (clear regions that are coherent across frames). Clamping known depths prevents hallucination while letting the model fill gaps with learned priors.

  • ROBO
    DCT-Diffusion — transformer + diffusion completes depth on transparent objects

    Transparent objects are a hard case for depth completion: they lack reliable features and defeat both LiDAR (light passes through) and monocular regression (geometry is ambiguous). A transformer naturally handles sequential refinement over many tokens, and a diffusion model has learned to imagine plausible 3D structure from incomplete data. DCT combines a transformer backbone with diffusion-based depth completion, iterating to refine the surface estimate on transparent regions.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Encode the RGB and sparse depth inputs with a transformer encoder, producing per-token features. A diffusion decoder then iteratively denoises a learnable depth representation (e.g., depth logits per spatial location) for T steps. At each denoising step, the model reads the corruption level (t) and outputs a denoising direction that moves the current estimate toward a plausible surface. Non-transparent regions (where LiDAR is confident) are clamped to the LiDAR value; transparent regions are refined by the diffusion process.

    Why it works: The transformer's expressive capacity models long-range context (room structure, object scale), while the diffusion loop's iterative refinement lets the model escape poor local minima. Transparent surfaces require holistic reasoning — knowing the room layout to infer that a glossy region is glass, not air — which transformers handle well.

  • ROBO
    Bat-VUFN — bio-inspired camera + ultrasound fusion, input-quality weighted

    Bats use sonar — they emit sound and time its return — to navigate in darkness. Combining a camera and ultrasonic sensors mimics this bio-inspired strategy: the camera sees shape and texture in good light, ultrasound probes blind spots and dark regions. A learned fusion network must weight each modality by its reliability (camera input quality in darkness, ultrasound quality with occlusion). Bat-VUFN adaptively gates the fusion based on local input quality, learned from data.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Extract features from the camera (CNN) and ultrasound (sparse distance measurements) separately. For each spatial region, compute a confidence score for each modality based on local input characteristics (blur, low luminance for camera; reflective absorption for ultrasound). Apply a learned gating network that outputs per-modality weights, summing to 1. Fuse the depth estimates via weighted combination, then upsample and refine.

    Why it works: Each sensor excels in different conditions — camera in daylight, ultrasound in dark and cluttered spaces. By learning to predict when each is reliable, the network avoids trusting a poor estimate and leans on the modality that works in the current context. This adaptive fusion is more reliable than fixed averaging.

6D pose estimation & localization

robotics
the problem

A robot arm about to grasp an object needs to know not just where the object is, but exactly how it is oriented — all 6 degrees of freedom (3 translation, 3 rotation). A bounding box is not enough. The object might be at arm's length but rotated 90° from what the arm expects, making the grasp impossible. Estimating 6-DoF pose from an RGB image means finding dense correspondences between the image and the object's known 3D model, then solving for the rigid transform that brings them into alignment.

the approaches

Pin down the full 6-DoF pose of a camera or an object from RGB(-D) — the bridge from perception to grasping and to localizing in a known scene.

Zero-shot (CAD-model) pose and splat-map localization are the fast-moving edges.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: single 640×480 RGB image of a YCB-V mustard bottle on a table; its CAD model is known.
  2. Zero-shot correspondences (ZeroBP): detect the bottle's 2D keypoints; lift to 3D using the CAD model's known geometry — no object-specific training.
  3. PnP solve: 14 2D-3D correspondences → RANSAC PnP in 3 ms — translation error 4 mm, rotation error 1.8°.
  4. Refine: ICP on the depth channel reduces rotation error to 0.6°.
  5. Grasp check: project the grasp points in the object frame to the image — they land within 3 px of the expected contact zones.
  6. Pick success: pose error < 5° and < 1 cm gives 94% grasp success on this object in real trials.
  • ROBO
    ZeroBP — position-aware correspondence for zero-shot 6D pose from a CAD model

    Robot grasping requires knowing an object's 6-DoF pose in the hand-eye frame. Traditional pipelines require per-object training: learn a CNN to detect the object, estimate its keypoints, then solve PnP. For a robot arm to generalize to new objects, it needs zero-shot pose from CAD models alone. ZeroBP detects 2D keypoints in the image without object-specific training, then lifts them to 3D using the known CAD geometry — solving the 6-DoF problem in one pass.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Given an RGB image and a known CAD model of an object, first detect the object's 2D bounding box and approximate location (using a generic detector or hand-tuning). Then, for each pixel in the detected region, predict whether it belongs to a keypoint class (e.g., corner, edge midpoint) — this is learned on synthetic renderings of diverse objects, so it generalizes. Keypoint detection is position-aware but object-agnostic. For each detected 2D keypoint, look up its 3D location in the CAD model. With 4+ 2D-3D correspondences, solve the PnP problem via RANSAC to recover the 6-DoF pose.

    Why it works: Geometric features (corners, edges) are consistent across objects, so a single detector trained on diverse synthetic data predicts keypoints for any unseen object. The CAD model provides the 3D ground truth for each 2D keypoint, bypassing the need for manual pose labelling.

  • ROBO
    You Only Estimate Once — one-stage real-time 6D pose: labels, centroid offset, rotation

    Traditional 6-DoF pose estimation pipelines involve multiple stages: detect the object, segment it, extract keypoints, solve PnP. Each stage adds latency. Real-time robotic grasping needs faster inference. YOEO is a one-stage detector that directly outputs pose labels (object class), the 2D centroid offset from the bounding-box center, and a rotation encoding — all in one forward pass, with no intermediate keypoint extraction.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Take an RGB image and pass it through a single-stage detector backbone (YOLO-style). Instead of just outputting class and bounding box, add auxiliary regression heads: one predicts the 2D offset of the object's 3D center in the image (sub-pixel precision), another predicts rotation (as a 6-D unit vector or 3×3 matrix parameterization). At inference, extract the per-object predictions and solve a PnP-like problem or directly use the predicted center offset + rotation to get the 6-DoF pose.

    Why it works: One-stage detection avoids multi-step pipelines and their compounding latency. The center-offset and rotation predictions are natural regression targets that the network can learn from synthetic data. Real-time pose is critical for grasping at robot speeds.

  • ROBO
    LoGS — SfM + Gaussian-splat map + retrieval for visual localization with fewer images

    A robot revisiting a scene needs to relocalize — to figure out where it is in a pre-built map. Classical SLAM uses sparse features (ORB, SIFT) matched across frames to compute pose. Modern approaches use dense Gaussian-splat maps (photoreal, highly informative) but can't match splats to new images directly. LoGS combines structure-from-motion (SfM) anchor points, a Gaussian-splat dense map, and image retrieval to relocalize with fewer images than traditional feature matching.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Pre-build a map: run SfM on reference images to get sparse points and camera poses. Upgrade those poses to a dense Gaussian-splat map (high-quality, photorealistic). At query time, retrieve the K nearest reference images (via learned embedding or simple SIFT features). For the query frame, use the sparse SfM points from those K images as 3D-2D correspondences, then solve PnP via RANSAC. Refine by viewing the splat map from the PnP pose and checking photometric alignment.

    Why it works: Gaussian splats are dense and photorealistic, so small pose errors produce obvious misalignment in rendered vs observed pixels — a strong refinement signal. Using sparse SfM points from nearest neighbours reduces the dimensionality of the search space, making RANSAC faster and more reliable than matching all database points.

  • ROBO
    LEADER — reliable local-to-global correspondences for LiDAR relocalization

    LiDAR point clouds are sparse but accurate in 3D. Relocalization (finding the robot's pose in a known LiDAR map) is normally hard because LiDAR returns are identity-less — there's no way to label a point as 'this is the left edge of the doorframe at (2.3, 4.1, 0.9)' without dense matching. Classical approaches use hand-crafted features (e.g., FPFH descriptors). LEADER learns local-to-global correspondences: it predicts which points in the current LiDAR frame correspond to which 3D locations in the map, enabling a direct PnP-style solve.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Input: current LiDAR points (N points, unlabeled) and a known 3D map (M points, georeferenced). Process the current scan with a PointNet-style encoder to extract per-point features. For each point, predict a soft correspondence to the M map points (via learned attention or explicit matching). Weight the matches by confidence. With K confident correspondences, solve the 6-DoF transform using weighted least squares on point pairs, or use a reliable solver (IRLS on Lie algebra, similar to ICP).

    Why it works: Learning correspondences directly avoids the need to compute hand-crafted descriptors and hand-tune descriptor matching thresholds. The network learns which geometric signatures are stable and distinctive, improving matching in ambiguous regions (texture-less walls). Soft correspondence weights let the solver automatically down-weight uncertain matches.

Dynamic-scene & 4D depth

vision
the problem

Static 3D reconstruction assumes the world holds still while you capture it. Put a person, a car, or a waving branch in the scene and that assumption breaks: the same pixel in frame 1 and frame 2 comes from different 3D points because the scene deformed. You need to recover both geometry AND the motion field simultaneously — a 4D reconstruction — from a video where neither is given directly, and where the same video is consistent with infinitely many (shape, motion) pairs.

the approaches

Recover geometry and motion together from video with moving, deforming objects — a 4D reconstruction — where the same frame could arise from different motion+shape combinations.

Feed-forward 4D and dynamic point maps are collapsing this into a single pass.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: 48-frame video of a person raising their arm — monocular, 30 Hz, 720p.
  2. Any4D forward pass: token-based transformer processes all 48 frames at once — one forward pass, no per-frame optimisation.
  3. Outputs: per-frame pointmaps (H×W×3) + per-pixel 3D motion vectors (H×W×3) + camera poses — all in one 2.1 s run.
  4. Arm trajectory: the wrist travels 0.62 m in 3D space over 48 frames — matches a motion-capture ground truth to within 1.8 cm.
  5. Static background: the wall behind the person has near-zero motion vectors (0.3 mm RMS) — correctly separated from the moving body.
  6. Comparison: a static-scene DUSt3R run on the same frames produces a blurred smear at the arm — geometry and motion entangled.
  • CVPR
    Any4D — unified feed-forward metric 4D (geometry + motion) for N frames

    Standard 3D reconstruction assumes a static scene: capture images from many viewpoints, and solve for geometry. Add dynamic actors (people, cars) and the same pixel in different frames corresponds to different 3D points because the scene deformed. Any4D jointly predicts 4D geometry and motion in one feed-forward pass: for N video frames, it outputs per-frame depth (or pointmaps) plus per-pixel 3D velocity, all without per-scene fitting.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Input: a video of N frames. A token-based transformer processes all frames together (not pairwise). Each frame's tokens are enriched with temporal position embedding. The decoder predicts two outputs per frame: (1) a pointmap (H×W×3 XYZ for that frame), and (2) per-pixel 3D velocity (H×W×3 motion vector). At inference, extract the pointmaps and motion; the motion vectors reveal which 3D points moved, which stayed static. A single forward pass, no optimization.

    Why it works: The transformer sees all frames simultaneously, so it can segregate static structure (walls, tables) from dynamic motion (limbs, vehicles) by learning temporal coherence patterns. The pointmap + motion factorization is a natural representation that lets the model express both geometry and motion without entanglement.

  • CVPR
    V-DPM — extends dynamic point maps to video for feed-forward 4D + scene flow

    Dynamic point maps predict per-pixel 3D points from a single RGB image — geometric guessing. But when the scene moves (a person waves), the map's points move too; a static point map is invalid. V-DPM extends dynamic point maps to video: it predicts pointmaps over time plus per-pixel 3D flow, giving a 4D reconstruction (geometry + motion) and scene flow as a byproduct.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Input: a video clip of T frames. A ViT backbone processes frame features. A decoder predicts: (1) a pointmap for each frame (3 coordinates per pixel, in world frame), (2) per-pixel 3D scene flow (3D displacement from frame t to t+1). Enforce consistency: if pixel (x,y) has point p at frame t and flow v, then at frame t+1, a neighbouring pixel with point p+v should have the same 3D point (within noise). The consistency loss couples the predictions and forces them to be jointly coherent.

    Why it works: Temporal consistency constraints force the model to separate true geometry from transient motion. A hallucinated point that doesn't persist across frames will produce flow contradictions, so the model learns to output real surfaces and true motion.

  • CVPR
    Mesh4D — a VAE encodes deformation for topology-consistent 4D meshes from one camera

    Reconstructing dynamic scenes as meshes is hard: meshes have fixed topology, but a deforming body's surface doesn't — holes appear, surfaces split and merge. A VAE encodes deformations as latent vectors: if a base mesh is given, a small latent code can represent how it bends, stretches, and twists. Mesh4D trains a VAE on 4D mesh sequences (monocular video of deforming objects), learning a compact deformation space so that inference can predict 4D meshes with fixed topology.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Offline: collect 4D ground-truth mesh sequences (from mocap or multi-view reconstruction) and train a VAE that encodes per-frame vertex offsets from a base template into a low-dimensional latent code, then reconstructs the deformed mesh. Online: given a monocular video, a neural network predicts a sequence of latent codes (one per frame). Decode each code to a mesh. The VAE's latent space is smooth, so interpolating latent codes gives smooth deformations.

    Why it works: VAEs learn a continuous deformation manifold, so the network can generalize beyond training deformations via interpolation. Fixed topology via the base mesh avoids the need to predict vertex correspondence or handle topology changes, simplifying the problem considerably.

  • CVPR
    DetAny4D — streaming 4D detection with a 280k-sequence 4D-annotated dataset

    Training 4D models needs 4D ground truth — synchronized multi-view video or mocap-annotated sequences. These are expensive and rare. To scale 4D learning, DetAny4D introduces a new 280,000-sequence 4D-annotated dataset and tackles streaming 4D detection: online prediction of 4D trajectories (object position, geometry, motion) frame-by-frame, without waiting for all frames.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Architecture: a recurrent transformer that maintains a hidden state encoding the scene's geometry and motion history. Each new frame: extract features, fuse with the hidden state, predict the current frame's pointmap + per-object 3D position and velocity. Output a stream of detected 4D bounding boxes and per-object meshes or pointmaps. The hidden state allows causal prediction (no future frames), and recurrence lets temporal context accumulate.

    Why it works: Streaming avoids the latency of batching frames, critical for real-time robotics. Large-scale 4D data enables learning rich motion and deformation priors. Recurrent hidden states efficiently represent which scene regions are dynamic vs static, improving prediction accuracy.

Depth from generative & diffusion priors

vision
the problem

Monocular depth is geometrically ambiguous: many 3D scenes project to the same image. A regression network resolves this by training on depth-labelled images — but it inherits those labels' biases (mostly indoors, daylight, nearby objects) and fails on scenes far from its training data. A diffusion model has seen far more of the visual world and knows what plausible 3D looks like from texture, lighting, and object statistics. The idea is to use that generative prior as a geometry prior — treating depth estimation as a conditional image-generation problem, not a regression.

the approaches

Use a generative model — a diffusion prior — as the inductive bias that resolves monocular depth’s inherent ambiguity, and to bridge synthetic-to-real gaps.

The generative prior supplies the ‘what plausible 3D looks like’ that geometry alone can’t.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Input: single photo of a foggy forest — no training data of foggy forests exists in depth datasets.
  2. Iris forward pass: the image is encoded as a conditioning signal; a diffusion model generates a depth-image pair over 20 denoising steps.
  3. Distillation loss: a student depth-regression head is trained to match the diffusion output — learns the prior without running full diffusion at test time.
  4. Metric anchor: one GPS altitude reading pins the absolute scale — z = 14.2 m at the horizon.
  5. Result: relative depth ranking accuracy (δ1 metric) = 0.91, vs 0.74 for a standard regression model on the same foggy scene.
  6. Failure mode: mirror surfaces — the diffusion model hallucinates geometry behind the reflection; the depth map shows a phantom room.

    Why it works: Diffusion models learn visual signatures of transparency (gradual focus blur, refraction ripples) from data. By conditioning on RGB, the model knows where transparency is likely (clear regions that are coherent across frames). Clamping known depths prevents hallucination while letting the model fill gaps with learned priors.

    DiffSSC — denoising diffusion completes semantic LiDAR scans through occlusion

    LiDAR occupancy grids are sparse: only points that were hit are recorded; empty space and occluded regions are unknown. Semantic scene completion (SSC) aims to predict the full grid (occupied/empty) and classify occupied voxels (car, tree, road). Diffusion models have learned the structure of scenes (walls are usually vertical, cars are rectangular) and can use these priors to hallucinate hidden geometry. DiffSSC uses diffusion-based completion to inpaint occluded regions in LiDAR scans, jointly predicting geometry and semantics.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: Input: a sparse 3D LiDAR grid (voxels that returned a hit, with intensity). Create a dense grid where hit voxels are observed and the rest are masked (unknown). A diffusion prior iteratively refines this grid over T steps, conditioned on the observed hits and any additional input (camera images, LiDAR intensity patterns). At each step, the model predicts the denoising direction for unknown voxels, learning to place walls, ground planes, and obstacles that are plausible given the observed points. Finally, a classifier predicts the semantic label (vehicle, pedestrian, building, etc.) for each occupied voxel.

    Why it works: Diffusion learns scene-level priors: walls are planar and continuous, ground is flat, objects have canonical shapes. These priors let the model extrapolate beyond the sparse LiDAR returns, filling occlusions correctly. Joint geometry + semantics prediction is more powerful than either alone.

Part IV

Where it stands

Recovering 3D from images is the oldest problem in computer vision and still not solved. What is genuinely still hard:

Metric scale from one eye. Monocular depth is scale-ambiguous by construction; getting reliable metric distance without a second view or IMU is still hard.
Hidden geometry. Transparent, specular, and thin surfaces actively hide their 3D structure and defeat matching-based depth.
Accuracy vs the onboard clock. Dense SLAM, MVS, and global bundle adjustment are heavy; real-time on a small robot forces sparse, approximate choices.
Dynamic & deforming scenes. 4D reconstruction compounds the ambiguity — the same video can come from different motion + geometry pairs.
Feed-forward generalization. One-pass 3D predictors are fast but generalize less sharply to geometry unlike their training data.
Robustness in the wild. Low light, weather, texture-less walls, and long-term appearance change still break perception; fusion and events help, not fully.
