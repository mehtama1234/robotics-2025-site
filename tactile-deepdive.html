The big picture — connecting the dots

Every robot in the world relies on one broken assumption: that if it knows where its hand is in space, it can do the task. That assumption fails the moment anything in the world pushes back. A key press goes unfinished because the robot does not feel the resistance. A robot peeling an apple gouges too deep or fails to grip because force control is missing. A connector that should click into place stays loose because the insertion force is not measured. This is the core problem: real contact work is defined not by target position but by target force — and that force changes moment to moment as geometry, friction, and compliance shift in ways impossible to predict ahead of time.

Five moves the field is making:

  • ① Record force in human demonstrations, then teach the robot to replay it. Capture what a person does — including how hard they press — and train the robot to copy it. ForceMimic uses a wearable device (ForceCapture) to record both motion and force without a robot present, then trains a controller (HybridIL) to output both target position and target force at each step. Learning Visuotactile Skills With Two Multifingered Hands builds a low-cost remote-control system (HATO) from prosthetic hands with touch sensors and records human operators doing two-handed tasks.
  • ② Build sensors that are cheap, robust, and easy to swap. Touch sensors that work and hold up under use are the foundation. AnySkin separates the soft sensing skin from electronics so the skin peels off and snaps back on without tools or recalibration — skills trained on one skin work on a fresh one. PolyTouch packs a camera-based touch surface, microphone, and small outward-facing camera into one finger-shaped unit with 20x the lifespan of commercial sensors. ORCA is an open-source tendon-driven hand with 17 joints and built-in touch sensors that self-tighten and automatically tension cables to survive long training runs.
  • ③ Blend touch with vision so each fills the other's gaps. Cameras are blocked by the robot's own hand; touch sensors only measure what is already in contact. Combining them lets each cover the other's weak spots. ViTa-Zero figures out the exact pose of a held object without any touch training data by starting from a camera estimate and using touch readings to pull it toward the contact point. Adaptive Visuo-Tactile Fusion with Predictive Force Attention automatically shifts weight between sensors moment to moment — it watches force readings unfold and rebalances without any human labels.
  • ④ Learn to control force and softness directly. Rather than just sense force, teach the controller to actively manage compliance — how much the arm should give way when pushed. Adaptive Compliance Policy learns from human demonstrations how much the arm should give at each point in space and time, then uses a diffusion policy to turn that into smooth motions that avoid large contact forces. TacDiffusion trains a generative model on recordings of the three-dimensional forces felt during successful peg insertions so it can generate good force sequences for new insertions and new part shapes.
  • ⑤ Share knowledge across sensors and make transfer work. Labs use different touch sensors, so data from one cannot be used in another. ACROSS converts each sensor's signals into a shared 3D shape mesh, so old data from a discontinued sensor can be reused on a newer one. Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining trains on two different sensors touching the same objects to map both into a shared internal space. Canonical Representation and Force-Based Pretraining of 3D Tactile converts raw 3D touch data to a standard form and pre-trains a feature extractor using force prediction before fine-tuning on specific tasks.

The throughline: this theme is the field closing the gap between what a robot knows (where its hand is) and what it needs to know (how much the world is pushing back). Vision cannot fill this gap — contact happens at sub-millimeter scales and faster than vision loops can react. Force and touch let the robot detect slip, measure real-time pressure, and decide whether to push harder, ease off, or stop. The papers here show how to record human force patterns, build durable sensors, fuse touch with sight, and transfer learned skills across different hardware. Still open: reliability over thousands of repeated contacts, generalization to surfaces and objects very different from training data, and making touch signals transfer cleanly across sensor types and robot hands — progress on all three is active but not yet bulletproof.

The problem — in depth

When you press a key on a keyboard, your fingers feel resistance and stop pushing before you break anything. When you peel a carrot, your hand adjusts grip strength ten times a second in response to how the peel pulls back. A robot that only tracks where its hand is in space — without any sense of how hard it is pressing — cannot do either of those things. It will snap the key, gouge the carrot, or fumble the peel entirely. This is the core problem: many real tasks are defined not by where you move but by how much force you apply, and the force changes in ways you cannot predict ahead of time.

This matters because the gap between robots that work in labs and robots that work in homes or factories is largely a gap in contact handling. Lab demos pick up objects sitting still on clean tables. Real jobs — plugging in a cable, wiping a counter, screwing a cap, separating stuck panels, caring for a person — all require the robot to push, slide, peel, or press against objects that push back. The object's resistance changes as the task goes on. A pure position controller (one that only tracks "move hand to point X") has no way to respond to that feedback. It either pushes too hard and breaks something, or not hard enough and fails.

The deeper issue is that vision alone cannot fill this gap. A camera can tell you roughly where an object is, but it cannot tell you whether the grip is slipping, how much a part is flexing, or whether a connector is seated. The hand itself blocks the view. Contact happens at sub-millimeter scales that cameras cannot resolve. And contact events happen faster than any vision-based loop can respond.

This connects directly to the one big challenge every robot faces: taking in an unclear, changing world, deciding what to do, and moving a real body — all fast, cheap, safe, and general. Touch and force close the loop between the body and the world in a way that vision cannot. Without them, the "move" part of the loop is open: the robot acts but does not truly feel what is happening. With them, the robot has a signal it can react to, a way to detect when something is going wrong, and a basis for deciding whether to push harder, ease off, or stop.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Recording force during human demonstrations, then teaching the robot to replay it

In plain termsWhen a person peels a carrot, their hand knows exactly how hard to press — capture that force, not just the motion, and the robot can copy it instead of guessing and either crushing the carrot or dropping it.
The sub-problemStandard motion-capture systems record where the hand moves but not how hard it presses, so a robot copying the recording moves to the right place but applies the wrong force — pressing too hard or too lightly. Capturing both motion and force is key to learning tasks like peeling or handling delicate parts.
Force capture via wearable device — ForceMimic. A person wears a glove-like device (ForceCapture) while doing the task without a robot present, recording both motion and force; a controller (HybridIL) then trains the robot to output both target position and target force at each step.
Bimanual visuotactile imitation — Learning Visuotactile Skills With Two Multifingered Hands. Built a low-cost remote-control system (HATO) from off-the-shelf parts and touch-sensing prosthetic hands, then recorded human operators and trained the robot to replay both visual and touch signals.
Diffusion policy conditioned on force — Force-Conditioned Diffusion Policies for Compliant Sheet Separation Tasks. Trains on expert recordings that include force data, then uses a diffusion policy to produce actions that respond to live force readings at run time.

A robot trained on human force patterns can now handle contact-heavy tasks like vegetable peeling with 54.5% higher success than vision-only control.

2. Building sensors that are cheap, robust, and easy to replace

In plain termsTouch sensors wear out like phone screens — swap a broken one for a fresh one and the robot should keep using all the skills it learned, not start from scratch because the new sensor feels slightly different.
The sub-problemTouch sensors wear out quickly, and when they do, replacing them usually breaks the learned skills that were tuned to that specific sensor's characteristics. Sensors must be cheap to replace and durable enough to survive thousands of repeated contacts without recalibration.
Separable skin sensing — AnySkin. The soft sensing skin peels off and snaps back on without tools or recalibration, so skills trained on one skin instance still work on a fresh replacement.
Multi-modal finger sensor — PolyTouch. Packs a camera-based touch surface, microphone, and small outward-facing camera into one finger-shaped unit with 20x the lifespan of commercial sensors.
Open-source tendon-driven hand — ORCA. A 17-joint hand with built-in touch sensors, self-tightening joints, and automatic cable tensioning so it survives long training runs; designs and instructions are fully public.
Humanoid-scale tactile fingertip — HumanFT. Fits a tiny camera, pressure circuit, and vibration circuit into a fingertip-sized soft shell small enough for humanoid hands.

Robots can now swap sensors and hands without losing learned skills or requiring expensive recalibration.

3. Blending touch with vision so each fills the other's gaps

In plain termsYour eyes go blind inside a dark pocket but your fingers work fine; use both at once and you find keys anywhere. Same for robots — cameras see shape, touch finds exactly where to grip and how much the object is bending.
The sub-problemCameras cannot see where the hand is touching or how much the object is deforming; touch sensors only measure what is already in contact and miss the big picture. Neither is sufficient alone, so combining them lets each cover the other's blind spots.
Touch-refined pose estimation — ViTa-Zero. Starts from a camera-only pose estimate of a held object, then uses touch readings to pull the estimate toward the actual contact point and joint angles to eliminate impossible positions.
Adaptive sensor weighting — Adaptive Visuo-Tactile Fusion with Predictive Force Attention. Automatically shifts weight between sensors moment to moment by watching force readings as the task unfolds, without needing human labels.
Touch repair for opaque objects — FusionSense. Rebuilds the 3D shape of transparent or very dark objects by building a rough shape from camera images, then using touch readings to fix the fine surface details.
Language grounding for multimodal sensing — Beyond Sight (FuSe). Takes a pretrained robot policy trained mostly on cameras and fine-tunes it to use touch and sound by training it to describe what each sensor is reading in plain language.

A robot using both vision and touch can handle tasks where vision alone is blocked or ambiguous, with zero-shot reasoning across sensing modes.

4. Learning to control force and softness directly

In plain termsReach for a doorknob gently; grab a sports ball with more grip; pick up a egg with extreme care. A smart robot learns when to be stiff for power and when to be soft so it does not break things.
The sub-problemA robot that only controls position will push with full stiffness until it hits something, then either cause damage or get stuck. Instead, the robot should learn to be soft (compliance) at the right moments — giving way when needed, pushing firm when safe — so contact never breaks things.
Compliance profile via diffusion — Adaptive Compliance Policy. Learns from human demonstrations how much the arm should give at each point in space and time, then uses a diffusion policy to convert that into smooth motions that avoid large contact forces.
Force-domain generative model — TacDiffusion. Trains a generative model on recordings of the 3D forces and torques felt during successful peg insertions, so it learns to generate good force sequences for new insertions and new part shapes the robot has never seen.
Real-time online force learning — Efficient Online Learning of Contact Force Models. Fits a simple force model to sensor data in real time on a GPU, updating it during the task itself, which matters for precise connector insertion where even small model errors cause failure.

A robot controlling both position and compliance achieves 95.7% success on high-precision insertions it has never practiced.

5. Sharing knowledge across sensors and making pre-trained features

In plain termsYour sense of touch works the same whether you bought brand-name gloves or generic ones — a robot's touch skills should work the same way, moving to a new sensor without losing everything learned on the old one.
The sub-problemDifferent labs use different touch sensor types, so datasets built for one sensor are useless on another. A skill trained on an old sensor breaks when that sensor is discontinued. Sensors need a shared language so data and skills transfer across hardware types.
Sensor normalization via 3D shape — ACROSS. Converts each sensor's signals into a shared 3D shape (a mesh showing how the sensor surface bends under contact) so old data from a discontinued sensor can be reused to train models for a newer one.
Contrastive pretraining across sensors — Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining. Trains a model on two different sensors touching the same objects, teaching it to map both sensors' signals to the same shared internal space.
Canonical form and force-based pretraining — Canonical Representation and Force-Based Pretraining of 3D Tactile for Dexterous Visuo-Tactile Policy Learning. Converts raw 3D touch data to a simpler standard form, then pre-trains a feature extractor by teaching it to predict forces from touch data alone before fine-tuning on specific tasks.

Skills and datasets now transfer across sensor types without re-labeling, and pretraining reduces the demonstrations needed to learn new tasks by 78%.

Where it stands

Force and touch sensing in robots has moved from a mostly hardware question to a mostly learning question. Researchers can now build usable, low-cost touch sensors and record human demonstrations that include force. Diffusion-based policies that condition on force have shown clear results on precise tasks like peg insertion and connector plugging that pure position control cannot handle. The remaining hard parts are reliability over long runs, generalization to surfaces and objects very different from training, and making touch signals transfer cleanly across sensor types and robot hands — progress on all three is active but not complete.

Learning Visuotactile Skills With Two Multifingered Hands

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Teaching a multi-fingered robot to do two-handed tasks requires hardware (dexterous hands) and controllers that are either prohibitively expensive or do not exist at low cost, so most robots stay single-handed and clumsy.

The approach. Build a cheap teleoperation rig using modified prosthetic hands (HATO) that already have touch sensors embedded, record human operators doing bimanual tasks over that interface, and train a policy to replay both motion and contact signals from camera and touch data.

What's unique. Demonstrates end-to-end bimanual visuotactile learning using off-the-shelf prosthetics instead of custom hardware, showing that dataset size and touch-sensor fusion both matter for performance.

What it solves

Teaching a two-handed robot with many fingers to do precise tasks using both sight and touch is expensive because the needed hardware and controllers do not exist at low cost.

How it works

They built a cheap remote-control system (HATO) using off-the-shelf parts and modified prosthetic hands that have touch sensors, then recorded human demonstrations and trained the robot to copy those actions using both camera images and touch data.

Why it matters

End-to-end system enabling bimanual multifingered visuotactile imitation learning with an empirical study of dataset size, sensing modality, and visual preprocessing effects on policy performance.

ForceMimic: Force-Centric Imitation Learning with Force-Motion Capture System for Contact-Rich Manipulation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. When a human peels a vegetable or handles delicate parts, their fingers apply specific, changing pressures — but standard motion capture records only where the hand moves, not how hard it presses, so a robot copying the video applies the wrong force and breaks or fumbles the task.

The approach. Record both motion and force using a wearable glove (ForceCapture) that a human wears while performing the task without a robot present, then train a hybrid controller (HybridIL) that learns to output both target position and target force at each timestep.

What's unique. Decouples force recording from robot hardware — the person wears a glove, not a robot arm — making it fast to collect. Shows 54.5% success gain over vision-only for contact-heavy tasks like peeling.

What it solves

Robots learning to do contact-heavy tasks like peeling vegetables by copying human demonstrations fail because the recordings capture where the hand moves but not how hard it presses.

How it works

ForceCapture is a wearable glove-like device a person wears while doing the task without a robot present, recording both motion and force. HybridIL then learns a policy that outputs both a target force and a target position, and a controller applies them together.

Why it matters

An end-to-end force-centric demonstration and learning system that captures human force profiles without a robot and trains a hybrid force-position controller, increasing peeling task success by 54.5% over vision-only baselines.

Adaptive Compliance Policy: Learning Approximate Compliance for Diffusion Guided Control

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A robot trained only to move its arm to a target position will push with maximum stiffness and crash through delicate objects, or get stuck on an obstacle and fail because it has no strategy to ease off or give way when the world pushes back.

The approach. Learn from human demonstrations a "compliance profile" — a map of how soft the arm should be at each location in space and each moment in time — then use that profile to condition a diffusion policy that generates smooth, compliant motions.

What's unique. Encodes softness as a learnable spatial-temporal map from demos, not just a single stiffness value. Achieves over 50% improvement over position-only baselines by letting the arm actively give way.

What it solves

Robots that only control position push too hard when they make contact with objects, causing failures in tasks that require careful force management.

How it works

The system learns from human demonstrations how much the arm should "give" at each point in space and time — called a compliance profile — then uses a diffusion policy (a type of generative model) to turn that profile into smooth motions that avoid large contact forces.

Why it matters

A demonstration-driven framework that learns task-specific spatiotemporal compliance profiles to condition diffusion-based visuomotor control, achieving >50% improvement over position-only baselines.

TacDiffusion: Force-Domain Diffusion Policy for Precise Tactile Manipulation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Inserting a peg into a hole requires applying the exact right force sequence in 3D — push too hard and the peg jams, too soft and it misses. A policy trained for one peg shape usually fails on new shapes because the force dynamics changed but the robot has no way to adapt on the fly.

The approach. Train a generative model on force and torque sequences recorded during successful insertions, learning the signature of what good forces feel like. At test time, the model generates a force sequence that should work, a smoothing filter bridges the gap between the slow generative model and the fast control loop.

What's unique. Operates in force/wrench space, not Cartesian space — the policy thinks about forces directly rather than hand positions. Achieves 95.7% zero-shot transfer to new part shapes.

What it solves

Robots need to insert pegs or small parts into tight holes with very high precision, and a skill learned for one part shape must also work on new shapes it has never seen.

How it works

They train a generative model (diffusion policy) on recordings of the 3D forces and torques felt during successful insertions, so the model learns to generate good force sequences for new insertions; a filter smooths out timing mismatches between the slow generative model and the fast control loop.

Why it matters

Achieves 95.7% zero-shot transfer success across diverse high-precision insertion tasks by applying diffusion policy in the force/wrench domain rather than Cartesian action space.

PolyTouch: A Robust Multi-Modal Tactile Sensor for Contact-Rich Manipulation Using Tactile-Diffusion Policies

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Tight-insertion tasks (pegs, clips, connectors) need touch feedback because the hand occludes the view, but existing touch sensors wear out after hundreds of contacts, die mid-experiment, and require expensive re-tuning when replaced.

The approach. Pack a tactile camera, microphone, and outward camera into one durable finger-shaped unit. Feed all three signals together with arm position to a diffusion-based policy trained from human demonstrations doing the same contact task.

What's unique. Multi-modal in a single finger (20x commercial sensor lifespan). Combines three sensing modalities — touch, sound, vision — in the same policy, outperforming touch-alone or vision-alone.

What it solves

Tasks like inserting a peg or fastening a clip require precise touch feedback, but camera-only robot policies fail because the hand blocks the view, and existing touch sensors wear out quickly.

How it works

PolyTouch is a finger-shaped sensor that combines a camera-based touch surface, a microphone for sound, and a small outward-facing camera into one unit; the signals from all three are fed together with arm position data into a diffusion-based control policy trained from human demonstrations.

Why it matters

A durable multi-modal tactile finger (20x commercial sensor lifespan) paired with tactile-diffusion policies that significantly outperform haptic-oblivious baselines on contact-rich tasks.

Beyond Sight: Finetuning Generalist Robot Policies with Heterogeneous Sensors via Language Grounding

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Large-scale robot policies trained mostly on camera data cannot interpret touch or sound sensors, which are critical when vision is blocked or ambiguous — they have no learned mapping from sensor readings to action.

The approach. Take a pretrained vision-based policy and fine-tune it to read touch and sound by adding two losses: one that pulls different sensor readings into a shared learned space, one that trains the model to describe what each sensor is reading in plain language.

What's unique. Uses language grounding to bridge modalities — the policy learns to say what it feels, which lets it reason across cameras, touch, and sound without separate multimodal datasets.

What it solves

General-purpose robot AI trained mostly on camera data cannot use touch or sound sensors, which are needed when vision is blocked or not enough on its own.

How it works

FuSe takes a pretrained robot policy and fine-tunes it on tasks that include touch and sound by training two extra losses: one that pulls sensor readings from different modalities closer together in a shared space, and one that trains the model to describe what each sensor is reading in plain language.

Why it matters

A finetuning recipe that extends any generalist policy to heterogeneous sensing modalities via language grounding, enabling zero-shot multimodal prompting and cross-modal reasoning without large multimodal datasets.

Canonical Representation and Force-Based Pretraining of 3D Tactile for Dexterous Visuo-Tactile Policy Learning

The big idea — first principles

The problem. 3D tactile arrays produce high-dimensional point clouds of the sensor surface deforming under contact, but there are no large labeled datasets or pretrained models to extract useful features, so each new task requires collecting hundreds of labeled demos from scratch.

The approach. Convert raw 3D touch point clouds to a canonical form (standard geometry-normalized representation) to simplify learning, then pre-train a feature extractor by teaching it to predict forces directly from touch — self-supervised without labels — before fine-tuning on specific manipulation tasks.

What's unique. Force-based self-supervised pretraining reduces labeled demos needed by 78%. Works across four dexterous tasks without large labeled datasets, using only the robot's own force sensor as free labels.

What it solves

Learning useful features from the high-dimensional touch data produced by 3D tactile sensors on multi-fingered hands is hard because there is no large labeled dataset and no pretrained model to start from.

How it works

They first convert raw 3D touch data into a simpler standard form (canonical representation) to reduce geometric complexity, then pre-train a feature extractor by teaching it to predict both local and total forces from the touch data alone, before fine-tuning on specific hand manipulation tasks.

Why it matters

Proposes canonical 3D tactile representations with force-based self-supervised pretraining, achieving 78% average success rate across four contact-rich dexterous manipulation tasks without large labeled datasets.

AnySkin: Plug-and-Play Skin Sensing for Robotic Touch

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Touch sensor skins wear out and need replacement, but touching the electronics or applying new glue changes the sensor's response, breaking all the learned manipulation skills that were tuned to the original sensor's particular characteristics.

The approach. Separate the soft magnetic sensing skin from the electronics using a snap-on magnetic interface so the skin can be swapped without touching anything else, and show that policies trained on one skin instance generalize to a fresh replacement with zero recalibration.

What's unique. First tactile sensor with zero-shot cross-instance generalization — skills transfer to a completely new physical skin without any retraining or recalibration.

What it solves

Touch sensors on robot hands are hard to swap or repair because the sensing element and the electronics are combined, and replacing a worn skin usually breaks the learned manipulation skills that were tuned to that specific sensor.

How it works

They separate the magnetic sensing skin from the electronics so the skin peels off and snaps back on without tools or recalibration, and they show that manipulation skills trained on one skin instance still work on a fresh replacement.

Why it matters

First uncalibrated tactile sensor demonstrating zero-shot cross-instance generalization of manipulation policies, with a streamlined fabrication process enabling plug-and-play replacement without recalibration.

TransForce: Transferable Force Prediction for Vision-Based Tactile Sensors with Sequential Image Translation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Vision-based touch sensors come in different versions with different marker patterns or colors. When you switch to a new model, you must collect hundreds of new force-labeled images from scratch because the visual appearance changed.

The approach. Train a neural network to translate images from the old sensor to look like images from the new sensor, then feed those translated sequences to a second network that predicts forces; this way the force predictor never sees the old sensor's actual appearance.

What's unique. Domain translation via sequential images — translating full image streams rather than static frames — enables accurate force prediction on unseen sensor variants without re-labeling.

What it solves

When you buy a new touch sensor with different colors or marker patterns, you have to collect a large new set of force-labeled images from scratch before it can predict forces.

How it works

A neural network first converts images from the old sensor to look like images from the new sensor, then a second network reads those converted image sequences to estimate forces in all three directions.

Why it matters

Demonstrates that domain transfer via sequential image translation enables accurate shear-direction force prediction on unseen sensor configurations without re-labeling.

VITaL Pretraining: Visuo-Tactile Pretraining for Tactile and Non-Tactile Manipulation Policies

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Learning to handle objects through touch requires large amounts of data, and it is wasteful because a robot trained on camera+touch data cannot use that touch knowledge at test time if the touch sensor is absent or broken.

The approach. Pretrain a shared visual-tactile representation by training on paired camera and touch data, then use that learned representation to initialize a policy that runs at test time using only the camera — the touch knowledge is baked into the learned features.

What's unique. Touch data helps learning but is not required at inference — improves camera-only policy accuracy to nearly tactile-agent levels, e.g., USB plugging rises from 20% to 85%.

What it solves

Teaching a robot to handle objects through touch requires a lot of demonstrations, and a robot without touch sensors at test time cannot use what was learned from touch.

How it works

The robot is first trained on paired camera and touch-sensor data to learn a shared visual-tactile representation. That representation is then used to initialize a policy that runs at test time using only the camera, with no touch sensor.

Why it matters

Demonstrates that visuo-tactile pretraining improves non-tactile policy performance to near-tactile-agent levels, including a USB-plugging task where accuracy rises from 20% to 85%.
