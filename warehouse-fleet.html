Running the fleet

A single robot is the easy part. A hundred of them sharing the same aisles, junctions, chargers, and pick stations is where warehouses win or grind to a halt. Five fleet-level decisions — who does what, who goes first, how to break a jam, how to stay charged, and how to keep the stations balanced — each a plain rule, run and measured, with the failure it prevents. The first pipeline at fleet scale.

Every stage below is a place a fleet quietly loses money or seizes up: overdriving, colliding, deadlocking, stranding, or starving idle stations. Each is a coordination rule measured against the failure it prevents — the difference between a warehouse that flows and one that jams.

1

Hand out the jobs

Multi-robot

A wave of pick orders drops and every idle robot grabs the job nearest to it. That looks efficient robot-by-robot, but it leaves a robot on the far side sprinting across the whole warehouse for a job another robot was right next to — the fleet's total travel balloons.

Assign the whole fleet at once to minimize total travel; grabbing the nearest job first is greedy and leaves everyone else stranded.
total travel — greedy nearest25.0 m
total travel — optimal assignment18.6 m
fleet driving saved26%

For 7 robots and 7 jobs, greedy nearest-first assignment racks up 25.0 m of total travel. Solving the assignment optimally does the same work in 18.6 m — 26% less driving for the identical set of jobs.

2

Cross the intersection

Navigation

Several aisles meet at one junction, and robots from every direction plan straight through it whenever they arrive. Their independently-optimal paths all want the same square at the same second — and two robots in one square is a collision.

Reserve the shared intersection so one robot crosses at a time; letting everyone enter when they like is how they collide in it.
collision steps — free-for-all4
collision steps — reserved0
total added wait19 steps

With 6 robots converging on the junction, letting them enter freely produces 4 collision time-steps. Reserving the cell drops that to 0 — for just 19 steps of total waiting across the fleet.

3

Break the jam

Multi-robot

Two robots meet head-on in a one-wide aisle, each needing to reach the other end. Give them the same polite rule — 'if something's in the way, stop and wait' — and they stop nose to nose and wait for each other forever. The aisle is jammed and neither job finishes.

Break a head-on corridor tie with a yield rule (one robot ducks into a bay); a symmetric 'both wait' deadlocks forever.
deliveries done — symmetric wait0 (deadlock)
deliveries done — yield rule2
steps to clear the jam9

In a one-wide corridor with a passing bay at cell 3, the symmetric 'both wait' rule completes 0 of the two deliveries — a permanent deadlock. The yield rule clears the jam and completes 2 in 9 steps.

4

Keep the fleet alive

Multi-robot

Robots run on batteries and there is always another job to take. Chase jobs until the battery is empty and a robot dies mid-aisle — now it is not just idle, it is an obstacle that has to be rescued, and it took its half-finished job down with it.

Send a robot to charge while it can still reach the charger; running every robot until it dies strands them dead in the aisles.
stranded robots — run till dead8 of 8
stranded robots — scheduled0
jobs done — run-till-dead vs scheduled16 vs 200

Across 8 robots, running each until it dies strands 8 of them in the aisles and completes only 16 jobs. Charging by reachability strands 0 and completes 200.

5

Balance the stations

Multi-robot

Robots carry every tote to the same convenient pick station because it is closest. That station's queue grows without bound while the other two stand idle — the whole fleet's throughput is capped at what one station can process, no matter how many robots you add.

Send each item to the shortest-queue station; piling every job on one station caps the whole fleet at that station's rate.
throughput — pile on one0.33 items/step
throughput — balanced1.0 items/step
max queue — one vs balanced400 vs 1

With 3 pick stations (each 1 item per 3 steps), piling every tote on one station caps throughput at 0.33 items/step and lets its queue blow up to 400. Shortest-queue routing reaches 1.0 items/step with a max queue of 1.
