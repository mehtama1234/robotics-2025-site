The big picture — connecting the dots

Legged robots are always a fraction of a second away from falling. Unlike wheeled machines or arms, they have to push exactly the right amount of force at exactly the right time to stay upright, and adjust on the fly when the ground shifts. That is hard enough. But the real problem runs deeper: a controller trained to walk in a perfect physics simulator — where friction is predictable, motors obey commands instantly, and the floor never surprises — will fail the moment it meets real hardware, where everything is slightly wrong. The simulator-to-reality gap is the central puzzle. Add that every robot now needs to do multiple tasks — a humanoid must walk and use its arms, a quadruped must climb ladders and cross rough terrain — and a single hand-coded controller per task becomes impossible. The convergence of papers here reflects the field attacking three interconnected fronts: close the sim-to-real gap by building simulators that actually match hardware, train single controllers that can handle many behaviors by finding a unified representation (motion imitation, CPG modulation, command masking), and exploit the physics of the body itself — the natural pendulum swing of the legs — to move efficiently instead of fighting it.

The main angles the 2025 papers are taking:

  • ① Train in simulation with domain randomization, then transfer directly. Berkeley Humanoid: A Research Platform for Learning-Based Control shows that when the robot is designed to be easy to simulate (simple actuators, well-understood dynamics), only mild randomization is needed. Robust Ladder Climbing with a Quadrupedal Robot adds hardware morphology co-design — custom hooked feet and teacher-student distillation make the policy transferable to real climbers.
  • ② Build one controller that accepts many modes of input. HOVER: Versatile Neural Whole-Body Controller for Humanoid Robots unifies walking, reaching, and carrying through motion-imitation teacher policy and DAgger distillation. AllGaits: Learning All Quadruped Gaits and Transitions uses a central pattern generator (CPG) that one network modulates to select among nine gaits. MOVE: Multi-Skill Omnidirectional Legged Locomotion With Limited Observations combines vision and proprioception with contrastive learning so one policy handles climbing, jumping, crawling, and stairs.
  • ③ Close the sim-to-real gap by modeling real hardware inside the simulator. Cycloidal Quasi-Direct Drive Actuator Designs with Learning-Based Backstepping Control learns the nonlinear input-output behavior of cycloidal-gear motors and embeds that learned model in the simulator. Legged Robot State Estimation with Invariant Extended Kalman Filter Using Neural Measurement Network combines a neural network with Kalman filtering to estimate speed and foot contact on slippery ground. Physically-Consistent Parameter Identification of Robots in Contact solves for the true mass and inertia values of a robot using only motor data, no force sensors needed.
  • ④ Use the robot's body shape to move efficiently. The Duke Humanoid is designed so its legs' natural pendulum swing matches a useful walking speed, then trained to ride that swing. Adaptive Energy Regularization for Autonomous Gait Transition lets the robot discover on its own when to switch between walking and trotting by penalizing energy per meter. Zippy takes it to the extreme — one motor, rounded feet, no sensors, pure passive dynamics.
  • ⑤ Combine leg motion with arm control in real time. Mobile-TeleVision trains legs and arms separately using human teleoperation data, then combines them at run time. Real-Time Whole-Body Control of Legged Robots with Model-Predictive Path Integral Control runs thousands of action sequences in parallel on multiple cores, scores them by the goal, and picks the best — all at 50 Hz on real hardware.

The throughline: the field is converging on bridging simulation and reality not through brute-force randomization alone, but through (a) hardware design that makes simulation accurate, (b) unified representations like motion imitation or CPG modulation that let one policy span many behaviors, and (c) explicit modeling of the physics that matters — friction, motor delays, body inertia — so simulators match hardware. The 2025 work is productionizing those insights: the controllers now work on real humanoids and quadrupeds for hours, in complex terrain, switching tasks without retraining. Still open: matching human walking smoothness and arm precision at the same time on humanoids, sustaining useful work in truly unpredictable outdoor environments for days at a time, and rapidly teaching a deployed robot a new movement skill without any retraining.

The problem — in depth

Walking looks easy. Humans do it without thinking. But when you try to build a machine that walks, you run into a set of hard problems fast.

A walking robot is always about to fall. At any moment, one or more feet are off the ground. The robot has to push against the floor with just the right amount of force, at just the right time, to keep its body from tipping. That force changes every fraction of a second. Miss the timing and the robot goes down.

Terrain makes this worse. Real ground is not flat. There are steps, gravel, wet surfaces, slopes, and random objects. The robot cannot see the whole path ahead. It has to feel what the ground is doing under each foot and adjust on the fly. A small surprise — a patch of ice, a loose stone — can end a walk that was going fine.

Energy is a second hard problem. Moving a heavy body on legs burns a lot of power. The legs on a well-built robot have a natural swing to them, a pendulum-like motion that carries energy from one step into the next for free. Getting a computer-trained controller to find and use that natural swing, rather than fighting it, is not automatic. Most controllers trained by trial and error in a computer spend energy in ways that would exhaust a real machine quickly.

The third problem is the gap between the computer world and the real world. The standard method for teaching a robot to walk is to practice inside a physics simulator — a computer program that pretends to be the physical world. Simulation is fast, cheap, and safe. You can run millions of training steps in hours with no risk of damage. But the simulator is never quite right. It gets joint friction slightly wrong, motor delays slightly wrong, the give of the floor slightly wrong. A walking controller trained entirely in a fake world often fails the moment it meets the real one. This gap is called the sim-to-real gap, and closing it is one of the central puzzles in the field.

On top of all that, legged robots are now being asked to do more than walk. Humanoid robots need to carry things, open doors, and operate in spaces built for people. Four-legged robots need to climb ladders, push heavy boxes, and navigate rough outdoor ground. A separate hand-crafted controller for each task is too slow and too expensive to build. The field wants one controller that can handle many tasks.

All of this connects to the one big problem every robot has: take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do it fast, cheap, safe, and general. Legged locomotion sits squarely at the "move" part of that loop, but it reaches back into sensing (the robot must read its own body and the terrain) and forward into decisions (which skill to use, when to switch, how to combine walking with arm work). A robot that cannot walk reliably cannot do anything else reliably either.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Training in simulation, then moving to the real robot

The sub-problemA controller trained in a perfect simulator—where friction is predictable, motors obey commands instantly, and the floor never surprises—fails the moment it meets real hardware. The solution is to vary the training conditions so widely that the one controller that survives all of them also survives reality.
Domain randomization on a well-designed robot — Berkeley Humanoid (ICRA). Built a mid-size humanoid with simple joints accurate to simulate, trained a walking controller in Isaac Gym, applied only mild condition randomization, and transferred directly to the real robot with no extra tuning.
Morphology co-design for grip — Robust Ladder Climbing with a Quadrupedal Robot (IROS). Added custom hooked feet, trained on a wide range of ladder angles and rung sizes in simulation, then moved to real hardware without retraining.

When the robot hardware is designed to be easy to simulate, the transfer gap becomes much cleaner.

Building one controller for many tasks

The sub-problemA separate hand-coded controller for each task becomes impossible to maintain. The field wants one controller that can handle many behaviors—walking, reaching, climbing—without retraining for each one.
Motion imitation with mode masking — HOVER (ICRA). Trains a teacher policy on a large library of full-body movements, then distills a single student policy that accepts different command types (velocity, position, joint angles) and can switch between them mid-episode.
Central pattern generator with modulation — AllGaits (ICRA). A single network controls a rhythm generator whose settings select among nine different leg-beat patterns, so one policy spans all known walking styles.
Vision and proprioception fusion — MOVE (ICRA). Trains a single network to handle climbing, jumping, crawling, and stair-climbing using both camera images and body-sensor data, with contrastive learning to infer what the camera cannot directly see.

One controller spanning many behaviors reduces training burden and lets the robot adapt without redeploying.

Closing the sim-to-real gap by modeling real hardware

The sub-problemInstead of papering over inaccuracy with randomization, make the simulator match real hardware by explicitly modeling the physics that matters—motor response, state estimation under uncertainty, true mass and inertia.
Learned motor model — Cycloidal Quasi-Direct Drive Actuator Designs (ICRA). Trained a small neural network to learn the actual input-output behavior of cycloidal-gear motors from real data, then embedded that learned model inside the simulator.
Neural state estimation — Legged Robot State Estimation with Invariant Extended Kalman Filter Using Neural Measurement Network (ICRA). A small neural network estimates the robot's speed and foot contact from sensor readings on slippery ground, feeding those estimates into a mathematical tracking filter.
Hardware identification without force sensors — Physically-Consistent Parameter Identification of Robots in Contact (ICRA). Figures out the true mass and inertia values using only motor data, by mathematically canceling out the effects of floor contact.

Explicit modeling of real hardware physics makes the simulator faithful enough to transfer policies directly.

Using natural motion and passive physics to save energy

The sub-problemLegs, when built right, want to swing at a certain rhythm for free. Fighting that rhythm costs energy; using it saves energy. The controller should ride the body's natural pendulum rather than override it.
Hardware designed for natural swing — The Duke Humanoid (ICRA). Designed the leg proportions so the natural pendulum swing matches a useful walking speed, then trained the controller to ride that swing rather than fight it.
Energy reward for gait discovery — Adaptive Energy Regularization for Autonomous Gait Transition (ICRA). Added a single reward term during training that penalizes energy used per meter traveled, and let the robot discover on its own when to switch between walking and trotting.
Passive dynamics without feedback — Zippy (ICRA). The smallest known power-autonomous two-legged robot uses rounded feet and one motor, relying entirely on physical shape to stay balanced with no sensors and no feedback.

Exploiting the body's natural physics saves energy and lets simpler hardware succeed.

Combining walking with arm work

The sub-problemHumanoid robots need to walk and use their arms at the same time, but legs and arms share the body's balance. Training one policy to handle both tends to hurt arm precision, so the controller must somehow do both without sacrifice.
Separate leg and arm policies, combined at runtime — Mobile-TeleVision (ICRA). Trained the legs and arms separately: a model trained on human teleoperation data learns arm motion patterns, and a separate walking policy stays balanced while those patterns play out. The two are merged at run time.
Real-time sampling over thousands of futures — Real-Time Whole-Body Control of Legged Robots with Model-Predictive Path Integral Control (ICRA). Runs thousands of random action sequences in a simulator on multiple processor cores in parallel, scores them by goal achievement, and picks the best—all fast enough to run on the real robot in real time.

Both approaches let humanoids achieve arm precision and walking balance simultaneously without task-specific tuning.

Where it stands

Legged robots can now walk over rough ground, climb ladders, switch between gaits, and do basic arm tasks — all using controllers trained mostly in simulation and moved to real hardware. The sim-to-real gap is narrowing, and single controllers are getting broader. What is still open: robots that can sustain useful work for hours in unpredictable outdoor settings, humanoids that can match a person's arm speed and walking smoothness at the same time, and methods for quickly teaching a robot a new movement skill without retraining from scratch.

Paper by paper — method & code review

Berkeley Humanoid: A Research Platform for Learning-Based Control

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Training a walking controller in simulation and moving it to real hardware fails because the simulator's motor model is wrong — it ignores friction, cogging torque, and the complex nonlinear behavior of real gearboxes. Pretending the simulator is accurate enough won't work; you have to fight the gap with massive domain randomization, which is expensive and brittle.

The approach. Instead of fighting inaccuracy, design the robot hardware to be easy to simulate accurately. Use simple joint types, high-torque quasi-direct drives with known characteristics, and measure the real friction and motor cogging from hardware. Build a simulator that includes these measured nonlinearities explicitly.

What's unique. Rather than applying randomization to paper over model mismatch, this paper flips the problem: the hardware is designed first to be simulatable, then only mild randomization is needed. The simulator includes an identified tanh-friction model and motor cogging explicitly, so the gap is physics-based, not empirical.

ICRA 2025 · 14 citations · sub-approach: train in sim then transfer (domain randomization)

What it does

Berkeley Humanoid is a mid-scale (~51 cm standing height, ~0.515 m CoM), in-house-built humanoid with 12 degrees of freedom (6 per leg) whose entire locomotion controller is a single neural network trained in simulation and deployed to hardware without any fine-tuning. The core claim is that careful hardware design — anthropomorphic kinematics, lightweight structure, high-torque quasi-direct-drive actuators with gear ratio 81:1 — produces a robot whose simulation model is accurate enough that only light domain randomization is needed to close the sim-to-real gap. The result is omnidirectional walking, single- and double-leg hopping, and outdoor trail traversal over hundreds of meters, all from one policy.

Sub-approach: train in sim then transfer (domain randomization). No teacher–student distillation, no actuator neural network, no vision — pure proprioceptive RL with physics-parameter randomization.

The method, in detail

Robot hardware.

The robot has 12 actuated joints: per leg — hip rotation (HR), hip abduction/adduction (HAA), hip flexion/extension (HFE), knee flexion/extension (KFE), foot flexion/extension (FFE), foot abduction/adduction (FAA). All joints use brushless DC motors through an 81:1 gear reduction, modeled in simulation as DC motors. Effort limits by joint group: HR/HAA 20 Nm, HFE 30 Nm, KFE 30 Nm, FFE 20 Nm, FAA 5 Nm. Initial standing height is 0.515 m.

Inputs (observation vector).

The policy receives only proprioceptive signals, concatenated into a single flat vector:

  • base linear velocity (3D, estimated on-board; noise ±0.1 m/s)
  • base angular velocity (3D; noise ±0.2 rad/s)
  • projected gravity vector (3D; noise ±0.05)
  • velocity commands (linear x, linear y, angular z, heading — 4 scalars)
  • joint positions by group: HR/HAA/HFE joints (±0.03–0.08 rad noise), KFE (±0.05), FFE (±0.08), FAA (±0.03)
  • joint velocities for all 12 joints (noise ±1.5 rad/s)
  • last action (12 scalars)

No height scan is used for the rough-terrain policy (the heightscan term is explicitly set to None in roughenv_cfg.py), so the policy is blind to terrain shape — it infers terrain from contact and proprioception alone.

Actuator model (the key sim-to-real trick).

Rather than a simple PD controller, each joint uses a custom IdentifiedActuator class (subclasses IsaacLab's DCMotor) that applies a physics-identified friction model on top of the DC motor torque:


applied_torque = PD_output - (friction_static * tanh(vel / activation_vel) + friction_dynamic * vel)

Static and dynamic friction coefficients per joint group are identified from real hardware data (e.g., KFE has friction_static=0.8 vs. HR/HAA at 0.3). Joint armature (reflected motor inertia) is set from CAD: e.g., KFE armature = 1.5e-4 × 81. This IdentifiedActuator is the primary mechanism for closing the sim-to-real gap — the motor's nonlinear friction and reflected inertia are explicitly modeled rather than lumped into domain randomization.

Policy and training.

Algorithm: PPO (proximal policy optimization) via RSL-RL, run inside IsaacLab (built on Isaac Sim 4.0.0).

  • Actor and critic: identical MLP structure, [512, 256, 128] hidden units, ELU activations.
  • Training: 30,000 iterations, 24 steps per env per iteration, PPO clip 0.2, learning rate 1e-3 (adaptive KL schedule targeting 0.01), 5 epochs per update, 4 mini-batches, GAE lambda 0.95, discount gamma 0.99.
  • Flat-terrain variant uses smaller networks [128, 128, 128] and 15,000 iterations.

Reward function.

The reward is a sum of task rewards and penalties computed per environment step:

| Term | Weight | Description |

|---|---|---|

| tracklinvelxyexp | +1.0 | Exponential kernel on lateral velocity tracking error (std=0.5) |

| trackangvelzexp | +0.5 | Exponential kernel on yaw rate tracking (std=0.5) |

| feetairtime | +2.0 | Rewards steps with air-time in [0.2, 0.5] s when moving |

| linvelz_l2 | -2.0 | Penalizes vertical base velocity |

| angvelxy_l2 | -0.05 | Penalizes pitch/roll angular rates |

| jointtorquesl2 | -1e-5 | Penalizes torque use (energy penalty) |

| actionratel2 | -0.01 | Penalizes rapid action changes |

| feet_slide | -0.25 | Penalizes foot velocity while in contact |

| undesired_contacts | -1.0 | Penalizes knee/thigh contacts with ground |

| flatorientationl2 | -0.5 | Penalizes torso tilt |

| dofposlimits | -1.0 | Penalizes joints near their limits |

| jointdeviationhip | -0.1 | L1 penalty on HR/HAA deviation from default |

Domain randomization.

Applied at episode startup (not re-sampled each step):

  • Friction of every body: static and dynamic in [0.2, 1.25]
  • Link masses: scaled ±10% (factor [0.9, 1.1]) for all bodies; base torso mass offset ±1 kg
  • Joint armature: scaled [1.0, 1.05]
  • Joint default positions: offset ±0.05 rad
  • Joint friction model: scaled ±10%
  • During episodes: random velocity pushes every 10–15 s (±0.5 m/s linear)

Commands and curriculum.

Velocity commands are sampled uniformly: vx ∈ [-1, 1] m/s, vy ∈ [-1, 1] m/s, yaw ∈ [-1, 1] rad/s. Heading command with stiffness 0.5 is used instead of raw yaw rate. Terrain curriculum starts at level 0 and advances as the robot succeeds.

Reviewing it yourself

What to read first: Start at actuatorpd.py (30 lines) to see the IdentifiedActuator.compute() method — it is compact and the conceptual core. How hard is it to follow: Moderate. The code is clean and well-structured. Anyone already familiar with IsaacLab or the legged-gym codebase will read it in under an hour. For a newcomer, understanding the manager hierarchy (ObservationManager, RewardManager, EventManager) adds 2–3 hours of ramp-up. Run verdict

Cannot run on CPU only. Isaac Sim 4.0.0 requires an NVIDIA GPU (RTX class recommended; minimum RTX 2070 per NVIDIA's docs) and a Linux system with the Omniverse runtime. The physics simulation (PhysX) runs on GPU. Training 30,000 iterations with hundreds of parallel environments requires a GPU with at least 8–12 GB VRAM.

A trained policy checkpoint (a small MLP: 3 hidden layers, 512/256/128 units) could in principle run inference on CPU — the forward pass is trivial. However, the repo provides no pretrained checkpoint and no standalone deployment script; running inference requires the full Isaac Lab/Isaac Sim stack, which mandates a GPU. In practice: GPU + NVIDIA-capable Linux machine required for any use of this codebase.

Robust Ladder Climbing with a Quadrupedal Robot

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A quadruped's paws cannot grip ladder rungs — they slip no matter how much friction is on the floor. A clever sim-to-real policy transfer cannot solve a physical impossibility.

The approach. Redesign the feet. Add hooked aluminum end-effectors shaped to passively grip a rung under both compression and tension. Now the physics makes climbing possible. Train the policy in simulation using those new feet, with a teacher policy that sees perfect information and a student that learns from noisy hardware sensors alone, so the policy never overfits to simulator clarity.

What's unique. Rather than fight physics with bigger randomization, this paper uses morphology co-design — the robot body is designed to make the task feasible. The teacher-student distillation ensures the student policy trains under the same sensing constraints as real hardware will have.

IROS 2025 · 4 citations · sim-to-real transfer (domain randomization + teacher-student distillation) with hardware morphology co-design

What it does

A reinforcement learning controller is trained entirely in simulation to make an ANYmal D quadruped climb industrial ladders at angles from 70° to 90°, then deployed zero-shot on real hardware with a 90% success rate. The robot is given custom hooked aluminum end-effectors that let it grip ladder rungs under both compression and tension, a morphology change that makes the policy tractable. This is a sim-to-real transfer paper: the key engineering is a teacher policy with privileged simulation state, distilled into a student that runs on noisy real-sensor input alone, combined with aggressive domain randomization over ladder geometry and perturbations.

The method, in detail

Hardware platform and end-effector

The base robot is the ANYmal D (ANYbotics AG), a 12-DOF torque-controlled quadruped. A custom hooked end-effector replaces the standard foot: an aluminum core with a 3D-printed shell, a concave contoured hook opening of radius 2.75 cm, and a passive geometry that keeps the foot stable on a rung across a wide angle range without active control. This design lets the robot exert both compressive (pushing into the rung) and tensile (pulling against the rung) forces, which is what makes a ladder-gripping gait physically achievable.

Policy inputs

The student policy (what runs on hardware) receives:

  • Proprioception: gravity vector, current joint positions and velocities, joint position-tracking error history at t=0, t=-20 ms, t=-40 ms, last 8 IMU readings (linear acceleration + angular velocity at 400 Hz), a standing-mode flag that activates within 15 cm of goal.
  • Ladder state (measured directly on hardware, provided as scalars): ladder presence flag, inclination angle, width, rung radius, inter-rung spacing, rung count.
  • Ladder pose: position and yaw of the bottom rung expressed in the robot's base frame (provided by a motion-capture system during hardware experiments).
  • Recurrent belief encoder: the student uses a recurrent (GRU) belief encoder trained to reconstruct privileged information from its observation history.

The teacher policy additionally receives: privileged foot contact forces, friction coefficients, external forces/torques, base mass variation, foot airtime, full 2 x 1 m height scan at 10 cm resolution, and noiseless versions of all ladder parameters.

Policy architecture

  • Teacher: a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that encodes the 8-step IMU history, concatenates with all proprioceptive + privileged observations, and outputs 12 joint position targets at 50 Hz.
  • Student: a recurrent belief encoder (GRU) maps the sequence of noisy observations to a latent belief state; this is concatenated with current observations and passed through an MLP to produce joint position targets at 50 Hz.
  • A proportional-derivative (PD) controller on the joints runs at 400 Hz and converts position targets to torques.

Training setup — teacher first, then distillation

Training uses the LeggedGym framework (Isaac Gym physics simulator) with 4,096 parallel environments on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090.

Teacher training (15,000 epochs, ~3 days):

  • Two terrain types generated randomly in each environment: rough ground (boxes, slopes) and ladders with variable geometry.
  • Curriculum: agents start on easier ladders (broader, flatter rungs, lower angles) and the curriculum adapts automatically — as success rate rises, ladder steepness and rung difficulty increase.
  • Batch: 48 rollout steps per update.
  • Key reward terms:

- Position tracking to waypoint goal: weight 3.0 (dominant term)

- Heading alignment: weight 0.5

- Base motion smoothness: weight 0.2

- Joint regularization: -0.001 per joint per step

- Foot slip penalty: -0.25

- Limb collision: -0.1; base collision: -1.0

- Action rate smoothing: -0.01

- Orientation penalty (terrain-dependent)

  • A dense position-tracking reward (with a goal generated continuously along the ladder) replaces the sparse terminal reward, which the authors found critical for improving success rates.

Student distillation (5,000 epochs, ~1.5 days):

  • Student observes the same environments but only has access to noisy, hardware-available observations.
  • Supervised loss: student actions match teacher actions (behavior cloning objective).
  • Recurrent belief encoder is trained simultaneously to reconstruct privileged state from observation history.

Domain randomization

  • Ladder dimensions: length 1-3 m, width 1.0-1.25 m, inter-rung spacing 27.5-32.5 cm, rung radius varied down to 2.5 cm (elliptic cylinders).
  • Inclination: progressed via curriculum from flat to near-vertical (90°).
  • Physics: base mass variation, foot friction coefficients, external random forces and torques applied to the base and feet, random velocity offsets.
  • Initial state: 50% of environments initialized from the final state of the previous episode (with randomized velocities), providing diversity.

The trick that makes it transfer

Three things working together: (1) the hooked foot hardware removes the physical impossibility of rung grip on a smooth paw; (2) the actuator model used in simulation is the ANYmal D's learned actuator network (a small neural net that maps desired torques to realized torques, capturing real joint dynamics including friction and delays), reducing the sim-to-real gap in low-level torque response; (3) teacher-student distillation means the deployed policy was never exposed to privileged state — it learned from the start to operate under the same observation noise as real hardware.

HOVER: Versatile Neural Whole-Body Controller for Humanoid Robots

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A humanoid robot needs to walk, reach, and carry things all from one control system. Training separate policies for each task and trying to blend them at runtime breaks precision — walking pulls energy from arm control and vice versa. One unified policy trained for all of them tends to sacrifice both.

The approach. Teach the robot full-body motion patterns from human motion-capture data (AMASS), using motion imitation as the common language. Then distill that broad teacher into a student that accepts three different kinds of commands at once — velocity commands for walking, joint-angle commands for precise arm work, and keypoint-position commands for whole-body reaching — with random masking during training so the policy learns which command channels are live.

What's unique. Motion imitation provides a unified representation that naturally handles the coupling between legs and arms. The key trick is mode masking: the student learns which commands are enabled by seeing the mask in the observation itself, so the same network can switch between walking, reaching, and carrying mid-episode without retraining.

ICRA 2025 · 12 citations · sub-approach: one controller for many tasks or gaits (multi-mode whole-body control via teacher-student policy distillation)

What it does

HOVER solves a specific problem: humanoid robots need different control "languages" depending on the task — navigation wants a velocity command, tabletop manipulation wants joint-angle commands, loco-manipulation wants hand/foot position commands — and prior work trained a separate policy for each. HOVER instead uses full-body kinematic motion imitation as a single shared abstraction layer: a motion-tracking teacher policy, trained on retargeted human motion-capture data, is then distilled into one student policy that accepts all three command modes simultaneously, with missing modes masked out at random during training. The result is a single neural network that can switch command modes mid-episode and runs on a real Unitree H1 humanoid.

Sub-approach: multi-task / multi-mode — one controller for many tasks and gaits, unified through motion imitation as a common backbone rather than domain randomization as the primary bridge.

The method, in detail

Inputs

The *teacher (oracle) policy* receives privileged simulator state: per-body rigid-body positions, orientations, linear and angular velocities, plus the reference motion frame (next pose from the AMASS retargeted clip). The *student policy* receives only quantities available on real hardware: joint positions, joint velocities, base angular velocity from an IMU, a gravity vector (also IMU-derived), and 25 timesteps of action history (rolling window). Both policies output 19 target joint positions, which are converted to torques on the robot by PD controllers.

Command space and mode masking

Three command modes are defined:

  • Root tracking: desired base linear velocity, height, roll/pitch/yaw (navigation-style joystick commands)
  • Kinematic position tracking: 3D world-space coordinates of body keypoints (head, hands, feet)
  • Local joint-angle tracking: 19 target motor angles (used for upper-body manipulation)

At the start of each simulated episode, a binary "mode mask" (drawn from Bernoulli(0.5)) selects which command dimensions are active. Within the active dimensions a second "sparsity mask" further randomly zeroes out individual keypoints. This forces the policy to handle any subset of commands, not just pre-defined combinations.

Teacher policy (oracle)

Trained with PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) inside Isaac Gym. The network is a three-layer MLP with hidden sizes [512, 256, 128]. The goal state fed to the teacher is the difference between the reference motion pose and the current robot pose: reference orientation minus current orientation (using geodesic subtraction on SO(3)), reference position minus current position, plus reference velocity and angular-velocity differences, and the absolute reference pose. The teacher has full access to the simulated state and motion-reference.

Motion data comes from AMASS (a large human motion-capture database). AMASS clips are retargeted to the 19-DOF Unitree H1 via SMPL body-model fitting and gradient-descent keypoint matching. Retargeting the full dataset on a 32-core CPU takes approximately 4 days.

Reward function

Weighted sum of task and regularization terms. Key positive rewards:

  • DoF position tracking: weight 32.0
  • Body keypoint position: weight 80.0
  • Root velocity: weight 100.0
  • Root rotation: weight 20.0

Negative (penalty) terms: joint velocity (−4e-3), joint acceleration (−1.1e-5), action rate (−3 lower / −0.625 upper body), torque limit violations (−2), joint position limit violations (−125), episode termination (−250), foot stumble and bad contact orientation.

Student distillation (DAgger)

The student is trained with DAgger (Dataset Aggregation), a behavioral-cloning variant that avoids compounding errors. During student rollouts in simulation, the corresponding oracle state is computed from the same trajectory, and the teacher policy is queried to produce reference actions. The student minimizes the L2 loss between its own output and the teacher's reference actions. The student never sees privileged state; it infers what it needs from its 25-step proprioception history and the (masked) command vector.

Sim-to-real transfer

Domain randomization is applied to physical simulation parameters (friction, mass, center-of-mass position, motor strength) following the OmniH2O methodology. Actions are 19 PD-controller setpoints; the PD gains are tuned on the real H1. No explicit actuator network is used — the gap is closed via randomization and the DAgger distillation step, which exposes the student to its own error distribution rather than the teacher's.

Hardware

Unitree H1 humanoid, 19 DOF, approximately 51.5 kg, 1.8 m. Experiments include 20 retargeted AMASS standing/locomotion sequences, mode-switch transitions mid-walk, and Apple Vision Pro teleoperation (upper-body joint commands) with occlusion-robust masking active.

AllGaits: Learning All Quadruped Gaits and Transitions

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Prior work trains quadrupeds to trot — the safe, symmetric gait used in most RL work. But real animals switch between gaits depending on speed and terrain; the trot is optimal only in a narrow range. A policy trained to always trot misses efficiency gains and can't adapt.

The approach. Put a Central Pattern Generator (a set of coupled oscillators that produce rhythmic motion without external timing) between the neural network and the joints. The policy doesn't output joint angles directly; instead it modulates the frequency and amplitude of the oscillators. A discrete gait-selection matrix (swapped at runtime) sets the phase relationships between legs to realize walk, trot, pace, gallop, or any of nine gaits.

What's unique. The CPG structure enforces rhythmic, anatomically-plausible motion for free — no reward for gait synchrony needed. One policy can generate all nine gaits by swapping a single coupling matrix. The paper measures Cost of Transport per gait on real hardware, showing trot isn't actually the most efficient across the full speed range.

ICRA 2025 · 4 citations · sub-approach: one controller for many tasks or gaits (single policy over all 9 canonical quadruped gaits via CPG modulation)

What it does

AllGaits trains a single deep RL policy that can produce all nine standard quadruped animal gaits — lateral sequence walk, amble, trot, pace, bound, pronk, canter, transverse gallop, and rotary gallop — and transition between any pair of them instantaneously, at any running speed, on command. The key architectural choice is placing a biologically-inspired Central Pattern Generator (a CPG: a system of coupled Hopf-style oscillators that produces rhythmic output without external rhythm input) between the neural network and the joints; the policy does not output joint positions or torques directly but instead modulates the CPG's per-leg amplitude and frequency, while the gait identity (which of the nine gaits is active) is set by swapping a pre-defined phase-offset coupling matrix. Using this decomposition, the authors show that the trot — the near-universal choice in prior RL locomotion work — is not actually the most energy-efficient gait across the full speed range, and that the framework lets them measure Cost of Transport (COT, energy per unit distance) for each gait empirically on a real robot.

Sub-approach: multi-task / multi-gait — one controller that generates all gaits, with gait identity as a runtime-switchable discrete input rather than a reward-shaped emergent behavior.

The method, in detail

Observation inputs

At each 10 ms timestep the policy receives:

  • Velocity command: desired forward base-frame speed v*_x (scalar, resampled from [0.2, 3] m/s every 5 s during training)
  • Body state from onboard IMU and joint encoders: orientation (roll/pitch/yaw), base linear velocity, base angular velocity
  • Joint state: 12 joint positions and 12 joint velocities (3 joints × 4 legs)
  • Foot contact booleans: 4 binary signals from foot contact sensors
  • Previous action: the last RL action issued (efference copy)
  • CPG state (efference copy of oscillator state): {ri, rdoti, thetai, thetadoti} for each of the 4 oscillators — amplitude, amplitude rate, phase, phase rate

Notably, the gait coupling matrix, the gait type label, and the pattern formation parameters (body height h, foot offset xoff, ground clearance gc, ground penetration g_p) are NOT part of the observation. The policy never sees which gait is requested; the gait identity enters only through the coupling matrix applied to the CPG, so the same policy weights handle all nine gaits.

Architecture

An MLP with the same hyperparameters as CPG-RL (Bellegarda & Ijspeert, RA-L 2022): a three-layer network with hidden sizes [256, 160, 128] and ELU activations (from the CPG-RL paper; AllGaits inherits this without modification). Output is 8 real-valued numbers, clipped to action ranges.

Action space

Action vector a = [mu, omega] in R^8:

  • mu_i (intrinsic amplitude) in [1, 2] for each of 4 legs
  • omega_i (intrinsic frequency) in [0, 8] Hz for each of 4 legs

These 8 outputs are the only interface between the neural policy and the locomotion mechanism.

CPG: Rhythm Generation Layer

The CPG consists of four coupled oscillators (one per leg), integrated at 1 kHz. Each oscillator i has:

Amplitude dynamics:

rdoubledoti = a * (a/4 * (mui - ri) - rdot_i)

Phase dynamics:

thetadoti = omegai + sumj [ rj * wij * sin(thetaj - thetai - phi_ij) ]

where:

  • ri, thetai: oscillator amplitude and phase (state)
  • mui, omegai: intrinsic amplitude and frequency (policy outputs)
  • w_ij = 10 (fixed strong coupling weight for all i,j pairs)
  • phi_ij: phase bias between oscillators i and j — this encodes the gait identity
  • a: positive constant controlling convergence speed

The nine gaits are defined by nine distinct 4x4 phase-offset matrices Phi, where each entry phi_ij specifies the desired phase lead/lag between legs i and j. For example, trot has front-right and hind-left in phase, front-left and hind-right in phase, and the two pairs 180 degrees apart. Gait identity is switched by swapping Phi, which can be done mid-episode without resetting the oscillator states; the oscillators smoothly converge to the new gait's phase relationship due to the coupling terms.

Pattern Formation Layer

The CPG oscillator output (ri, thetai) is mapped to a desired foot position in the body frame:

x-position: xifoot = xoff - dstep * (ri - 1) * cos(thetai)

z-position: zifoot = -h + gc * sin(thetai) if sin(theta_i) > 0 (swing phase)

zifoot = -h + gp * sin(thetai) otherwise (stance phase)

where:

  • h in [0.18, 0.35] m: body height (re-sampled at each env reset)
  • x_off in [-0.08, 0.03] m: foot offset along body x-axis relative to hip
  • g_c in [0.02, 0.12] m: maximum swing ground clearance
  • g_p in [0, 0.015] m: maximum stance ground penetration (allows compliance)
  • d_step: maximum step length (fixed by user at deployment)

These (x, z) desired foot positions are then converted to joint angles via inverse kinematics of the Go1's 3-DOF leg, and the resulting target joint angles are tracked by a PD controller running at 1 kHz.

Reward function

Four reward terms only; no gait-style rewards:

| Term | Formula | Weight |

|---|---|---|

| Velocity tracking | exp(-||v*x - vx||^2 / 0.25) | 3 * dt |

| Lateral/vertical velocity penalty | -||v_yz||^2 | 2 * dt |

| Angular velocity penalty | -||omega_xyz||^2 | 0.1 * dt |

| Power consumption | -|tau . q_dot| | 0.001 * dt |

where dt = 0.01 s (policy runs at 100 Hz). The key claim: no reward terms beyond these four are needed. No foot-contact symmetry reward, no gait-cycle reward, no joint-torque smoothness term — the CPG structure itself enforces rhythmic, gait-appropriate motion.

Training setup

  • Simulator: Isaac Gym (NVIDIA) with PhysX physics engine
  • Robot: Unitree Go1 quadruped (12 DOF, ~12 kg)
  • 4,096 environments in parallel on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GB VRAM)
  • Algorithm: PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization), inherited from leggedgym / rslrl framework
  • Terrain: flat terrain only during training
  • Episode length: 20 s
  • Gait coupling matrix: resampled from the 9-gait set every 3 s (uniform random)
  • Velocity command: resampled every 5 s from [0.2, 3] m/s
  • External push perturbation: 0.5 m/s magnitude, random direction, every 15 s per episode
  • Episode reset: triggered if base or thigh contacts ground

Domain randomization

| Parameter | Low | High | Unit |

|---|---|---|---|

| Per-link mass | 70% | 130% of nominal | |

| Added base mass | 0 | 5 | kg |

| Coefficient of friction | 0.3 | 1.0 | |

| PD gain Kp | 30 | 100 | |

| PD gain Kd | 0.5 | 2.0 | |

Gains are re-sampled at every environment reset. No actuator net, no system identification, and no adaptive domain randomization (curriculum); these five scalar ranges plus the flat-terrain training are the full sim-to-real gap-closing strategy.

Sim-to-real transfer and hardware deployment

The trained policy runs zero-shot on the real Unitree Go1 at 100 Hz. The oscillator integration runs at 1 kHz onboard. The paper does not describe the onboard compute or communication stack (no mention of whether it runs on the Go1's internal Raspberry Pi/Jetson or an external laptop), which is typical for Go1 locomotion papers — the Go1 runs its PD controller onboard at 1 kHz and accepts high-level joint-position commands over Ethernet. Hardware experiments demonstrate all nine gaits and leg-failure robustness (one or two legs disabled mid-run). Cost of Transport is measured on hardware by integrating motor current × voltage.

MOVE: Multi-Skill Omnidirectional Legged Locomotion With Limited View in 3D Environments

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Low-cost quadrupeds have one front-facing depth camera, so when the robot needs to walk sideways or backward, the camera sees nothing. A voxel map or recurrent memory of past observations could help, but that's expensive. How does one policy handle multiple movement skills (climbing, jumping, crawling, stairs) when vision only covers the forward direction?

The approach. Train a neural network with two separate encoding branches that never share weights (pseudo-siamese). One branch sees only the front camera and proprioceptive history (what will be available on hardware). The other branch sees a full 360-degree cube map of depth images plus privileged terrain info (only in simulation). Train them to produce consistent representations via contrastive learning — so the forward-camera branch learns to infer what the backward camera would see.

What's unique. Contrastive training forces the limited-camera branch to "hallucinate" the full scene from proprioception alone, so at inference the robot can move in any direction using learned mental models of unseen terrain. One policy handles five distinct skills (climbing, jumping, stairs, crawling, confined spaces) by learning where vision applies and where proprioception must take over.

*ICRA 2025 · 3 citations · sub-approach: sim-to-real transfer, multi-task/multi-skill, perception-proprioception fusion*

What it does

MOVE solves a specific, practical problem: low-cost quadrupeds carry only a single narrow front-facing depth camera, so when the robot needs to move sideways or backward, it is visually blind in the direction of travel. Rather than building an expensive voxel map to patch this coverage gap, MOVE trains a single end-to-end neural policy that fuses depth images with proprioceptive history via a learned fusion called a pseudo-siamese network (two asymmetric encoder branches that share no weights), and switches its internal reliance between vision and proprioception depending on whether the movement direction falls inside the camera's field of view. The result is one policy that handles extreme climbing (0.7 m height), long jumps (0.9 m), stair traversal, and crawling through confined spaces (0.2 m gap) — with zero-shot transfer to a real DEEP Robotics Lite3 quadruped.

Sub-approach: train in sim then transfer (domain randomization + teacher-student privileged training), combined with one controller for many tasks/gaits.

The method, in detail

Robot and sensors. The hardware is a DEEP Robotics Lite3 quadruped (small, 610 × 370 × 445 mm), with a single Intel RealSense D435i depth camera mounted front-facing. Onboard compute is a RK3588 ARM processor (no GPU at inference).

Policy inputs. Proprioception is a 10-step history of: body angular velocity (ω), gravity direction in body frame (g), xy velocity command and yaw-rate command, 12 joint angles (θ), 12 joint angular velocities (θ̇), and previous action (a{t-1}). Exteroception at inference is one front-facing depth image (dt) from the D435i.

Privileged training observations. In simulation only, the policy also receives a cube map — five egocentric depth images facing front, upward, downward, left, and right — plus sparse depth samples around the four foot positions (l_t^foot). These give the training-time "teacher" information that is not available on real hardware.

Pseudo-Siamese Network (PS-Net) architecture.

The two branches process different observation sets but do not share weights (this is what makes it pseudo-siamese rather than true siamese):

  • *Standard encoder* (used at both train and inference time): an MLP processes the proprioceptive history stack; a CNN extracts visual features from the single front-facing depth image; a Transformer encoder with self-attention fuses those two modalities; a GRU preserves memory across timesteps. Outputs a latent vector for policy and value heads.
  • *Surroundings encoder* (training time only): a separate MLP projects privileged proprioception; a separate CNN processes the privileged cube-map depth images; a cross-attention block (queries from proprioception, keys/values from the cube-map CNN) fuses them. An asymmetric skip connection passes visual embedding only through a skip path, which prevents representation collapse when proprioceptive inputs are nearly identical between branches.

Learning objective. Training jointly minimizes two losses on top of RL:

  • *Reconstruction loss*: a VAE KL divergence term on the latent code, plus MSE predicting the next proprioceptive observation, MSE predicting body linear velocity, and MSE reconstructing the front-facing depth image.
  • *Contrastive loss*: negative cosine similarity between normalized feature predictions from the standard encoder and the surroundings encoder, with a stop-gradient on the surroundings-encoder side to prevent collapse (similar in spirit to BYOL/SimSiam).

Combined: L = Lreconstruction + Lcontrast.

The point of this combination: the standard encoder (which sees only the front camera) is trained to predict representations consistent with the surroundings encoder (which sees the full cube map). At inference, the standard encoder has learned to mentally fill in what the unseen directions probably look like.

RL algorithm and training setup. PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) with an asymmetric actor-critic — the critic receives privileged observations during training; the actor (policy) does not (this is sometimes called "student-teacher" or "privileged critic" training). Training runs 1,000 parallel robots in Isaac Gym with a terrain curriculum that progressively increases obstacle height, jump distance, stair riser height, and crawl-space clearance. Reward function is described as adapted from prior quadruped locomotion work (not detailed with explicit weights in the paper); it includes tracking of velocity commands, a smoothness penalty on joint torques and actions, and terrain-appropriate task bonuses.

Skill representation. No discrete skill switch is exposed to the user. Skills emerge from the curriculum: the same policy is rewarded for completing progressively harder terrain types simultaneously. The exteroception vs. proprioception weighting is implicit — when the movement command direction matches the camera's field of view, the visual branch provides strong features; when moving laterally or backward, visual features degrade and the contrastive training ensures the proprioceptive branch takes over smoothly. No gating network, no mode flag.

Sim-to-real gap closure. Domain randomization on depth images injects patterned noise matching the D435i's sensor artifacts. Camera z-axis offset is randomized up to 0.2 m. The terrain curriculum is the main adaptation mechanism. No actuator network or separate system identification is used — the Lite3 is a relatively backdrivable platform where this gap is manageable. Transfer is zero-shot.

Cycloidal Quasi-Direct Drive Actuator Designs with Learning-Based Torque Estimation for Legged Robotics

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Cycloidal gearboxes pack high torque density but produce torque ripple, friction losses, and nonlinear dynamics that simple motor models can't capture. A simulator using an idealized motor model will generate controllers that work in sim but fail on real hardware with real cycloidal drives, and domain randomization alone won't bridge a systematic model mismatch.

The approach. Train a small neural network (a stacked GRU) on real hardware test-rig data to learn the actual input-output behavior of the cycloidal actuator — joint position error, velocity, and acceleration go in; predicted output torque comes out. Embed this learned actuator model in the simulator, replacing the idealized physics.

What's unique. Rather than randomizing the simulator, this paper learns what the real actuator actually does and puts that model in the simulator. The GRU correctly predicts the 17.6 Hz torque ripple frequency, which a simpler MLP baseline completely misses. A trained RL policy then experiences the true actuator dynamics during training.

What it does

Legged robot reinforcement learning controllers trained in simulation fail to transfer cleanly to real hardware in part because the simulator cannot faithfully reproduce the nonlinear torque behavior of physical actuators — specifically cycloidal gear drives, which produce torque ripple, friction-dependent losses, and high-frequency oscillations that no simple spring-damper model captures. This paper addresses that problem from two sides: it designs a new custom actuator (the C-QDD, Cycloidal Quasi-Direct Drive) that packs high torque density into a lightweight form, and trains a small neural network — called an Actuator Network — to model the actuator's complex dynamics from real measurement data. The learned model can substitute for the idealized physics in simulation, shrinking the gap between what the RL controller experiences in training and what it encounters on the physical robot.

The sub-approach is hardware-model: closing the sim-to-real gap by building a learned model of the real actuator's dynamics, rather than domain randomization or policy distillation. The underlying idea traces to the actuator-net concept from MIT Cheetah / ETH Zurich legged robotics work; this paper extends it to cycloidal drives, which are more complex than the conventional planetary or direct-drive transmissions for which actuator nets were originally developed.

The method, in detail

Hardware design. The C-QDD uses a 10:1 cycloidal reduction stage — a type of epicyclic gear where a lobe-shaped disk (the cycloidal disk) rolls against pins, producing very high contact ratio and therefore high torque density with low backlash (7.03 ± 1.3 arcminutes measured). The drive is precision-machined from 4140 alloy steel. Peak torque on the bench is 89.9 Nm at the output shaft; torque density reaches 64.21 Nm/kg. Efficiency is 82.3% and torque control bandwidth is 34.3 Hz at a 5 Nm setpoint. Cycloidal drives are mechanically robust but introduce torque ripple — a periodic variation in output torque at the frequency of gear tooth engagement — and manufacturing-tolerance-sensitive friction that is hard to model analytically.

Data collection. An inverted pendulum test rig drives the actuator through a range of sinusoidal trajectories: frequencies 0.3–1.5 Hz, amplitudes 0.5–1.5 rad, with two pendulum masses (1.14 and 2.28 kg) at arm positions 0.25–0.6 m. Wall-contact sequences are added to capture hybrid dynamics (the joint hitting a hard stop). Ground-truth torque is recorded alongside joint position, velocity (numerically differentiated), and acceleration (second derivative). The raw data is pre-processed into CSV files with columns Error (position error from setpoint), Velocity, Acceleration, and Torque.

Actuator Network architecture. The model is a stacked GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit — a recurrent neural network variant that maintains a hidden state summarizing recent history without the vanishing-gradient problem of vanilla RNNs). Specifically:

  • Input: a window of the last 30 timesteps, each timestep providing three features — position error, velocity, and acceleration (the PVA-GRU variant; the simpler PV-GRU drops acceleration). Inputs are normalized to [-1, 1] using fixed physical bounds: ±6.28 rad error, ±250 rad/s velocity, ±2500 rad/s² acceleration.
  • Architecture: 4-layer stacked GRU, hidden size 30, with 0.03 dropout between layers.
  • Output: a single scalar, the predicted output torque (normalized to ±150 Nm range, then denormalized).
  • The last-timestep GRU hidden state is passed through a linear layer to produce the scalar output.
  • Total parameters: small (hidden_size=30, 4 layers, 3 inputs → roughly 15k–20k parameters).

Training setup. Supervised regression on real hardware data. Loss is mean squared error (MSE) between predicted and measured torque. Optimizer is AdamW, initial learning rate 1e-4, with a One Cycle LR scheduler (cosine annealing, warmup over first 12% of steps, final LR 5e-6), batch size 64, up to 3500 epochs with early stopping (patience 400). Logging via Weights & Biases. Training data is split from the inverted pendulum recordings; a separate held-out evaluation set is used for reporting RMSE.

The key performance numbers. PVA-GRU achieves RMSE 0.97 Nm and variance 0.92 Nm on the test set, versus 1.71 Nm RMSE for a tuned MLP baseline — a 43.5% improvement in RMSE and 68.2% in variance. Critically, the GRU correctly tracks the 17.6 Hz torque ripple frequency (ground truth 17.2 Hz), which the MLP misses entirely. CPU inference time for the GRU is 1.49 microseconds per step — fast enough to run inside any real-time control loop.

Sim-to-real integration. The trained actuator network is dropped into the physics simulator in place of the default idealized actuator model. When the RL controller issues a torque command, the network predicts what the real drive would actually output given the recent joint-state history. This means the RL policy trains against the true nonlinear characteristics of the cycloidal drive — torque ripple, friction, bandwidth limits — rather than against a perfect model. The paper demonstrates this reduces the sim-to-real gap and enables better RL controller performance on physical hardware.

What is not in the paper. The paper focuses on the actuator and estimation framework. The RL controller itself (Isaac Gym, reward functions, domain randomization schedule) is referenced as the downstream consumer but not detailed in this work.

Legged Robot State Estimation with Invariant Extended Kalman Filter Using Neural Measurement Network

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Knowing where the robot is (position drift) and whether each foot is in contact with the ground is hard. Standard Kalman filters assume clean contact kinematics, but on gravel, ice, or soft mats those assumptions break. A robot blind to contact ends up predicting the wrong forces and wrong walking dynamics.

The approach. Train a small neural network (a GRU-MLP) in simulation to predict the robot's base velocity and per-foot contact probability from proprioception alone (IMU and joint encoders). Feed those neural predictions into a mathematical Kalman filter (specifically an invariant EKF, which respects 3D rotation geometry) that fuses them with leg kinematics.

What's unique. The neural network handles nonlinear perception in messy terrain; the Kalman filter enforces physical consistency and handles uncertainty geometrically. Together they reduce position drift by 61.8% over a model-only baseline, and the network deliberately undertrained (early stopping) generalizes better than longer training, a counterintuitive finding.

ICRA 2025 · 4 citations · Sub-approach: state estimation (neural-augmented proprioceptive filter)

What it does

The paper addresses a core infrastructure problem for legged robots: knowing where you are and whether each foot is on the ground, using only on-board sensors (no camera, no GPS). Traditional model-based filters assume clean contact kinematics — they break badly on debris, slippery ice, or soft mats. This work trains a neural network (the Neural Measurement Network, NMN) in simulation to estimate the robot's base linear velocity and per-foot contact probability; those two outputs are then injected as probabilistic measurements into an invariant extended Kalman filter (InEKF), which handles the geometry of 3D rotation correctly by operating on a matrix Lie group. The result is a proprioceptive state estimator that never touches real-world data during training, closes the sim-to-real gap through targeted regularization, and cuts position drift by 61.8% over a model-only baseline across four challenging outdoor terrain types.

Sub-approach: state estimation — specifically a hybrid neural/model-based filter trained in sim and deployed zero-shot on hardware. It is not a locomotion policy; it feeds pose estimates to whatever downstream controller is already running.

The method, in detail

Inputs to the NMN

The NMN receives only proprioceptive signals — nothing external:

  • IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit): roll-pitch gravity vector, angular velocity ω, linear acceleration a, at 166 Hz.
  • Joint encoders: 12 joint positions and 12 joint velocities at 500 Hz.
  • Previous joint targets (the commands sent one step earlier).
  • Velocity command from the high-level user input.

No contact force/torque sensors are used. Everything comes from encoders and the IMU.

NMN architecture

The NMN is a GRU-MLP — a gated recurrent unit (GRU, which is a recurrent cell that maintains hidden state across time) with hidden dimension 128, followed by a two-layer MLP with dimensions [256, 128]. The architecture mirrors the locomotion policy that was already trained to walk the robot: in fact, the NMN reuses the frozen hidden state of that policy's GRU as a shared temporal feature, so it has access to the same compressed locomotion context without extra compute. The NMN then predicts two things per timestep:

  • Base linear velocity v_t^b (3-vector in body frame).
  • Foot contact probability c_t per leg (scalar in [0,1]).

Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF)

A standard EKF (extended Kalman filter) represents uncertainty as a Gaussian and linearizes nonlinear dynamics. An invariant EKF additionally respects the Lie group structure of 3D rotation — the state lives in SE_{N+2}(3), a product of rotation matrices and vectors, and the filter's error is defined in a coordinate-free way that gives better convergence guarantees on curved spaces than naively applying a Cartesian EKF to rotation matrices.

State vector: rotation matrix Rt, linear velocity vt, body position pt, contact foot positions dt (one per leg in contact), IMU bias terms — all encoded together as a block matrix in SE_{N+2}(3).

Propagation step (prediction): IMU gyroscope and accelerometer drive the state forward between timesteps using standard inertial navigation equations.

Correction steps (measurement update): two sources of measurement:

  • Leg kinematics: forward kinematics (using joint encoder positions) gives predicted foot positions in world frame. These are right-invariant observations, meaning the update respects the Lie group structure.
  • NMN velocity: the estimated body velocity vt^b from the NMN is formulated as a right-invariant observation Yt = Xt^{-1} b + Vt, where Xt is the current state, b is a target, and Vt is Gaussian noise. This is injected only when the robot speed exceeds 0.1 m/s (to avoid stationary drift) and after a 10 Hz low-pass filter.

Contact gating: the NMN's contact probability c_t is thresholded at 0.5 to gate whether the leg kinematics measurement for each leg is included in the correction step. Leg measurements are only used when the foot is confidently in contact — this prevents foot-slip from corrupting the filter on slippery or soft terrain. Contact outputs are also low-pass filtered at 40 Hz.

Training setup

The NMN is trained entirely in RaiSim (a rigid-body physics simulator developed at ETH/KAIST), running 400 parallel environments at 4 kHz with the locomotion policy already deployed (policy parameters are frozen throughout NMN training).

Terrain randomization for data collection: hills with amplitude U(0.2, 1.4 m), steps/stairs U(0.02, 0.18 m), friction coefficient U(0.4, 1.2), occasional slip patches (1% probability, friction U(0.3, 0.4)). The robot is commanded random velocity targets, generating diverse walking data.

Loss: binary cross-entropy for contact probability; MSE for velocity against the simulated ground-truth. Optimizer: Adam, learning rate 5e-4, 32 epochs per batch of 400-step trajectories.

The specific tricks that make it transfer to real hardware

Three regularization choices matter; the paper tests them ablatively:

  • Smoothness loss: an auxiliary term penalizing first- and second-order changes in the predicted velocity over consecutive timesteps. This discourages the network from predicting jerky, high-frequency velocity estimates that the real hardware cannot track. Applied to velocity only (contact is inherently discontinuous).
  • Early stopping at 200 iterations: the NMN is deliberately undertrained. Longer training overfits to the simulated contact model and degrades real-world accuracy. This is the dominant regularizer for velocity estimation.
  • Counterintuitive finding on domain randomization: applying standard domain randomization (randomizing robot parameters, sensor noise) *hurts* velocity prediction once the simulator already has an accurate robot model (Raibo's dynamics are tightly characterized). The paper explicitly recommends against domain randomization when the simulator is high-fidelity — a meaningful result for practitioners.

Physically-Consistent Parameter Identification of Robots in Contact

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Knowing a robot's actual mass, center of mass, and inertia tensor is essential for accurate simulation. But modern robots lack foot force sensors, so you can't directly measure the ground-reaction forces needed to identify these parameters. CAD values are often wrong, and black-box neural networks that ignore physics can produce negative masses or impossible inertias.

The approach. Project the dynamics equations into the mathematical subspace where ground-reaction forces have no effect (the null space of the contact Jacobian). In that subspace, the inertial parameters become solvable from joint torques alone. Formulate parameter recovery as a convex optimization problem (a semidefinite program with physical constraints: mass must be positive, inertia must satisfy triangle inequalities).

What's unique. This paper recovers physically-realizable inertial parameters from motor current and joint encoders, with no force sensors and no machine learning. The result outperforms both CAD values and black-box MLPs, using far fewer samples, because the physics constraints prevent impossible solutions.

ICRA 2025 · 3 citations · sub-approach: hardware model (inertial parameter identification, sim-to-real gap via physics-based parameter ID)

What it does

Modern quadruped and humanoid robots lack force/torque sensors at the feet, yet knowing a robot's exact inertial parameters (link masses, centers of mass, moments of inertia) is essential for accurate simulation and model-based control. This paper sidesteps the missing contact-force measurements by projecting the whole-body dynamics equations into the mathematical subspace (null space) where contact forces have no effect, then recovering the inertial parameters by solving a convex optimization problem (a semidefinite program expressed as linear matrix inequalities, or LMIs) that enforces physical realizability — parameters must correspond to a real mass distribution with positive mass, a center of mass inside the link, and a positive-definite inertia tensor satisfying the triangle inequality. The result is validated on a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped; identified parameters outperform both CAD values and a deep-MLP black-box baseline in torque prediction accuracy, with far fewer samples needed to generalize.

Sub-approach: hardware model — closing the sim-to-real gap by identifying real hardware inertial parameters from joint torque/current measurements alone, without any external force sensing.

The method, in detail

Problem setup. A floating-base robot (6 unactuated base degrees of freedom + n joints) obeys the rigid-body dynamics equation M(q)v̇ + C(q,v)v + g(q) = Sᵀτ + Jcᵀλ, where q are generalized positions, v velocities, τ joint torques, λ contact forces, Jc the contact Jacobian, and S selects actuated joints. All terms left of the contact wrench are linear in the stacked inertial parameters φ (a 10-vector per link: mass, mass×CoM offset, and six independent inertia-tensor entries).

Null-space projection. Define the orthogonal projection P = I − Jc† Jc (where Jc† is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse of the contact Jacobian). Premultiplying the dynamics equation by P annihilates the contact-force term Jcᵀλ entirely, because P·Jcᵀ = 0. The projected equation Y(q,v,v̇)φ = Pτ_meas is now a linear system in φ alone, solvable without any contact-force measurement. The regressor matrix Y is assembled using Pinocchio (a fast C++/Python rigid-body dynamics library).

LMI formulation. Rather than solving the linear least squares unconstrained (which can yield physically impossible parameters, e.g. negative mass), the problem is cast as a semidefinite program (SDP):

minimize ‖Yφ − Pτ‖² + γ‖φ − φ₀‖²

subject to three LMI constraints:

(1) The pseudo-inertia matrix Σ (a 4×4 symmetric positive-semidefinite matrix encoding mass, first moment, and second moment) is PSD: Σ ≽ 0.

(2) The center of mass lies within a bounding ellipsoid for the link (first-moment constraint).

(3) The triangle inequality on principal moments of inertia holds (second-moment constraint).

This is solved via CVXPY with the MOSEK interior-point SDP backend. The regularization term pulls parameters toward nominal values φ₀ (e.g. from CAD or manufacturer specs), weighted by γ = 10⁻².

Neural-network baseline. For comparison the authors train a 5-hidden-layer MLP (2048 units each, LeakyReLU + Tanh) end-to-end on the same dataset to predict joint torques directly from (q, v, v̇). This is a black-box actuator/dynamics model — it captures the same dynamics but without physical constraints.

Inputs. At each time step: joint angles q ∈ ℝⁿ, joint velocities v ∈ ℝⁿ (from encoder differencing), joint accelerations v̇ ∈ ℝⁿ (finite-difference + low-pass filter), joint torques τ ∈ ℝⁿ (from motor current). All signals filtered with a 5th-order zero-phase Butterworth low-pass filter at 10 Hz cutoff. The contact Jacobian Jc is computed from forward kinematics given the known contact schedule.

Data collection on Spot. 10,000 samples at 100 Hz. Motions used: base wobbling (all feet planted), squatting, forward-backward walking, side-to-side walking. The method is purely offline; no online adaptation occurs.

Sim-to-real transfer mechanism. There is no RL policy here. The contribution is upstream of control: once the identified φ parameters are loaded into a simulator (e.g., PyBullet, which was used for validation experiments), the sim dynamics match the real robot more closely, shrinking the sim-to-real gap for any downstream learning-based controller. The paper also tests the method in simulation (PyBullet) first, where ground-truth parameters are known, then on real Spot hardware.

Results. On hardware, the LMI method achieves 61.8% average reduction in Absolute Trajectory Error in position versus the model-based baseline. On sample efficiency, the LMI method generalizes well with as few as 100 samples; the MLP needs ~10,000.

The core idea in code — the mode masking / whole-body controller

The single mechanism that lets one student accept many command types is a per-environment binary mask that is (a) sampled at episode reset and (b) multiplied element-wise into the command vector *and* appended to the observation. It lives in two files.

1. The command-mode dictionary — neuralwbc/isaaclabwrapper/.../neuralwbcenvcfg_h1.py. Each "mode" names which mask elements are *enabled*, using regex over element names. This is where "walking vs. carrying vs. reaching" is actually defined:


DISTILL_MASK_MODES_ALL = {
    "exbody":   {"upper_body": [".*torso_joint.*", ".*shoulder.*joint.*", ".*elbow.*joint.*"],
                 "lower_body": ["root.*"]},
    "humanplus":{"upper_body": [...joints...],
                 "lower_body": [".*hip.*joint.*", ".*knee.*joint.*", ".*ankle.*joint.*", "root.*"]},
    "h2o":      {"upper_body": [".*shoulder.*link.*", ".*elbow.*link.*", ".*hand.*link.*"],
                 "lower_body": [".*ankle.*link.*"]},
    "omnih2o":  {"upper_body": [".*hand.*link.*", ".*head.*link.*"]},
}

Note the two *kinds* of tracked element the regexes hit: *link* names select kinematic keypoint commands (world-space body positions), while *joint* and root* select joint-angle and root-velocity/height commands. The default config trains an OmniH2O specialist (distillmaskmodes = {"omnih2o": DISTILLMASKMODES_ALL["omnih2o"]}); passing the full dict trains the generalist HOVER controller.

2. The mask sampler — neuralwbc/core/neuralwbc/core/mask.py::createmask(). Element ordering is fixed by createmaskelementnames() as [per-bodylocalpos] + [per-jointjointpos] + [7 root refs]. Each env randomly draws one mode, then optionally a Bernoulli(0.5) *sparsity* dropout on top:


selected_mode_indices = torch.randint(0, len(modes), (num_envs,), device=device)
for mode_idx, mode in enumerate(modes):
    mode_env_indices = (selected_mode_indices == mode_idx).nonzero(as_tuple=True)[0]
    if mode_env_indices.numel() > 0:
        mask[mode_env_indices.unsqueeze(1), mode_to_indices[mode]] = True
# Last we do the sparsity masking.
if enable_sparsity_randomization:
    mask = torch.bernoulli(mask * 0.5).bool()   # each enabled bit survives w.p. 0.5

This is invoked once per reset in neuralwbc/isaaclabwrapper/.../neuralwbcenv.py::reset_mask(), gated on distillation mode (the teacher trains without masking):


if self.cfg.mode.is_distill_mode() or self.cfg.mode.is_distill_test_mode():
    self._mask[env_ids] = mask.create_mask(
        mask_element_names=self.mask_element_names,
        mask_modes=self.cfg.distill_mask_modes,
        enable_sparsity_randomization=self.cfg.distill_mask_sparsity_randomization_enabled,
        device=self.device, num_envs=len(env_ids))

3. Applying the mask to the command — neuralwbc/core/neuralwbc/core/observations/studentobservations.py::computedistilledimitationobservations(). The three command sub-vectors are built independently, then each is zeroed by its slice of the mask. Because each body has 3 (x/y/z) command values but only 1 mask bit, the kinematic slice is repeat_interleave-d:


kinematic_command = compute_kinematic_command(...)   # (N, num_bodies*3) local link deltas
joint_command     = compute_joint_command(...)       # (N, num_joints)   ref - current joint pos
root_command      = compute_root_command(...)         # (N, 7) lin-vel(3) + roll/pitch/Δyaw(3) + height(1)

num_bodies = kinematic_command.shape[1] // 3
kinematic_mask = mask[:, :num_bodies].repeat_interleave(3, dim=-1)
kinematic_command *= kinematic_mask
joint_mask = mask[:, num_bodies : num_bodies + num_joints]; joint_command *= joint_mask
root_mask  = mask[:, num_bodies + num_joints :];            root_command  *= root_mask

observations = torch.cat([kinematic_command, joint_command, root_command, mask], dim=1)

The crucial detail is the last term: the mask itself is concatenated into the observation. The in-code comment states why — otherwise "the network has no way to determine the difference between a target state that is enabled but set to 0, or a target state that is 0 because it is disabled." So the student learns "which channels are live" as an explicit input, which is exactly what lets it switch command modes mid-episode. The rest of the student observation (computedistilledrobotstateobservation) is only hardware-available quantities: jointpos, jointvel, localbaseangvel, projectedgravity, plus last_actions and a 25-step history.

Teacher → student distillation

Distillation is DAgger, implemented in neuralwbc/studentpolicy/neuralwbc/studentpolicy/studentpolicytrainer.py. The teacher is the frozen PPO ActorCritic wrapped as NeuralWBCTeacherPolicy (defined in trainstudentpolicy.py), exposing act() (deterministic label) and act_rollout() (sampled, for data collection).

Who drives the rollout (the DAgger schedule). produceactions switches from teacher-driven to student-driven rollouts after studentrolloutiteration iterations (default 0, i.e. student drives immediately) — this is what exposes the student to *its own* error distribution rather than the teacher's:


def _produce_actions(self, obs):
    if self._iterations + self.start_iteration >= self._cfg.student_rollout_iteration:
        action = self._student.act(obs["student_policy"])   # student on-policy rollout
    else:
        action = self._teacher.act_rollout(obs["teacher_policy"])
    return action

Labeling. At every collected step, the teacher is queried on the *privileged* observation to produce the supervision target, and the full Slice (student obs, teacher obs, ground-truth action, applied action) is pushed to Storage:


gt_actions = self._get_ground_truth_actions(obs_dict["teacher_policy"])   # = self._teacher.act(...)
slice = Slice(policy_observations=obs_dict["teacher_policy"],
              student_observations=obs_dict["student_policy"],
              ground_truth_actions=gt_actions, applied_actions=actions)
self._storage.add(slice=slice)

The loss — updatestudentnetwork(). It is a plain per-sample L2 (Euclidean) norm between the student's deterministic output and the teacher's labeled action, averaged over the batch, scaled by daggercoefficient (default 1.0). There is no value/critic loss and no RL reward in the student stage — pure supervised imitation:


pred_action = self._student.act_inference(student_observations_batch)   # MLP mean, no sampling
kin_loss = torch.norm(pred_action - ground_truth_actions_batch, dim=-1).mean()  # RMSE
kin_loss = kin_loss * self._cfg.dagger_coefficient
self._optimizer.zero_grad(); kin_loss.backward()
nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(self._student.parameters(), self._cfg.max_grad_norm)
self._optimizer.step()

The student network (policy.py::StudentPolicy) is a bare MLP ([512,256,128], ELU) with a *non-trainable* action-noise std (self.std.requiresgrad = False) — the noise only matters for the on-policy act() sampling during rollout; actinference() returns the mean and is what the loss is computed against. Optimizer is Adam at learningrate = 5e-4. Contrast with the teacher, whose training is standard PPO from thirdparty/rslrl (OnPolicyRunner + ActorCritic) driven by the reward config in isaaclab_wrapper/rewards/.

The core idea in code — a robot built to be simulated accurately

The paper's claim is that the *hardware* was designed so its *simulation model* is faithful enough that light randomization suffices. In code that faithfulness lives in one small class: a DC-motor model with an explicit, hardware-identified friction term bolted on. That is IdentifiedActuator in actuators/actuator_pd.py — the entire class is 43 lines:


class IdentifiedActuator(DCMotor):
    cfg: IdentifiedActuatorCfg

    def __init__(self, cfg: IdentifiedActuatorCfg, *args, **kwargs):
        super().__init__(cfg, *args, **kwargs)
        self.friction_static = self._parse_joint_parameter(self.cfg.friction_static, 0.)
        self.activation_vel = self._parse_joint_parameter(self.cfg.activation_vel, torch.inf)
        self.friction_dynamic = self._parse_joint_parameter(self.cfg.friction_dynamic, 0.)

    def compute(self, control_action, joint_pos, joint_vel):
        # call the base method
        control_action = super().compute(control_action, joint_pos, joint_vel)
        # apply friction model on the torque
        control_action.joint_efforts = control_action.joint_efforts - (self.friction_static * torch.tanh(
            joint_vel / self.activation_vel) + self.friction_dynamic * joint_vel)
        self.applied_effort = control_action.joint_efforts
        control_action.joint_positions = None
        control_action.joint_velocities = None
        return control_action

The base DCMotor.compute() produces a PD torque and clips it to the motor's speed-dependent torque envelope. This subclass then subtracts a friction torque:


τ_applied = τ_DCMotor − ( friction_static · tanh(vel / activation_vel) + friction_dynamic · vel )

frictionstatic · tanh(vel/activationvel) is a smoothed Coulomb (stiction) term — near zero velocity it ramps smoothly through zero instead of a discontinuous sign flip; frictiondynamic · vel is ordinary viscous friction. The config that carries these three numbers is IdentifiedActuatorCfg in actuatorcfg.py, which just extends Isaac Lab's DCMotorCfg with three fields (frictionstatic [N·m], activationvel [rad/s], friction_dynamic [N·m·s/rad]), all MISSING until set per joint group.

Where do the numbers come from? Two of the repo's three README FAQ entries are about exactly this:

> Q: Where is the joint armature from? A: From CAD system.

> Q: Why does the friction of each joint so large? A: The motor we used has large cogging torque, we include it in the friction of the actuator model.

So the model deliberately folds motor cogging torque into the friction term rather than hoping domain randomization papers over it — this is the mechanism, not randomization.

The per-joint values are set in assets/berkeley_humanoid.py, one IdentifiedActuatorCfg per joint group, matched to joints by regex. Knee (KFE) example:


BERKELEY_HUMANOID_KFE_ACTUATOR_CFG = IdentifiedActuatorCfg(
    joint_names_expr=[".*KFE"],
    effort_limit=30.0,
    velocity_limit=14,
    saturation_effort=560,
    stiffness={".*": 15.0},
    damping={".*": 1.5},
    armature={".*": 1.5e-4 * 81},   # reflected inertia = rotor inertia × gear ratio²... here × 81 (the gear ratio)
    friction_static=0.8,            # knee: highest stiction of any joint
    activation_vel=0.1,
    friction_dynamic=0.02,
)

Five configs cover the 12 joints: HXX (hip rotation + abduction, .*HR/.*HAA, effort 20 N·m, friction_static 0.3), HFE (hip flex, effort 30, static 0.3), KFE (knee, effort 30, static 0.8), FFE (foot flex, effort 20, static 1.0), FAA (foot abduction, effort 5, static 0.1). Note the friction is not uniform — it was identified per group (KFE 0.8, FFE 1.0 vs HR/HAA 0.3), and every armature is written literally as <CAD rotor inertia> * 81 to make the 81:1 gear reduction explicit in the source.

These are wired into the robot in BERKELEYHUMANOIDCFG (an ArticulationCfg) at the bottom of the same file: it spawns Robots/berkeleyhumanoid.usd, sets the initial standing pose (pos=(0.0, 0.0, 0.515) and a full 12-joint jointpos default), enables self-collisions, uses 4 position / 0 velocity solver iterations, and maps actuators={"hxx":..., "hfe":..., "kfe":..., "ffe":..., "faa":...}. softjointposlimitfactor=0.95 keeps the policy off the hard joint stops.

Domain randomization — how mild it really is

All randomization is the RandomizationCfg class in velocityenvcfg.py. The striking thing when you read it against a typical legged-gym config is how narrow the ranges are and how much is startup-only (sampled once when the env spawns, never re-rolled during an episode). Verbatim:


@configclass
class RandomizationCfg:
    # startup
    physics_material = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_rigid_body_material, mode="startup",
        params={"asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", body_names=".*"),
                "static_friction_range": (0.2, 1.25),
                "dynamic_friction_range": (0.2, 1.25),
                "restitution_range": (0.0, 0.1), "num_buckets": 64})

    scale_all_link_masses = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_rigid_body_mass, mode="startup",
        params={"asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", body_names=".*"),
                "mass_distribution_params": (0.9, 1.1), "operation": "scale"})   # ±10%

    add_base_mass = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_rigid_body_mass, mode="startup",
        params={"asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", body_names="torso"),
                "mass_distribution_params": (-1.0, 1.0), "operation": "add"})    # ±1 kg torso

    scale_all_joint_armature = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_joint_parameters, mode="startup",
        params={"asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", joint_names=[".*"]),
                "armature_distribution_params": (1.0, 1.05), "operation": "scale"})  # +0–5% only

    add_all_joint_default_pos = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_joint_default_pos, mode="startup",
        params={"asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", joint_names=[".*"]),
                "pos_distribution_params": (-0.05, 0.05), "operation": "add"})    # ±0.05 rad calibration error

    scale_all_joint_friction_model = EventTerm(func=mdp.randomize_joint_friction_model, mode="startup",
        params={"asset_cfg": SceneEntityCfg("robot", joint_names=[".*"]),
                "friction_distribution_params": (0.9, 1.1), "operation": "scale"})  # ±10% on the friction model

How mild it is, term by term:

| What's randomized | Range | Mode | Note |

|---|---|---|---|

| Body friction (static & dynamic) | 0.2 – 1.25 | startup | 64 buckets |

| All link masses | ×0.9 – 1.1 (±10%) | startup | |

| Torso mass offset | −1 – +1 kg | startup | |

| Joint armature | ×1.0 – 1.05 (0 to +5%) | startup | one-sided, tiny |

| Joint default position | ±0.05 rad | startup | models calibration error |

| Friction-model coeffs | ×0.9 – 1.1 (±10%) | startup | scales the identified frictionstatic/frictiondynamic |

| Base push (velocity) | ±0.5 m/s in x,y | interval, every 10–15 s | see below |

What is not here is as telling as what is: no per-step observation-parameter noise beyond the fixed Unoise on observations, no PD-gain randomization, no latency/action-delay randomization, no big ±30% mass swings, no random restitution beyond [0.0, 0.1]. Armature is nudged by at most 5% in one direction. The friction *model* is only scaled ±10% — because the model itself is the identified quantity, the randomization is just acknowledging measurement uncertainty, not compensating for a missing model.

The one dynamic disturbance is pushrobot (mode "interval", intervalranges=(10.0, 15.0), velocityrange ±0.5 m/s). But even that is ramped in gently by a curriculum in CurriculumCfg, which only *starts* pushing hard at step 1500 and ramps the ceiling to 3.0 m/s:


push_force_levels = CurrTerm(func=mdp.modify_push_force,
    params={"term_name": "push_robot", "max_velocity": [3.0, 3.0],
            "interval": 200 * 24, "starting_step": 1500 * 24})
command_vel = CurrTerm(func=mdp.modify_command_velocity,
    params={"term_name": "track_lin_vel_xy_exp", "max_velocity": [-1.5, 3.0],
            "interval": 200 * 24, "starting_step": 5000 * 24})

The custom event functions randomizejointdefaultpos and randomizejointfrictionmodel (in mdp/events.py) exist precisely because Isaac Lab has no built-in event for "the identified friction model" — the latter loops over asset.actuators.values() and scales each actuator's frictionstatic / frictiondynamic tensors in place. So the friction model isn't just added to the physics; it's a first-class thing the randomization system knows how to perturb.

For the observation side, corruption noise (ObservationsCfg.PolicyCfg) is likewise modest: base lin-vel ±0.1, ang-vel ±0.2, gravity ±0.05, hip pos ±0.03, knee pos ±0.05, foot-flex pos ±0.08, joint vel ±1.5. And in the -Play- configs, enablecorruption=False and pushrobot=None — i.e. deployment/eval runs with randomization turned off entirely.

(One code-vs-paper note: roughenvcfg.py sets self.observations.policy.heightscan = None and self.scene.heightscanner = None, confirming the rough policy is blind to terrain — the height scan is defined in the base scene but explicitly removed for Berkeley Humanoid. The rough config also enables the two "optional" penalties: flatorientationl2.weight = -0.5 and dofposlimits.weight = -1.0. The reward set additionally includes a jointdeviationknee L1 term at -0.01 and a feetairtime window of [0.2, 0.5] s keyed to the .*faa foot bodies.)
