Flying, underwater, and outdoor field robots

The big picture — connecting the dots

Every robot does three things: take in the world, decide what to do, and move its body to execute that decision. In a clean lab, each step is hard; in the real world — the air, underwater, or rough outdoor ground — each step breaks in a different way, and often all at once. A drone flying through trees faces wind gusts and gaps barely wider than itself, with a camera that sees tiny objects it has never trained on and batteries that last minutes. Underwater robots battle murky water that eats light and signal alike, with almost no training data to learn from. Outdoor ground robots traverse terrain that shifts under them while carrying gear and constraints from the physical world that planning algorithms never see. The hard core problem uniting all three: how do you make robots that work reliably in the field — fast enough, safe enough, robust enough to the real world's mess — when the conditions that break your system are the ones you cannot control or even fully predict?

Five moves the field is making:

  • ① Replace hand-coded physics models with learned ones. The old way to fly a drone is to write out the physics by hand — rotors spin, creating lift, wind pushes the body — and this works until reality diverges. Learning Quadrotor Control from Visual Features Using Differentiable Simulation and Robust Self-Reconfiguration for Fault-Tolerant Control of Modular Aerial Robot Systems replace parts of the hand-coded model with learned policies trained in minutes instead of hours, so the drone adapts when conditions shift.
  • ② Measure and cancel disturbances in real time. Even a well-trained controller fails when something pushes on the robot unexpectedly. ProxFly: Robust Control for Close Proximity Quadcopter Flight Via Residual Reinforcement Learning sits on top of the main controller and sends corrective commands based only on the drone's own motion, with no wireless signals between drones. NDOB-Based Control of a UAV with Delta-Arm Considering Manipulator Dynamics measures the unwanted coupling forces between an arm and drone body and cancels them in real time.
  • ③ Fly or navigate through clutter using only cameras. A drone that can only carry a small camera — no heavy laser scanner — must navigate dense spaces safely at high speed. Vision Transformers for End-to-End Vision-Based Quadrotor Obstacle Avoidance maps camera images directly to flight commands by learning relationships across the whole image at once. UAV-DETR: Efficient End-to-End Object Detection for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Imagery fixes a different failure: standard detectors miss small objects in drone photos because they were designed for ground-level images.
  • ④ Fit through tight gaps, and perch to save power. Two physical realities shape flight in the wild: openings barely wider than the drone, and batteries that drain in minutes. Whole-Body Control Through Narrow Gaps from Pixels to Action learns precise body-tilting to squeeze through gaps a naive controller would clip. And because the only way to stretch a mission is to stop flying, several papers design graspers that latch onto a branch or wire with no power once closed, letting the drone perch and rest.
  • ⑤ Add safety layers that watch what the planner decides and block unsafe moves. A learned or planned trajectory can still steer the robot into a wall, so safety filters read the motion in real time and block commands that would cause a collision. A Control Barrier Function for Safe Navigation with Online Gaussian Splatting Maps runs this filter 15 times per second directly on a map made of hundreds of thousands of tiny 3D blobs that are still being built in real time.

The throughline: field robotics is not about discovering new theory — it is the work of taking methods that work in the lab (learned control, sensor fusion, obstacle avoidance, path planning) and making them survive contact with the real world. The specific challenge depends on the setting: air robots fight physics (wind, gravity, rotor coupling) and perception (fast-moving, distant, never-seen-before objects); underwater robots fight the medium itself (light attenuation, zero training data, fragile electronics) and the gaps in sensing; ground robots fight terrain that shifts and sensor noise that planning ignores. Still open: a controller trained for calm air fails in high wind; a grasper designed for tree branches may not handle pipes or wires; a detection network tuned for daytime drone imagery breaks at night. Most work evaluates on narrow benchmarks with code not yet released. Whether these single-problem solutions compose into a full perception and control stack that stays robust across the mess of the real world remains unsettled.

The problem — in depth

Every robot must do three things: take in what is around it, decide what to do, and move its body to do it. In a lab, that is hard enough. Put the robot in the air, underwater, or out in a field, and each of those three steps gets harder in its own way — often all at once.

A drone flying through a forest faces wind that shoves it off course and gaps between trees that are barely wider than itself. Its camera picks up small objects at odd angles and distances that a network trained on regular photos has never seen. Its battery lasts minutes, not hours. GPS stops working inside buildings or under thick canopy. And if the drone hits anything, it falls.

Underwater robots face murky water that eats light and sonar signal both. The water itself pushes back. Depth makes electronics fragile. There are almost no large training sets of underwater images, so learning-based methods that depend on massive data fall apart before they start.

Outdoor ground robots deal with rough, unpredictable terrain. They carry science gear, or pull cables, or inspect pipelines, and must do all of that with limited power in places where no one can quickly come to fix them.

All of these settings connect to the same core problem every robot faces: how do you take in a world that is unclear and changing, figure out what to do, move a real body, and do it fast, cheap, safe, and reliably enough to be useful? In the lab you can control some of these — good lighting, flat floors, reliable networks. In the field, none of that holds. The field is the hardest test of whether robots actually work.

The 293 papers in this topic do not propose a single new idea. They adapt what already works — learned control, safety filters, path planning, object detection — to the specific ways each environment breaks it. That is what makes field robotics a distinct research area: not a new theory, but the disciplined work of making existing methods survive contact with the real world.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Learning to fly without hand-coded rules

In plain termsA hand-written physics model works in the lab but breaks the moment real wind shows up or the drone's weight changes. Instead, let the drone learn how to fly by actually flying and watching what happens — it adapts instantly when conditions shift.
The sub-problemHand-coded physics models work in the lab but fail when reality diverges — a gust of wind, unexpected payload, or a rotor slightly off specs breaks the model entirely. The only way to adapt is to let the controller learn from real data instead.
Probabilistic motion model — DroneDiffusion. Learns the drone's motion as a range of possible futures rather than a single fixed prediction, staying useful across different payloads and variable wind.
Differentiable simulation — Learning Quadrotor Control from Visual Features Using Differentiable Simulation. A simulator computes exact gradients of flight outcome back through the physics, cutting training from hours to minutes by letting the policy follow gradients directly.
Visual navigation with differentiable rendering — GRaD-Nav. Combines differentiable physics with photorealistic image rendering to teach a drone to fly through gates using only a camera, proven on real hardware.

When the real world does not match your model, let the controller learn from data instead of being written by hand.

Designing controllers that resist outside forces

In plain termsYou can't predict every gust of wind or bump. So add a second brain that watches the drone's actual motion in real time and sends quick fixes to cancel out whatever is pushing it off course — like a copilot who only fixes problems, not plans the route.
The sub-problemEven a well-trained controller fails when something pushes on the drone unexpectedly. Rather than try to predict all disturbances, measure them in real time and cancel them out on the fly.
Residual learning for proximity — ProxFly: Robust Control for Close Proximity Quadcopter Flight Via Residual Reinforcement Learning (ICRA). When multiple drones fly very close together, rotor downwash disrupts neighbors; an add-on module trained through trial and error sits on top of the main controller, sending corrective commands based only on the drone's own motion, no wireless needed.
Disturbance observer for arm coupling — NDOB-Based Control of a UAV with Delta-Arm Considering Manipulator Dynamics (ICRA). Measures and cancels unwanted coupling forces between a mounted arm and the drone body in real time.
Model predictive control with state estimation — DR-MPC. For pipeline inspection, a planner looks ahead while a state estimator measures wind forces and feeds corrections back to the plan before drift occurs.

A main controller for calm conditions, plus a real-time watchdog that estimates disturbances and adjusts on the fly.

Flying through clutter using only cameras

In plain termsA camera sees the whole scene at once and reacts faster than a laser scanner can spin — but it's blind to tiny distant objects until it learns to see the whole picture, not just edges. Teach the camera-brain to spot the entire clutter pattern and steer away before the drone gets close.
The sub-problemA lightweight camera-only drone must navigate dense clutter safely and fast, but camera images are noisy, small objects vanish easily, and the time between seeing an obstacle and hitting it is measured in milliseconds.
Vision transformers for end-to-end control — Vision Transformers for End-to-End Vision-Based Quadrotor Obstacle Avoidance (ICRA). Splits camera frames into patches and learns relationships across the whole image at once, mapping directly to flight commands; learns in simulation, transfers to unseen environments without retraining.
Direct control from point clouds — Flying on Point Clouds with Reinforcement Learning. Simplifies 3D laser scans enough to run at 50 commands per second, enabling forest flight with no separate path planner.
Whole-body tilting through tight gaps — Whole-Body Control Through Narrow Gaps from Pixels to Action (ICRA). The drone must tilt its entire body to squeeze through a gap barely wider than itself; algorithm is initialized from positions along known good paths so it can actually discover solutions to this hard problem.
Drone-specific object detection — UAV-DETR: Efficient End-to-End Object Detection for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Imagery (IROS). Standard detectors miss small objects in drone photos because they were designed for ground-level images; combines features at many scales, keeps fine detail during downsampling, and connects features across processing paths to fix this for aerial imagery.

A camera-only drone that learns to navigate clutter at high speed by splitting the problem into control, detection, and precise body alignment.

Saving power and extending mission time

In plain termsHovering in the air is exhausting — the battery dies in minutes. But if the drone lands and grips a branch like a hawk on a perch, it burns almost no power, just holds on. The trick is a claw that locks tight without electricity.
The sub-problemDrones drain batteries in minutes, capping how far they can go, how long they can monitor, and what work they can do. The only way to extend a mission is to stop flying and perch somewhere, using power only to grip, not to stay aloft.
Single-motor three-fingered claw — Adaptive Perching and Grasping by Aerial Robot with Light-Weight and High Grip-Force Tendon-Driven Three-Fingered Hand Using Single Actuator. A three-fingered gripper driven by one motor can grab objects from any approach angle and lock tight with no power once latched.
Tendon-based branch perching — Tendon-driven Grasper Design for Aerial Robot Perching on Tree Branches. Grips a tree branch and locks in place using tendons with no power needed to stay on; a camera finds and guides the drone to land on good perches.
Wire-crawling solar recharging — RaccoonBot. A wire-monitoring robot grips without power (cannot fall), then crawls along the wire to find sunlit spots and recharge on the move.

Engineering solutions to the power-budget problem that no amount of smarter learning can fully solve — the physics of flight costs energy, but perching costs almost none.

Building safer flight with real-time safety filters

In plain termsThe main brain can make a bad call and aim straight at a wall. So put a separate safety layer that watches every move and blocks commands that would crash, like a copilot who can always override an unsafe command — no questions asked.
The sub-problemA learned or planned trajectory can still steer the drone into a wall or obstacle. You need a separate layer that watches all motion in real time and blocks unsafe commands, regardless of what the main controller says.
Safety filter over live 3D blobs — A Control Barrier Function for Safe Navigation with Online Gaussian Splatting Maps (ICRA). Runs a control barrier function at 15 times per second directly on a map made of hundreds of thousands of tiny 3D blobs (Gaussian splats) that are still being built in real time.
Motor thrust safety for manipulation — Safety-Critical Control for Aerial Physical Interaction in Uncertain Environment. Applies the same idea to a flying robot arm: the safety filter tracks estimation errors and adjusts arm motion just enough to keep all motor thrusts inside safe bounds.

An independent safety filter that watches the planned motion and blocks commands that would cause a collision, running fast enough to keep up with real-time perception.

Where it stands

Drone flight in controlled or semi-controlled conditions — racing, gate navigation, indoor obstacle avoidance — is now good enough to demonstrate reliably in research settings. Perching and battery extension hardware has been proven on real robots. What remains hard is generalizing across conditions: a controller trained for calm air fails in high wind; a grasper designed for tree branches may not handle pipes or wires; a detection network tuned for daytime drone imagery breaks at night. Underwater and outdoor ground field robots are still limited mainly by the lack of large training datasets and the difficulty of testing in the field at scale. The core challenge has not changed — surviving the gap between what was trained and what the real world delivers.

Paper by paper — the method

UAV-DETR: Efficient End-to-End Object Detection for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle Imagery

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Standard object detectors trained on ground-level photos fail on drone footage because small distant objects get blurred and lost during the detection process, leaving drones unable to see obstacles or targets that are visible to human eyes in the same image.

The approach. Enhance the detection backbone by combining features at multiple scales using frequency-domain tricks, keep sharp detail during downsampling, and connect features across different processing paths so nothing gets lost.

What's unique. Frequency-domain feature fusion preserves small-object detail where standard methods lose it — the 3-4% boost comes from keeping information alive during downsampling, not from richer training data.

What it solves

A standard object detection method designed for regular photos doesn't work well on drone pictures because small objects get lost and detail gets blurred.

How it works

They enhance the standard method with three upgrades: a module that combines features at many sizes using frequency tricks, a downsampling step that keeps details sharp, and a module that glues features together across different paths.

Why it matters

A DETR-based UAV object detection framework with frequency-domain feature enhancement that improves AP by 3.1% and AP50 by 4.2% over baseline on the VisDrone dataset.

Vision Transformers for End-to-End Vision-Based Quadrotor Obstacle Avoidance

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Flying a drone fast through clutter using only a camera requires reacting in milliseconds; convolutional networks learn to navigate but fail at high speeds because they process the image as a fixed grid and miss spatial relationships that matter when timing is tight.

The approach. Use vision transformers — which split the image into patches and learn which patches relate to each other — to map camera frames directly to flight commands, letting the network discover the relationships that matter at speed.

What's unique. Transformers generalize to unseen environments without retraining where CNNs overfit; they also work better at 7 m/s flight speed because they learn global relationships first, not local features.

What it solves

Flying a drone fast through cluttered spaces using only a camera feed requires a controller that reacts quickly and still works in places it has never seen, which standard convolutional camera-to-control networks fail to do at high speeds.

How it works

They train vision transformer models (ViT) — which split images into patches and learn relationships across the whole image at once — to map depth camera images directly to flight commands, testing speeds up to 7 m/s in simulation.

Why it matters

First use of vision transformers for end-to-end vision-based quadrotor control, showing ViT outperforms CNNs at high speeds and generalizes better to unseen environments, with recurrence further reducing energy cost.

Whole-Body Control Through Narrow Gaps from Pixels to Action

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Squeezing a drone through a gap barely wider than itself requires precise body tilting, which is a hard geometry problem; learning this from camera images alone is nearly impossible because there are so few successful paths the learning algorithm can stumble into.

The approach. Train two policies: first on point clouds (which are easier to reason about geometrically), then transfer that knowledge to camera images by having the camera policy learn to mimic the point-cloud policy during flight; seed the learning with trajectories from a physics planner so it starts near solutions.

What's unique. Combining model-based initialization (a planner-generated seed trajectory) with RL-to-pixel distillation avoids hand-crafted reward shaping that's normally needed for such a constrained problem.

What it solves

Flying a drone through a gap just barely wider than itself requires precise tilting of the whole body, and learning this from raw camera images is very hard because there are very few successful paths for the learning algorithm to discover.

How it works

They first train a control policy using 3D point-cloud inputs (which are easier to learn from), then transfer that knowledge to a camera-image policy by having the camera policy mimic the point-cloud policy's behavior during flight; to help the learner find good paths, it is reset to positions along trajectories generated by a physics-based planner.

Why it matters

Combines model-based trajectory initialization with RL-to-pixel distillation to learn whole-body narrow-gap traversal entirely from pixels without hand-crafted reward shaping.

NDOB-Based Control of a UAV with Delta-Arm Considering Manipulator Dynamics

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Mounting a robot arm on a flying drone creates bidirectional chaos: the arm's motion shakes the drone off course, and the drone's motion jerks the arm, so their controllers fight each other and both fail.

The approach. A disturbance observer watches the coupling forces between arm and drone in real time and sends corrective commands to cancel them; a high-pass filter simultaneously amplifies fast arm commands so the arm can move quickly without the main controller fighting it.

What's unique. Coupling-aware compensation lets a delta-arm achieve millimeter-level end-effector accuracy despite being mounted on a flying platform — decoupling the two control loops post-hoc rather than redesigning the hardware.

What it solves

When a robot arm is mounted on a flying drone, the arm's motion shakes the drone and the drone's motion disturbs the arm, making precise arm positioning very difficult.

How it works

A disturbance observer (NDOB) measures and cancels the unwanted coupling forces between the arm and drone in real time, while a high-pass filter separately boosts fast arm commands so the arm can move quickly without fighting the drone controller.

Why it matters

Achieves millimeter-level end-effector accuracy on a fully autonomous delta-arm aerial manipulator by jointly compensating for dynamic coupling and selectively amplifying fast manipulator motions.

Learning Quadrotor Control from Visual Features Using Differentiable Simulation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Training a drone to fly from camera images using standard trial-and-error learning requires billions of simulated flight steps and hours of compute because the learning algorithm explores blindly, with no information about which direction improves the policy.

The approach. A physics simulator that computes exact gradients lets the learning algorithm flow backward through the physics equations to see how each pixel change affects the final flight outcome, then follow those gradients directly to better policies.

What's unique. Differentiable simulation collapses training from hours to minutes by giving the learner a gradient signal through the physics — it's the difference between blind search and mathematically guided optimization.

What it solves

Training a drone to fly using only camera images with standard trial-and-error (reinforcement learning) requires enormous amounts of simulated flight time and takes hours or days.

How it works

A differentiable simulator lets the learning algorithm compute exact gradients of the flight outcome back through the physics model to the policy. The drone learns to fly from raw visual features in minutes instead of hours by following these gradients directly.

Why it matters

Demonstrates that differentiable simulation with a surrogate model trains vision-based quadrotor control orders of magnitude faster than model-free RL, bridging from state-based (seconds) to vision-based (minutes) learning.

Robust Self-Reconfiguration for Fault-Tolerant Control of Modular Aerial Robot Systems

The big idea — first principles

The problem. When a modular drone loses a rotor or motor, it needs to physically rearrange itself — detach failed modules and reattach working ones — but the wrong order can leave it in a shape that it cannot fly or control at all.

The approach. A planner works out a step-by-step disassembly and reassembly order, checking after each step whether the craft in its current configuration is still controllable before proceeding to the next step.

What's unique. Maximizing controllability margin at every intermediate stage prevents getting stuck in an uncontrollable configuration — safety first, rather than optimizing for speed and hoping.

What it solves

When a drone made of detachable modules loses a rotor or a module, it needs to rearrange itself, but the steps taken during that rearrangement can put the craft in a shape it cannot actually fly in.

How it works

A planning algorithm works out a step-by-step order to detach and reattach modules, checking after each step whether the craft in its current shape can still be controlled and flown stably before moving on.

Why it matters

A reconfiguration planning algorithm that maximizes controllability margin at every intermediate stage, enabling fault-tolerant self-reconfiguration with improved trajectory tracking and fewer assembly steps.

ProxFly: Robust Control for Close Proximity Quadcopter Flight Via Residual Reinforcement Learning

The big idea — first principles

The problem. When drones fly within arm's length of each other, the downwash air from one's rotors destabilizes the others, a disturbance that standard controllers cannot predict or handle because it depends on positions the controller never trained on.

The approach. Add a small learned module on top of a conventional controller that watches only the drone's own motion (no radio between drones) and sends corrective impulses to cancel the destabilizing effects of nearby rotorwash.

What's unique. Residual RL avoids exploration from scratch by building on a working controller as a baseline — the add-on learns only the correction signal, not the entire flight policy.

What it solves

When multiple drones fly very close together, the downward air pushed by one drone's rotors disrupts the others, causing instability that standard controllers cannot correct.

How it works

A small add-on AI module trained through trial and error (residual reinforcement learning) sits on top of a conventional controller and sends small corrective commands based only on the drone's own motion data, with no wireless messages needed between drones.

Why it matters

ProxFly enables stable close-proximity multi-quadcopter flight and midair docking by residual RL that skips exploration from scratch via baseline controller guidance.

Multi-UAV Formation Control with Static and Dynamic Obstacle Avoidance via Reinforcement Learning

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Training a flock of drones to fly in formation while dodging obstacles fails to transfer from simulation to real flight because the simulator cannot capture all the wind and sensor errors the real world will throw at it.

The approach. Use curriculum learning (progressively harder scenarios) plus automated reward search to figure out which objectives matter most, then train an attention-based encoder that learns to focus on the right parts of the scene.

What's unique. Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer works because the curriculum and attention mechanism together let the policy learn what's actually important, not what the simulator prioritizes.

What it solves

A flock of drones has to fly in tight formation while dodging both fixed walls and moving objects, and the training setup does not transfer to real flight.

How it works

The system first learns which rewards matter most for flying straight, avoiding hits, and staying together. Then it practices on harder and harder scenarios. A smart encoder learns to pay attention to the important parts of the scene.

Why it matters

Automated reward search combined with curriculum learning and attention-based encoding achieves zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for multi-UAV formation flight that outperforms planning and RL baselines in collision-free rates.

A Control Barrier Function for Safe Navigation with Online Gaussian Splatting Maps

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A learned planner or controller can steer a robot into a wall even though it's being built live in real time; you need a safety filter that watches the planned motion and blocks unsafe commands, but doing this on a map made of hundreds of thousands of live 3D blobs is computationally brutal.

The approach. A control barrier function reads the Gaussian splat map directly and computes which motor commands would cause a collision, blocking only those commands while letting everything else through, all at 15 Hz on a GPU.

What's unique. Processing live GSplat maps directly is 20-50x faster than converting to dense grids or NeRF occupancy — the map's native representation is the safety filter's native computation.

What it solves

Keeping a robot safely away from obstacles is hard when the map is built from hundreds of thousands of tiny 3D blobs (Gaussian splats) that are still being updated in real time.

How it works

They built a safety filter (called a Control Barrier Function) that reads all the Gaussian blobs directly and blocks unsafe moves, running at 15 times per second on a GPU while the map keeps updating in the background.

Why it matters

First CBF-based safety filter operating over live GSplat maps, 20-50x faster and less conservative than NeRF-based safety methods, validated on a drone with only onboard perception.

MARVEL: Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Constrained Field-of-View Multi-Robot Exploration in Large-Scale Environments

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A team of robots mapping a large unknown space must choose both where to move and where to point their cameras (the field-of-view is narrow); the number of possible direction choices explodes as the space grows, making decision-making computationally intractable.

The approach. Each robot runs a learned policy that reads a graph of nearby teammates and unexplored frontiers, then action pruning cuts down the possible viewing directions to a manageable set before making a decision.

What's unique. Orientation-aware action pruning keeps decisions tractable across 90m x 90m environments; the policy generalizes without retraining even on much larger maps.

What it solves

Getting a team of robots to map a large unknown space efficiently is harder when each robot's camera or sensor only sees in one direction, because the robots must also choose where to point their sensors.

How it works

Each robot runs a learned policy (MARVEL) that reads a graph of nearby robots and unexplored frontiers, and a pruning step cuts down the possible directions to look at so the decision stays manageable even in large areas.

Why it matters

Introduces constrained-FoV multi-robot exploration as a MARL problem with orientation-aware action pruning, achieving generalization to 90x90 m environments and real drone deployment without retraining.
