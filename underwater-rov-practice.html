Underwater ROVs — the real problem

A vehicle in the sea has no ground to stand on and no GPS to trust, so every stage is a fight against buoyancy, water it drags along, and currents that push it off station.

Each stage below pairs our measured demo with the real practical problem behind it: who actually hits it (the industry or system), what it costs when it fails (real failure modes and incidents), how it is solved in production today, and the honest gap between our clean sim and the real thing. The demo captures the essence; this is the world it lives in.

1

Trim to neutral

Our demo: trimming ballast cuts net buoyancy from 20 N to 0.4 N, so idle drift drops from 0.63 m/s to 0.09 m/s.

Who actually hits it

Every work-class ROV and science vehicle is ballasted to near-neutral buoyancy before a dive; Oceaneering and other operators of offshore inspection, maintenance and repair (IMR) ROVs trim floats and weights so thrusters do useful work instead of fighting the vehicle's own float.

What it costs when it fails

An untrimmed vehicle either shoots to the surface the instant it idles or sinks away from the worksite, and burns a vertical thruster fighting buoyancy for the entire mission, wasting power and thruster life.

How it's solved in production

Operators add syntactic-foam flotation and lead ballast to reach slight positive or neutral buoyancy, a standard pre-dive trim procedure; the residual restoring force is kept small enough that station-keeping is cheap.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo treats buoyancy as one clean number; real vehicles change buoyancy with depth as foam and housings compress, seawater density varies with salinity and temperature, and payload swaps re-trim the whole vehicle.

2

Brake for the water

Our demo: braking against 50 kg dry + 45 kg added mass eliminates the 0.38 m overshoot a dry-mass-only plan produces.

Who actually hits it

Anyone docking or approaching subsea structure — ROVs mating to a subsea tree, AUVs homing to a docking station — must account for the water the hull drags along, which for a bluff underwater body can roughly equal or exceed the dry mass.

What it costs when it fails

Planning a stop with only the dry mass under-brakes the vehicle, so it overshoots and collides with the dock, the wellhead, or delicate subsea hardware; at working speeds even a small overshoot can mean an expensive impact.

How it's solved in production

Underwater vehicle controllers use hydrodynamic models — Fossen's 6-DOF formulation with an added-mass matrix M_A added to the rigid-body mass M_RB — so the acceleration and braking plan uses the effective, not the dry, inertia.

Toy demo → real system

We use a single constant added-mass number; real added mass is a full 6-DOF matrix that changes with heading and coupling, and nonlinear drag (not just inertia) dominates the real braking curve.

3

Crab into the current

Our demo: crabbing 17.5 deg upstream (asin of 0.3 m/s current over 1.0 m/s speed) holds the line, versus 6.0 m of cross-track error when pointing straight at the goal.

Who actually hits it

Any vehicle transiting or holding station in a current — survey AUVs on a trackline, work-class ROVs holding position over a subsea worksite in tidal or loop-current flow — must ferry-angle into the flow.

What it costs when it fails

Pointing straight at the goal lets the current sweep the vehicle downstream off its planned track, so a survey line is missed or the vehicle is pushed off station and away from the work, sometimes draping its tether across obstacles.

How it's solved in production

Dynamic positioning and station-keeping controllers estimate the current and add a feedforward crab angle or offsetting thrust; DVL bottom-lock gives ground-relative velocity so the controller can null out drift and hold position even against current.

Toy demo → real system

Our current is a known constant; real currents are unknown, unsteady, and depth-varying, and tether drag adds a large disturbance our free-flying vehicle ignores.

4

Fly the seabed

Our demo: altitude-hold keeps a 2.0 m clearance over rising ground, while a constant-depth transit falls to -1.0 m clearance and crashes.

Who actually hits it

Survey AUVs and inspection ROVs flying imaging sonar, cameras, or pipe-tracking payloads a fixed height above the bottom rely on altitude following; DVLs and altimeters are standard fit for exactly this.

What it costs when it fails

Holding a constant depth flies the vehicle straight into ground that rises toward it, colliding with the seabed, a reef, or subsea structure and damaging the vehicle or payload; a lost altitude reference can also let it wander too high for its sonar to see.

How it's solved in production

A DVL or single-beam altimeter measures height above bottom and the controller holds a commanded altitude, terrain-following the seabed; the DVL's four angled acoustic beams give both altitude and ground-relative velocity from bottom tracking.

Toy demo → real system

Our terrain is a smooth known profile; real altimeters lose bottom lock over soft mud, steep slopes, or out of range, and reactive altitude-hold cannot see a vertical cliff ahead the way a look-ahead sonar can.

5

Steady the arm

Our demo: reaction compensation cuts end-effector error on a free-floating base from 0.07 m to 0.006 m as a 15 kg arm sweeps 1.0 m on a 200 kg vehicle.

Who actually hits it

Free-floating intervention systems — a Schilling manipulator on a work-class ROV, or a science manipulator on a hovering AUV — where moving the arm shoves the whole vehicle because nothing is bolted to the seabed.

What it costs when it fails

Moving the arm on an unanchored vehicle pushes the base backward by reaction, so the end-effector misses its target — a bad grab on a valve or connector, or an unwanted collision with the structure being worked on.

How it's solved in production

Coordinated vehicle-manipulator control compensates the arm's reaction with the thrusters (or the ROV grabs the structure with a second, powerful grabber arm to anchor itself), so the dexterous arm can work against a steady base.

Toy demo → real system

We model a single rigid arm on a rigid base; real coupling includes hydrodynamic added mass on the moving links, thruster lag, and umbilical forces, and precise free-floating manipulation remains hard in practice.
