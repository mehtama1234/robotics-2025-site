250 papers · a rule across everything — how the body itself is built shapes what the robot can sense, decide, and do

The big picture — connecting the dots

Rigid robots are built for factories, where every part sits still and the job never changes. When a robot meets the real world — uneven ground, fragile objects, a living body, or a narrow gap — rigidity becomes a liability. The problem is not whether to be soft or stiff, but how a body can shift between them on demand: soft when it needs to give, firm when it needs to push. This connects to the one core constraint all robots face: taking an unclear, changing world, deciding fast, moving a real body, doing it safely, cheaply, and generally. Soft body research touches every piece of that — a soft fingertip with a built-in sensor solves sensing and safety at once; a body that changes shape solves motion in constrained spaces; a printable, modular design lowers cost and raises generality.

Five moves the field is making:

  • ① Build artificial muscles from electricity, air, and fluid. Living muscles contract when a nerve fires; researchers build artificial muscles using voltage, air pressure, or pressurized fluid. Portable, High-Frequency, and High-Voltage Control Circuits for Untethered Miniature Robots Driven by Dielectric Elastomer Actuators reached 1,800 volts on a 2.5-gram board to power crawling robots. Stretchable Electrohydraulic Artificial Muscle for Full Motion Ranges in Musculoskeletal Antagonistic Joints paired electric muscles with clutches to get full contraction-and-extension range.
  • ② Route cables and tendons to avoid cross-talk and friction. Many soft robots use cables run through the body like tendons, keeping heavy motors at the base. D3-ARM: High-Dynamic, Dexterous and Fully Decoupled Cable-Driven Robotic Arm re-routed cables so each motor connects to one joint with no accidental drag. A Comparative Study of Pulley and Bowden Transmissions in a Novel Cable-Driven Exosuit, the Stillsuit measured energy loss through friction and found pulleys far more efficient than tubes.
  • ③ Copy animal bodies and motion to handle real spaces. Worms, fish, and snakes move by sending bends along their body, squeezing through gaps that wheeled robots cannot enter. Leader-Follower Formation Enabled by Pressure Sensing in Free-Swimming Undulatory Robotic Fish trained a small neural network to steer a robot fish using only water-pressure readings on its sides, with no camera. Adaptive Concertina Locomotion of a Robotic Snake Through Narrow Uncertain Channels mimics how a snake grips tunnel walls and adjusts its wave pattern by reading motor effort.
  • ④ Sense inside a soft body without making it stiffer. Most sensors are rigid or bulky; putting them inside a soft body often weakens it. A Piezoresistive Printable Strain Sensor for Monitoring and Control of Soft Robotic Links printed a sensor directly into a soft arm by drawing silver ink onto a rubber base. Milli-Scale AcousTac Sensing Using Soft Helmholtz Resonators uses an air-filled soft pocket on the fingertip that shifts pitch when pressed, read by a microphone elsewhere.
  • ⑤ Build math and simulation that keeps pace with soft physics. Soft bodies have nearly infinite ways to bend, making them very hard to control. A Synergistic Framework for Learning Shape Estimation and Shape-Aware Whole-Body Control Policy for Continuum Robots trains two linked neural networks together — one estimates shape, the other decides how to move — so each helps the other improve.

The throughline: the field is learning that a body's shape and compliance are not obstacles to control — they are the control. A soft fingertip senses touch while it grips. A bendable cable-driven arm stays light at the tip. A body that changes stiffness handles both delicate and forceful tasks. Soft research pays off most when it solves two problems at once — safety and sensing, or generality and weight — instead of pretending either one is optional. Still open: full artificial muscles that match living muscle in strength, speed, and efficiency are not yet there. Controlling soft bodies reliably remains hard — the math is complex, sensors are limited, and simulation is slow. The gap between a lab proof-of-concept and a robot that works outside the lab without careful tuning stays large.

The problem — in depth

Every robot body is a bargain. Make it rigid and you get strength, repeatability, and easy math. Make it soft and you get something else: the ability to squeeze through gaps, cushion a fall, stretch around odd shapes, and touch a person or a piece of fruit without leaving a bruise.

The hard bargain is that the real world does not care which choice you made. A robot sent to pull weeds in a greenhouse must bend between plant stems. A robot helping a surgeon must navigate slippery, shifting tissue without tearing it. A search robot squeezed into rubble must change its shape as the gap changes. A robot arm helping a person who has lost muscle strength must push and pull without ever pressing too hard.

Rigid metal arms and legs, which almost all industrial robots have used for decades, were designed for factories where every part sits in a fixed spot and the job never changes. When the space is uneven, the object is fragile, or the task demands contact with a living body, rigid machines either break what they touch or cannot reach it at all.

The deeper problem is that stiffness is not always good or bad — it depends on the moment. When you pick up a coffee cup, your hand is soft enough to wrap around the glass but stiff enough not to drop it. When you grip a door handle, you stiffen up more. Living muscles do this automatically. A rigid robot joint cannot. A fully soft joint cannot either, because it has no stiffness at all. The real target is a body that can change how stiff it is on demand — soft when it needs to give, firm when it needs to push.

This connects to the one big problem all robots face: taking in an unclear, changing world, deciding what to do, moving a real body, and doing it fast, cheap, safe, and general. Soft body research touches every one of those pieces. A soft fingertip with a built-in sensor solves sensing and safety at once. A robot that grows from its tip through a narrow pipe (called a vine robot, though none of these papers are on that specific design) solves motion in constrained spaces. A body that cushions contact makes the robot safer near people. A body that can be printed or assembled from modules lowers cost and raises generality.

The field is not one idea. It is a loose collection of groups — engineers, biologists, materials scientists, and control theorists — each attacking a different piece of the same goal: bodies that handle the world's messiness instead of demanding the world be clean.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Muscles made from electricity, air, and fluid

In plain termsLiving bodies get their power from muscles that shrink and pull instantly. Robots need electrical or air-powered muscles that react as fast and pull as hard, but weigh almost nothing so the robot can move on its own.
The sub-problemLiving muscles contract instantly when triggered; artificial muscles need to match that speed and power while staying light enough for untethered robots to carry.
High-voltage power in a tiny package — Portable, High-Frequency, and High-Voltage Control Circuits for Untethered Miniature Robots Driven by Dielectric Elastomer Actuators (ICRA). Reaches 1,800 volts on a 2.5-gram board by stacking low-voltage steps in series, powering crawling robots at 15 pulses per second.
Full-range joint motion — Stretchable Electrohydraulic Artificial Muscle for Full Motion Ranges in Musculoskeletal Antagonistic Joints (ICRA). Pairs two hydraulic muscles on opposite sides of a joint with electric clutches so the joint can both contract and extend at over three cycles per second.
Tunable stiffness via air — Anisotropic Stiffness and Programmable Actuation for Soft Robots Enabled by an Inflated Rotational Joint (ICRA). Shapes an inflatable tube so only part wrinkles when pressurized; the bend direction and stiffness can be tuned by controlling which region wrinkles.

Artificial muscles now come in three flavors — voltage, fluid, and air — each trading off weight, speed, and range.

2. Cables and tendons pulling through a body

In plain termsThink of your arm — muscles at the shoulder pull cables through your arm to move your fingers. If you route those cables badly, pulling one finger accidentally moves another. Route them right and everything moves clean and precise.
The sub-problemCables can route motors to the base and keep robots light, but if cables share paths through joints, pulling one accidentally moves others. Clean routing is the difference between precise control and messy crosstalk.
One motor, one joint, no drag — D3-ARM: High-Dynamic, Dexterous and Fully Decoupled Cable-Driven Robotic Arm (ICRA). Re-routes cables so each motor connects to exactly one joint with no cross-talk, then adds tensioning to keep cables taut, achieving 1.29 mm positioning error.
Pulleys beat friction — A Comparative Study of Pulley and Bowden Transmissions in a Novel Cable-Driven Exosuit, the Stillsuit (ICRA). Measures energy loss through cable friction; pulleys reduce wasted energy to near zero, while flexible tubes waste 40–60 percent of motor power.
Program how hard to grip — Dexterous Assembly Using a Planar Hand Having Programmable Passive Compliance (ICRA). Uses opposing tendons on each finger; tightening one tendon before a task sets the direction the finger will yield, needing no sensors during contact.

Clean cable routing solves both dexterity and efficiency: precise joints and light, wearable exoskeletons that waste almost no energy.

3. Bodies that move like animals

In plain termsA snake slithers through tight tunnels by sending waves down its whole body. A wheeled robot gets stuck. By copying how animals bend and flow through space, soft robots can go where wheels and legs simply cannot fit.
The sub-problemWheels and legs fail on uneven ground, through pipes, and in dense vegetation; animals solve these by moving their whole body — sending waves down their shape to squeeze into spaces robots with rigid joints cannot reach.
Swimming by shape waves — AquaMILR+ (ICRA). Sends wave-like bends down a limbless body to swim; can stiffen or soften on command to deform around obstacles, and uses an internal screw to change buoyancy like a fish's air bladder.
Swim in formation from water pressure alone — Leader-Follower Formation Enabled by Pressure Sensing in Free-Swimming Undulatory Robotic Fish (ICRA). Trains a neural network to steer using only water-pressure sensors on the sides, with no camera; learned from one hour of human demonstrations via DAgger.
Snake gripping tunnels — Adaptive Concertina Locomotion of a Robotic Snake Through Narrow Uncertain Channels (ICRA). Mimics how a snake grips tunnel walls; adjusts its wave pattern in real time by reading motor effort to sense how tight the space is.
Microscale swimmer — Feedback Control of a Single-Tail Bioinspired 59-mg Swimmer (ICRA). A robot lighter than a grain of rice uses a shape-memory alloy tail that bends when heated and springs back when cooled.

Undulatory motion lets robots flow through gaps, dense spaces, and water where wheels and legs are useless.

4. Sensing inside a soft body

In plain termsYour skin feels touch without being stiff or hard. If you bolt a rigid camera or metal sensor onto a soft robot arm, the arm gets stiff and weak. The trick is to sense what you need — touch, bend, pressure — using soft materials so nothing breaks.
The sub-problemMost sensors are rigid or bulky; putting them inside soft robots makes them stiffer and weaker. The real need is to sense shape and touch without compromising the softness that makes the robot useful.
Print sensors into the arm — A Piezoresistive Printable Strain Sensor for Monitoring and Control of Soft Robotic Links (ICRA). Prints a rubber base, then draws conductive silver ink onto it; the sensor is built into the arm and changes resistance when bent.
Touch from sound pitch alone — Milli-Scale AcousTac Sensing Using Soft Helmholtz Resonators (ICRA). A tiny air-filled soft pocket on the fingertip shifts its pitch when pressed; a microphone elsewhere hears the change — no wires at the touch point.
Whiskers that see touch — A Magnetic-Actuated Vision-Based Whisker Array for Contact Perception and Grasping (ICRA). Eight thin flexible whiskers around a soft pad are moved by magnets; a camera watches how they bend, and a neural network reads the patterns to identify objects and choose a grip.

Sensors can now be printable, acoustic, or optical — all ways to sense without making the robot stiffer.

5. Simulation and math that keeps pace with soft physics

In plain termsA rigid arm's math is simple — each joint either bends or doesn't. A soft arm can curve in infinite ways, so you have to know exactly how it bends at every moment to control it. The math doesn't exist yet, so researchers mix hand-tuned physics with machine learning to get close enough.
The sub-problemSoft bodies have infinite ways to bend; you cannot predict what they will do without knowing their exact shape at every instant. Standard math does not scale, and pure machine learning wastes data. Bridging this gap is the bottleneck.
Physics baked into the model — Physics-Informed Split Koopman Operators for Data-Efficient Soft Robotic Simulation (ICRA). Builds a linear model by combining two kinds of observations and baking in known physical laws; stays accurate with far less data than a purely learned model.
Shape and control teach each other — A Synergistic Framework for Learning Shape Estimation and Shape-Aware Whole-Body Control Policy for Continuum Robots (ICRA). Trains two neural networks together: one estimates shape, the other decides how to move; each improves as it learns from the other's output.

The path forward is hybrid: ground learned models in physics, or use multiple networks that improve each other.

Where it stands

Soft and body-inspired robots can now move through narrow spaces, sense touch without rigid electronics, and change their stiffness on command — things rigid machines cannot do. Cable-driven arms and printable sensors are close to practical use. Full artificial muscles that match the strength, speed, and efficiency of living muscle are not yet there. Controlling soft bodies reliably is still the central open problem: the math is hard, the sensors are limited, and simulation is slow. The gap between a proof-of-concept lab robot and one that works outside the lab, without careful tuning, remains large.

Paper by paper — the method

Individual and Collective Behaviors in Soft Robot Worms Inspired by Living Worm Blobs

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Real worms move as tangled blobs, but there's no controlled robot platform to test how they coordinate and move as a group.

The approach. Build soft air-powered robots shaped like worms, then vary air pressure to control how tightly they tangle and measure what the group does in air and water.

What's unique. First soft robot platform that replicates collective worm behavior through physical entanglement, not simulation — making it a testbed for studying emergent group locomotion directly.

What it solves

Scientists do not fully understand how real worms move as a tangled group, and there has been no robot platform that can replicate and test that group behavior in a controlled way.

How it works

They built thin, air-powered soft robots shaped like worms with a very long and narrow body, similar to California blackworms. By changing how much air pressure the robots use, the researchers can control how tightly the robots tangle together and then measure how the group moves in air and water.

Why it matters

First soft-robot platform that faithfully replicates worm-blob collective behaviors through physical entanglement, providing a tunable testbed for studying emergent collective locomotion.

D3-ARM: High-Dynamic, Dexterous and Fully Decoupled Cable-Driven Robotic Arm

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Cable-driven arms can keep heavy motors at the base, but cables that share paths through joints accidentally move multiple joints at once, breaking precision.

The approach. Redesign cable routing so each motor drives exactly one joint with no cross-talk, add tensioning to keep cables taut, and move all electronics to the base.

What's unique. First fully decoupled cable routing removes crosstalk at the design stage rather than fighting it in control — achieving 1.29 mm positioning error with base-mounted actuators.

What it solves

Robot arms that use cables to transmit force have a problem where moving one joint accidentally moves others, making precise control hard.

How it works

They redesigned how cables run through each joint so each motor drives exactly one joint and nothing else. They also added a mechanism to keep cables taut over long runs, and moved all electronics to the base of the arm.

Why it matters

First fully decoupled cable-driven arm design achieving 1.29 mm average positioning error and 2.0 kg payload at 776 mm reach with all actuators base-mounted.

A Piezoresistive Printable Strain Sensor for Monitoring and Control of Soft Robotic Links

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Adding rigid sensors to soft arms makes them stiff and weak, yet sensing arm bend is needed for control.

The approach. Print the sensor directly into the arm: melt a soft rubbery base (TPU) via 3D printing, then draw conductive silver ink onto it as traces that change resistance when bent.

What's unique. Sensor is fully printable and built into the arm during fabrication — no assembly, no added stiffness, integrated from the start rather than bolted on.

What it solves

Putting a flexible bend sensor inside a soft robot arm without making it stiff or weak is hard to do with standard manufacturing.

How it works

A sensor is printed in two steps: a soft rubbery base (TPU) is printed by melting plastic filament, then silver ink is written onto it to form conductive traces; the result is a sensor that changes electrical resistance when bent and can be built into the arm during printing.

Why it matters

Demonstrates a fully printable, piezoresistive strain sensor with a gauge factor of 0.24 and stable repeatability in the 3–8% flexure strain range, integrated into a functional soft robotic link.

Stretchable Electrohydraulic Artificial Muscle for Full Motion Ranges in Musculoskeletal Antagonistic Joints

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Artificial muscles can only pull or push, so pairing two against each other for full range motion fails — one side prevents the other from working.

The approach. Pair hydraulic soft actuators (HASELs) with electric clutches and a controller that coordinates both sides, so one can disengage while the other moves.

What's unique. Clutches decouple the two sides of an antagonistic joint, letting each muscle fully contract and extend — three cycles per second — instead of fighting each other.

What it solves

Existing artificial muscles can only pull or only push, so robotic joints that pair two muscles against each other cannot move through their full range.

How it works

They pair electrically-driven hydraulic soft actuators (called HASELs) with electric clutches and add a controller that coordinates the two sides so the joint can both contract and extend, cycling up to 3.2 times per second.

Why it matters

A muscle-clutch architecture that enables full contraction-and-extension range of motion in antagonistic joints using non-stretchable artificial muscles, demonstrated on HASEL actuators.

Portable, High-Frequency, and High-Voltage Control Circuits for Untethered Miniature Robots Driven by Dielectric Elastomer Actuators

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Stretchy electrical muscles need 1,800 volts to work, but the electronics that deliver that voltage are usually too heavy for a tiny untethered robot to carry.

The approach. Stack many low-voltage circuits in series to reach high voltage on a tiny board — all in 2.5 grams with a power converter and camera onboard.

What's unique. Fits working high-voltage control in 2.5 grams without specialized chips — using basic series resistors to step voltage up for untethered miniature robots.

What it solves

Artificial muscles made from stretchy electrical material need very high voltage to work, but the electronics that deliver that voltage are usually too heavy for a small untethered robot.

How it works

The circuit connects many low-voltage parts in series to reach 1,800 volts and fits on a board weighing only 2.5 grams; paired with a tiny power converter, it drives a crawling robot at 15 pulses per second with onboard video.

Why it matters

A 2.5 g, series-resistor-based high-voltage circuit enabling untethered DEA-driven miniature robots at up to 1 kHz without specialized HV control ICs.

Mathematical Modeling and Rolling Motion Generation of Planar Seven-link Robot That Forms Passive Closed and Active Open Chains

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A seven-link robot can roll along a surface either passively (gravity) or actively (motors), but there was no unified math to describe both modes or how they interact.

The approach. Build separate models for closed-loop passive rolling and open-chain active rolling, then parametrize both in one framework that handles collisions and shape changes.

What's unique. First math that unifies passive-dynamic and active rolling in a single model — treating the same robot morphology as either a passive roller or an actively driven leaning octagon.

What it solves

A seven-link robot needs to roll along a surface, but there was no clear math to describe how it rolls—either by letting gravity do the work or by using its own motors.

How it works

The researchers built two separate math models: one for when all the links form a closed loop and roll downhill on their own, and one for when the links act as an open chain and motors drive the roll by holding a leaning octagon shape.

Why it matters

First mathematical formulation of rolling motion and collision dynamics for a planar seven-link robot that unifies passive-dynamic and actively controlled rolling in a single parametric framework.

CAFEs: Cable-Driven Collaborative Floating End-Effectors for Agriculture Applications

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Scaling farm automation to multiple robot arms over wide areas is expensive — each arm needs its own motors and cables, and they don't share infrastructure.

The approach. Multiple arms clip onto a single shared cable set and can attach or detach without stopping the system; spring-mass models and position control keep each arm stable while moving.

What's unique. Arms share one cable backbone and can hot-swap without individual cable actuators — scaling farms becomes modular and doesn't replicate actuators for each arm.

What it solves

Automating large-scale farm tasks like picking and placing fruit across wide areas with multiple robot arms is hard when each arm needs its own motors and cables.

How it works

Multiple robot arms share a single set of moving cables and can clip on or detach quickly without stopping the system. A spring-mass model and position control keep each arm stable while it moves.

Why it matters

A scalable multi-arm cable-driven system where arms share a single cable set and can hot-attach/detach without individual actuators for cable motion, enabling large-workspace agricultural automation.

A Synergistic Framework for Learning Shape Estimation and Shape-Aware Whole-Body Control Policy for Continuum Robots

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Flexible tube-like robots are hard to control because tracking their exact shape and moving them happen at the same time, and their physics are complex and messy.

The approach. Train two neural networks together: one estimates the robot's current shape, the other uses that shape to decide how to move, and each helps the other improve.

What's unique. Co-learning decouples shape estimation from control — instead of one network fighting both problems, each solves its own piece and they bootstrap each other to better performance.

What it solves

Flexible tube-like robots are hard to control because you must track their exact shape and move them at the same time, and their physics are messy and hard to model.

How it works

Two linked neural networks are trained together: one figures out the robot's current shape, and the other uses that shape to decide how to move the whole robot, with each network helping the other improve.

Why it matters

A synergistic dual-NODE architecture that co-learns shape estimation and shape-aware control for continuum robots, overcoming the shape-awareness gap in prior data-driven methods.

An Ultra-Light Seedling Planting Mechanism for Use in Aerial Reforestation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Drones planting trees need a mechanism strong enough to push seedlings into soil, but it must be light enough for a medium or large drone to carry.

The approach. Build a two-stage telescoping tube powered by compressed air that fires the seedling into the ground with enough force, all weighing only 8 kg.

What's unique. Ultra-light pneumatic planter at 8 kg works on drones without GPS — tuned to both clear-cut and selectively-harvested forests in a single design.

What it solves

Drones that plant trees need a mechanism strong enough to push a seedling into soil but light enough to fit within what a medium or large drone can carry.

How it works

A two-stage telescoping tube powered by compressed air fires the seedling into the ground with enough force, while the whole device weighs only 8 kg so it can be carried by a drone.

Why it matters

A novel ultralight (8 kg) double-telescoping pneumatic tree-planting mechanism validated for aerial deployment, enabling GPS-independent reforestation in both clear-cut and selectively-harvested forests.

Leader-Follower Formation Enabled by Pressure Sensing in Free-Swimming Undulatory Robotic Fish

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Getting one swimming fish to follow another in tight formation with no camera or external tracking — only onboard body sensors — is hard.

The approach. Train a small neural network (LSTM) using recorded demonstrations and DAgger correction that reads just two water-pressure sensors on the follower's sides plus motor state and tilt.

What's unique. Formation swimming uses only pressure sensing and one hour of training data — no vision, no external tracking, no dense model — yet maintains tight leader-follower formation.

What it solves

Getting one swimming fish robot to follow another in tight formation is hard when the follower has no camera or external tracking — only its own body sensors.

How it works

They train a small neural network (LSTM) using recorded demonstrations and a method called DAgger that corrects the network when it drifts — the network reads just two water-pressure sensors on the follower's sides plus the motor state and tilt, and outputs steering commands to stay behind the leader.

Why it matters

First demonstration of leader-follower formation swimming in free-swimming undulatory robotic fish using only two bilateral pressure sensors and less than one hour of training data.
