Gaussian process regression: know what you don't know

A Gaussian process returns not just a curve but a calibrated uncertainty band that widens exactly where the data runs out — so it still covers the truth in the gap and when extrapolating, while a point regressor is confidently wrong there.

A Gaussian process (GP) treats the unknown function as a distribution over functions and, given noisy training points, returns a closed-form posterior: a MEAN prediction and a VARIANCE at every input. Using an RBF kernel the posterior mean is Ks^T (K + sigma^2 I)^-1 y and the variance is Kss - Ks^T (K + sigma^2 I)^-1 Ks — so the uncertainty is a computed quantity, not an add-on. That variance is small near the training data and grows in the gaps between points and when extrapolating, giving a calibrated 2-sigma band that tells you where the model is guessing.

we ran itWe sampled y = sin(1.5x) + 0.5x at just 11 noisy points that leave a wide gap in [1, 4] and no data past x=5, then fit a GP (RBF kernel) and a degree-6 point regressor. In the gap and extrapolation region the GP's 2-sigma band covered the true function 100% of the time (its band widened 4.5x vs near the data), and its mean stayed close to truth (RMSE 0.33). The point regressor had no band and was confidently wrong there — RMSE 1.57, about 4.7x the GP's error, shooting off-scale at both ends. Near the data both were accurate (GP RMSE 0.06, poly 0.26).
the failure it shows A point regressor returns a single curve with no notion of confidence, so in a data gap or past the last training point it extrapolates a wrong answer with the same apparent certainty as a well-supported one. The GP instead grows its uncertainty exactly there — it flags where it has no evidence instead of bluffing.
