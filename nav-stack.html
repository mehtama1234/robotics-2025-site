navigation stack · the pipeline, stage by stage

Crossing a building

A mobile robot has to know where it is, plan a route, keep its body off the walls, dodge the people who wander across it, and actually drive the line smoothly. Five stages, five distinct sub-fields — each a plain rule, run and measured, with the failure it prevents. A production narrative, not a grab-bag: each stage feeds the next.

Each stage is one link: the pose feeds the planner, the plan is made safe by the costmap, the safe plan is defended against moving obstacles, and the final line is driven by the tracker. A weak link anywhere and the robot is lost, stuck, or in the wall.

1

Know where you are

Navigation

The robot's wheels report how far it turned, but wheels slip and the error compounds — after a minute of driving, dead-reckoning alone thinks the robot is a metre from where it actually is. Everything downstream plans from a lie.

Correct dead-reckoning with what you can measure; a particle filter fuses motion and sensing so the pose stops drifting.
mean pose error — dead-reckoning0.696 m
mean pose error — particle filter0.131 m
dead-reckoning error by the end1.168 m

Over a 60-step drive with 6 landmarks, dead-reckoning drifts to a mean pose error of 0.696 m (and 1.168 m by the end). A 600-particle filter fusing the same odometry with range measurements holds the error to 0.131 m.

2

Plan the route

Navigation

The robot needs the shortest route across a building-sized grid. Search outward blindly in every direction (Dijkstra) and you expand almost the whole map before reaching the goal — too slow to replan as things change.

Guide the search with a distance-to-go heuristic: A* finds the same shortest path as Dijkstra while expanding a fraction of the nodes.
nodes expanded — Dijkstra1263
nodes expanded — A*75
path length (both, optimal)75 steps

On a 1600-cell grid, Dijkstra expands 1263 nodes to find the 75-step path. A* finds the same optimal path expanding only 75 nodes — a 16.8× speedup.

3

Give the body room

Navigation

The planner treats the robot as a point, so it happily routes through the shortest gap in a wall. But the robot is not a point — it has width — and the shortest gap is narrower than the robot, so the 'valid' path drives the body straight into the wall.

Inflate every obstacle by the robot's radius before planning; a point-robot path collides the moment the body has width.
min clearance — raw plan1.0 cell (collides)
min clearance — inflated costmap3.0 cells (safe)
path length — raw vs inflated34 → 74

With a 2-cell-radius robot, the raw point-plan takes the short gap and comes within 1.0 cell of the wall — the body collides. Planning on the inflated costmap routes through the wide gap (34→74 cells longer) but keeps 3.0 cells of clearance — safe.

4

Dodge the pedestrian

Multi-robot

The planned route is clear of walls, but a person walks across it. A plan made against the static map drives the robot straight into a moving obstacle it never accounted for.

Plan in velocity space: forbid the velocities that lead to a collision and pick the closest allowed one.
min separation — drive straight0.00 m (hit)
min separation — velocity obstacles0.96 m (clear)
reaches goal after dodgingyes

Driving straight to the goal, the robot's separation from the crossing pedestrian drops to 0.00 m — a collision (radius 0.6 m). Choosing velocities outside the collision cone keeps a 0.96 m separation and still reaches the goal.

5

Follow the path

Control & MPC

A plan is a line on the floor; the robot still has to drive it. Steer toward the closest point on the path and the robot yanks the wheel left-right-left every step — the tracking looks fine on paper but the motors chatter and the ride is jerky.

Track a path by aiming at a lookahead point, not the nearest one; chasing the closest point makes the steering chatter.
steering chatter — chase nearest0.71 rad/step
steering chatter — lookahead0.045 rad/step
cross-track error (both)~0.073 m

Both controllers stay on the path (cross-track ~0.073 m either way). But chasing the nearest point chatters the steering at 0.71 rad/step, while a lookahead point cuts that to 0.045 rad/step — 16× smoother.
