On-orbit servicing — the real problem

A servicing spacecraft has no ground to push against and no room for a second try, so every stage is a fight to move an arm, hold an attitude, and grab another satellite without spinning itself off target or tearing itself apart.

← the space servicing demo · the build lab · home
Each stage below pairs our measured demo with the real practical problem behind it: who actually hits it (the industry or system), what it costs when it fails (real failure modes and incidents), how it is solved in production today, and the honest gap between our clean sim and the real thing. The demo captures the essence; this is the world it lives in.
1

Move without a foothold

Our demo: on a 500 kg base, swinging a 40 kg arm 0.8 m recoils the base 0.0593 m and misses by 0.0593 m, but cancelling the reaction cuts the end-effector error to 0.004 m.

Who actually hits it

Any free-flying robot-arm servicer hits this: Japan's ETS-VII (NASDA/JAXA, launched 1997), the world's first free-flying space robot, flew a 2 m six-DOF arm on a free-floating base and demonstrated 'zero-reaction' arm maneuvers; DARPA's Orbital Express (2007) and robotic servicers like the planned NASA OSAM-1 face the same coupling between arm and body.

What it costs when it fails

With nothing bolted down, conservation of momentum means moving the arm shoves the whole spacecraft backward, so the end-effector drifts off its target — a missed grapple point or an unintended contact with the client — and the arm's motion perturbs the base attitude the pointing loop must then fight.

How it's solved in production

Coordinated free-flyer/manipulator control plans the arm so its reaction is either cancelled by thrusters or nulled by counter-moving links (reactionless / zero-reaction maneuvers, flight-verified on ETS-VII), letting a dexterous arm work against an effectively steady base.

Toy demo → real system

We model one rigid arm on a rigid base with a single reaction to cancel; real systems have flexible links and appendages, fuel-slosh, thruster lag and plume impingement, and multi-arm coupling that make free-floating manipulation genuinely hard.

2

Hold the attitude

Our demo: a 0.05 N·m disturbance on a 200 kg·m^2 spacecraft drifts 0.45 rad off target in 60 s with no control, while a PD loop on the reaction wheels holds pointing to 0.0012 rad.

Who actually hits it

Essentially every three-axis-stabilized satellite carries reaction wheels (or control-moment gyros) for exactly this — communications satellites, Earth-observation platforms, and servicers like the Northrop Grumman Mission Extension Vehicles all hold attitude on spinning wheels rather than by firing thrusters continuously.

What it costs when it fails

A steady disturbance torque — solar pressure, gravity gradient, or the reaction from a moving arm — integrates into an ever-growing pointing error if the loop is open; lost attitude means antennas and sensors point away from their target and, during rendezvous, the docking sensors lose the client.

How it's solved in production

A closed-loop attitude controller commands the reaction wheels to produce counter-torque, holding the body on target to milliradian-class accuracy; this three-axis wheel-based control is the standard production approach across the satellite industry.

Toy demo → real system

Our disturbance is a single constant torque and the plant is a rigid rotor; real spacecraft see time-varying multi-axis disturbances, sensor noise and quantization, wheel friction and jitter, and structural flex that a textbook PD loop alone does not capture.

3

Unload the wheels

Our demo: under a persistent 0.05 N·m disturbance the wheels saturate (fraction 1.0 of 600 rad/s) and pointing blows up to 6.05 rad, but desaturating keeps wheel speed at 0.65 of the limit and pointing at 0.0012 rad.

Who actually hits it

Every reaction-wheel-controlled spacecraft must manage momentum this way; missions use thrusters for momentum unloading (or magnetic torquers in low Earth orbit, where the Earth's field is usable), a routine part of satellite operations in geostationary orbit where MEV-class servicers operate.

What it costs when it fails

A persistent one-sided disturbance spins the wheels ever faster toward their mechanical speed limit; a saturated wheel can produce no further torque, so the spacecraft loses attitude control until the wheel is unloaded — a serious operational failure for a satellite that must stay pointed.

How it's solved in production

Momentum desaturation, or 'wheel unloading,' fires thrusters to torque the body against the wheel momentum so the wheels can slow back into their usable range while attitude is held; this is standard practice, done periodically before the wheels approach saturation.

Toy demo → real system

Our model treats saturation as one speed threshold and desaturation as a clean torque; real wheel unloading trades fuel against control authority, couples into attitude during the burn, and in low Earth orbit often uses magnetic torquers instead of propellant — trade-offs our toy sim omits.

4

Berth on the port

Our demo: braking a 0.5 m/s approach at 0.02 m/s^2 needs a 6.25 m stop distance; a plan that brakes late hits the port at 0.265 m/s, while braking on time reaches contact at ~0 m/s.

Who actually hits it

Rendezvous and proximity operations (RPO) and berthing are the core of commercial life-extension: Northrop Grumman / SpaceLogistics MEV-1 docked with Intelsat 901 in February 2020 (the first docking of two commercial satellites in orbit) and MEV-2 docked with the active Intelsat 10-02 in April 2021 — both capturing a client via its liquid-apogee-engine nozzle.

What it costs when it fails

There is no atmosphere to bleed off closing speed, so a plan that brakes too late arrives at the docking port with high relative velocity; a hard contact can damage the client satellite or the servicer's docking mechanism, and there is no easy do-over in geostationary orbit.

How it's solved in production

Careful RPO profiles brake early enough that the servicer arrives at contact at a low, controlled velocity, using relative-navigation sensors to null closing rate and lateral offset before soft capture — the approach MEV-1 and MEV-2 flew successfully in GEO.

Toy demo → real system

We use a one-dimensional constant-deceleration stop; real berthing is six-DOF with lighting-dependent vision, plume and gravity-gradient disturbances, and tight lateral and angular tolerances at the capture interface that a straight-line braking model does not represent.

5

Match the spin

Our demo: grasping a 10 deg/s tumbling client without matching leaves the full 10 deg/s relative rate at contact, while spinning the chaser up to match cuts the relative rate to 0.3 deg/s.

Who actually hits it

Cooperative clients like the Intelsats MEV serviced are stabilized and easy to approach, but active-debris-removal targets are not: ESA's ClearSpace mission (contracted 2020) is designed to rendezvous with, synchronize with, and capture an uncooperative, tumbling object using a robotic capture mechanism — the hard case ETS-VII's capture experiments began to probe.

What it costs when it fails

Closing a grasp across a rotation rate loads the arm with the full relative angular velocity at the instant of contact, which can tear the manipulator or violently perturb both spacecraft; a botched grab on a tumbling target risks losing the arm, the servicer, or creating more debris.

How it's solved in production

The chaser first observes the target's tumble, then spins up to match its rotation so the relative angular rate at capture is near zero, and only then closes the grasp — matching before capture is the accepted approach for tumbling, uncooperative targets.

Toy demo → real system

Our target tumbles about a single known axis at a known rate; real debris tumbles about multiple axes with an unknown, drifting spin state that must be estimated from close-range sensing, and matching a fast complex tumble remains an open, one-shot problem no operational mission has yet flown.
