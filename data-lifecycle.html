Teaching a robot: which demonstrations to collect

A robot skill is only as good as the demonstrations you spend your budget on. Four data decisions turn that budget into a policy — where the skill is weak, which demo to collect next, which to keep, and when to stop. Each is a plain rule, run and measured on one honest imitation simulation (a point robot learning an action skill with one sharp move). Built in the demo-package style of the CV project: a tested, reusable core behind every stage.

One thread runs through every stage: demonstrations are the budget, and spending them uniformly wastes most of it. The lifecycle is how you spend the budget where the skill actually needs it.

1 map → 2 select → 3 curate → 4 stop  ·  find the weak spot, demonstrate there, keep only what teaches, and stop at the elbow.
1

Skill Coverage Map

You are about to spend expensive human demonstrations teaching a robot a skill, but you have no idea which part of the skill the policy is already weak at. Collect blindly and you pour demos into the parts it already knows.

Know where the policy is weak before you spend a single demonstration.

On held-out states, the ones the map flags as uncertain have error 0.163 — over 5.3× the error of the states it calls confident (0.031). Disagreement and true error correlate at 0.628.

2

Active Demonstration Selection

Human demonstrations are the budget. Collecting them uniformly wastes most of the budget re-teaching the easy majority of the workspace while the one hard move — the sharp part where the robot actually fails — stays under-sampled.

Collect the demonstration that changes the policy, not another easy one.
demos to target — active162
demos to target — random246
hard-region demos — active vs random149 vs 24
hard-skill error at equal budget0.144 vs 0.244

To reach the same accuracy on the sharp sub-skill (MAE 0.244), active selection needs 162 demonstrations versus 246 for random collection — and it lands 149 demos in the hard region where random lands only 24. At an equal 246-demo budget, active reaches MAE 0.144 vs random's 0.244.

3

Demonstration Curation

A raw collection log is full of near-duplicate demonstrations of the easy spot and a handful of bad ones — a mis-teleoperated grasp, a mislabeled action. Trained on the pile as-is, the bad demos poison the policy and the duplicates buy nothing.

Keep the data that changes the model; drop what repeats or poisons.
raw pile size → MAE200 → 0.218
curated size → MAE71 → 0.082
corrupted demos removed24
error reduction2.7×

The raw 200-demo pile (with duplicates and 24 corrupted demos) trains to MAE 0.218. Curated down to 71 demonstrations, the policy reaches MAE 0.082 — 2.7× better with about a third of the data.

4

Enough-Data Gate

Collection has no natural stopping point, so teams over-collect — paying for demonstrations long after the policy has stopped improving — or stop too early and ship a weak skill. Nobody can see where the learning curve has flattened.

Stop collecting when the next demonstration won't move the held-out score.
elbow — demonstrations120
full budget — demonstrations400
data used at the elbow30%
accuracy gain captured77%

On this skill the elbow lands at 120 demonstrations — just 30% of the 400-demo budget — yet it captures 77% of the total achievable accuracy gain (MAE 0.072 vs the full-budget 0.034). The remaining 70% of collection buys only the last 23%.

How this was built. Real imitation experiments (scripts/data_lifecycle_experiments.py, pure NumPy) produce the numbers above. Each stage is also a small tested demo package under source-code/learning/data-lifecycle/<stage>/ — a reusable core.js carrying the rule, with node assert-tests — so the insight is a primitive you can drop onto your own collection logs, not just a figure. The twin of the safe-deploy lifecycle: that one keeps a trained policy safe, this one decides what to train it on.
