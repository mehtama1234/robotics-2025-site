1

Find the racing line

Our demo: opening a 30 m center-line corner to a 114 m racing-line radius raises corner speed from 76 to 147 km/h and clears the corner 0.73 s sooner.

Who actually hits it

Autonomous-racing programs such as the Indy Autonomous Challenge and the earlier Roborace series, plus the F1TENTH 1:10-scale racing community, all plan a global racing line before they drive. TUM's Autonomous Motorsport team, which won and podiumed at the Indy Autonomous Challenge, built and open-sourced a minimum-curvature raceline planner used across both Roborace and IAC.

What it costs when it fails

In racing there is no partial credit: a line that opens the corner even slightly less than optimal forces a lower friction-limited corner speed, which bleeds time through the corner and down the following straight, and over a lap that gap decides the race.

How it's solved in production

Production race stacks compute a global trajectory offline as an optimization — a shortest-path or minimum-curvature line, or a full minimum-lap-time optimal control problem transcribed to a nonlinear program (e.g. via CasADi/IPOPT) — as in TUM's global_racetrajectory_optimization and CommonRoad raceline planner.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo optimizes one idealized constant-radius corner with fixed grip; a real planner must handle a whole track of compound curves, a grip surface that varies with tire temperature and wear, aerodynamic downforce that changes the limit with speed, and other cars that block the ideal line.

2

Respect the friction circle

Our demo: with grip capped at 1.5 g, braking and cornering both at 100% demands 141% of the limit and slides off track; trading braking for cornering holds the demand at 100% and stays gripped.

Who actually hits it

Every controller that drives a car at the limit — the MPC stacks used in F1TENTH racing and in Indy Autonomous Challenge / Roborace vehicles — encodes the friction (or traction) circle as a hard constraint on combined longitudinal and lateral acceleration.

What it costs when it fails

The tire has one shared budget of grip; asking for full braking and full cornering at once exceeds it, and because grip combines as a vector rather than a sum the car simply slides straight off the road, spinning or running off track.

How it's solved in production

Racing controllers keep the combined demand sqrt(ax^2+ay^2) inside mu*g by design: model-predictive controllers solve a constrained optimization each cycle with the friction-circle limit built in, trading braking against cornering so the vehicle rides the edge of grip rather than the corner of the box.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses a single fixed grip limit; a real car's mu changes continuously with tire temperature, pressure, surface, load, and downforce, so production stacks add online friction and slip estimation to keep the friction circle honest as conditions shift.

3

Brake at the last metre

Our demo: braking from 80 to 30 m/s needs 187 m; braking 20 m late still carries 39 m/s and overshoots the apex by 20 m, while braking early costs 0.30 s.

Who actually hits it

Optimal braking points are the core of both human racecraft and autonomous-racing controllers — from F1TENTH and Indy Autonomous Challenge stacks to the classic motorsport technique of trail braking, where the driver carries the brake past turn-in.

What it costs when it fails

Braking too late leaves the car carrying too much speed at the apex so it runs wide and misses the corner, while braking too early sheds speed the car did not need to lose, crawling into the corner and giving away time down the next straight.

How it's solved in production

Race controllers compute the braking distance d=(v0^2-vc^2)/(2*mu*g) and place the braking point so the car arrives at corner speed exactly at the apex; trail-braking transfers weight onto the front tires as the driver eases off the brake into the turn, improving front grip and rotation.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo assumes a constant deceleration on a straight line into one corner; real braking must model brake-force build-up, tire temperature, downforce that grows the braking limit at high speed, and the reality of other cars forcing a compromised, defensive braking point.

4

Find the grip peak

Our demo: at the 8 deg peak slip angle the tire makes 1.50 g of cornering force; steering on to 16 deg past the peak drops it to 1.19 g, losing 20% of the grip while turning harder.

Who actually hits it

Anyone modeling tires at the limit relies on the Pacejka Magic Formula, the most widely used semi-empirical tire model, which autonomous-racing controllers (F1TENTH, IAC, Roborace) use to predict lateral force versus slip angle.

What it costs when it fails

Lateral tire force climbs with slip angle to a peak and then falls, so steering past the peak gives less grip, not more — the car pushes wide in understeer exactly when the driver is asking for a tighter line.

How it's solved in production

Controllers target the slip angle at the peak of the tire curve rather than maximum steering: the Pacejka formula Fy = D*sin(C*arctan(B*alpha - E*(B*alpha - arctan(B*alpha)))) captures the peak (racing slicks peak near ~6 deg, road tires nearer 10-15 deg), and staying at that peak extracts the maximum lateral g available.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses one fixed slip curve; in reality the peak and its height shift with tire temperature, wear, load, and surface, so real stacks estimate grip online and couple lateral with longitudinal slip through the combined-slip form of the model.

5

Balance the brakes

Our demo: braking lifts 1698 N off the rear axle (down to 3207 N); an equal front/rear split then demands 1.53x the rear's remaining grip and locks it, while matching bias to the dynamic loads holds the rear at 1.00x and gripped.

Who actually hits it

Brake bias is a fundamental vehicle-dynamics tuning parameter in all of motorsport and a hardware/controls concern for autonomous race cars in the Indy Autonomous Challenge and Roborace, whose braking systems must respect the load transfer built into the chassis.

What it costs when it fails

Hard braking throws weight forward and lightens the rear axle; a split that sends too much force rearward overloads the now-lightly-loaded rear tires past their reduced grip, locking them and snapping the car into a spin at the worst moment.

How it's solved in production

The brake force is biased forward to match the load transfer m*a*h/L, splitting braking in proportion to the dynamic (not static) axle loads so neither axle exceeds its own grip; engineers tune bias so the car brakes just short of lockup, since a rolling tire grips better than a sliding one.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses a single steady deceleration and fixed geometry; real cars manage bias that shifts with deceleration level, fuel load, downforce, and tire state, and modern systems add ABS and electronic brake-force distribution to chase the lockup edge dynamically.
