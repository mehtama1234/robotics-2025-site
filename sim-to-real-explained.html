01 · the problem

Real robot data is a trickle

Deep learning runs on examples, and robot learning is no exception — a policy generalizes about as far as its data covers. The trouble is that robot data is the most expensive kind there is: each demonstration takes a human on real hardware in real, slow time, and every failure risks breaking a machine or a person.

So you get a trickle where you need a flood. The whole field of sim-to-real and data engineering is one response to that single fact: rather than collect everything, manufacture the rest — randomize it, capture it, generate it, or simulate it.

the actual math — one level deeper

A policy’s generalization tracks how well its training data covers the deployment distribution; robot demos cost time × hardware × human, so real coverage is tiny.

The data engine substitutes cheaper sources — sim, generated, captured — to expand coverage without the per-sample real cost.

02 · randomize

Domain randomization: make reality just one more sample

The oldest trick, and still the workhorse. Instead of trying to make one simulator perfectly match reality (impossible), make many wildly different simulators — scramble the textures, lighting, object masses, friction, sensor noise — and train the policy across all of them.

The bet is simple: if the policy is forced to succeed under every crazy variation, then the real world — with its particular textures and physics — is just one more sample inside that range. It transfers not because the sim was accurate, but because the policy learned to stop depending on the details that varied.

the actual math — one level deeper

Train over a distribution of simulator parameters ξ ~ p(ξ) (textures, masses, friction, noise): min E_{ξ~p(ξ)}[ loss(π, sim(ξ)) ].

If the real parameters ξ_real lie inside the support of p(ξ), the real world is just another draw → zero-shot transfer.

Too little randomization → overfit to the sim; too much → a conservative policy. The width of p(ξ) is the key knob.

03 · real2sim

Real2Sim: build the simulator from reality

Hand-built simulators are expensive to author and always a little wrong. The inverse approach: capture reality and simulate in it. Photograph or scan a real scene, reconstruct it — increasingly as a Gaussian splat or mesh — and drop physics and a robot into that reconstruction.

Because the simulator now inherits real appearance and real geometry, the sim-to-real gap starts small instead of large. This is exactly where the perception work meets the data engine: the splat you built to see the world doubles as the world you train in.

the actual math — one level deeper

Reconstruct geometry + appearance from real captures (SfM → Gaussian splat / mesh), then attach physics: the rendering distribution of the sim ≈ the real camera’s.

Differentiable rendering lets you also fit physical parameters to real rollouts — system identification through the reconstruction.

04 · generate

Generate the data

The newest and fastest-growing branch: don’t collect or even simulate — generate. Foundation models already know what objects, scenes, and motions look like, so a diffusion, video, or language model can manufacture training demonstrations and whole task curricula from a handful of seeds.

This turns the bottleneck from data collection into synthesis control — how precisely you can specify what you want and filter what you get. The scale is intoxicating (millions of examples for the price of GPU time), and the catch is permanent: fake data can teach fake lessons that don’t survive contact with reality.

the actual math — one level deeper

A generative model G(prompt, seed) → (observation, action) manufactures demonstrations; train on the mix of real + generated.

The risk is a distribution gap between G’s output and reality (and G’s own biases), so generated data is filtered, verified, or co-trained with real.

05 · the gap

The gap, and whether it closed

Every technique here serves one goal: shrink the distance between where you trained (sim, or synthetic) and where you deploy (real). That distance — a distribution mismatch — is the sim-to-real gap, and it is what makes a policy that’s flawless in simulation fumble on the robot.

Three moves narrow it: randomization widens the training distribution until it covers reality; adaptation aligns the two after the fact; learned simulators fit the sim to real data directly. But the only honest scorecard is transfer: a policy that shines in sim and fails on hardware didn’t learn the task — it learned the gap.

the actual math — one level deeper

The gap is a divergence D(p_sim, p_real) between training and deployment distributions.

Randomization enlarges p_sim to contain p_real; domain adaptation aligns features φ(sim) ≈ φ(real); learned simulators minimize D by fitting p_sim to real data.

Report transfer (real-world success), never just sim success — a large sim-real gap makes sim metrics meaningless.

The benefits people actually get

Every family below chases one or more of these — the recurring reasons the field manufactures data instead of only collecting it.

Scale

Spin up thousands of scenarios — objects, layouts, lighting — without a proportional amount of real deployment. Millions of examples for the cost of compute.

Safety

Robots can fail freely in simulation: no broken hardware, no hurt people. Dangerous edge cases become cheap to rehearse.

Cost & speed

No operator hours, no lab time per sample; iterate on rewards, scenes, and hyperparameters in minutes instead of days.

01

Domain randomization

robotics
the problem

A policy trained in one simulator learns the exact quirks of that simulator — the friction value you set, the mass you guessed, the camera exposure you hard-coded. When the real robot runs the same policy, those quirks are wrong, and the policy fails. Domain randomization fixes this by training across a wide cloud of possible simulators: every episode randomizes friction between 0.4 and 1.2, mass ±20%, lighting from 200 lux to 2000 lux, and so on. The policy never memorizes any single setting, so the real world becomes just one more sample in the cloud it already knows how to handle.

the approaches

The workhorse: vary the simulator so wildly across textures, dynamics, and sensor noise that the policy stops depending on any detail that changed — so reality is just one more variation.

Recent work automates the randomization itself (an LLM designing the distribution) and estimates the hidden real parameters online.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Pick the parameters to randomize. For a cube-grasping task, we identify five levers: table friction μ ∈ [0.3, 1.0], cube mass m ∈ [50 g, 200 g], camera exposure t ∈ [1 ms, 10 ms], joint damping d ∈ [0.01, 0.1] N·m·s/rad, and background texture (10 random images).
  2. Sample one environment per episode. Episode 4,712 draws μ=0.61, m=134 g, t=3.8 ms, d=0.044, texture=forest.jpg. The policy never sees these exact numbers again — each of 50,000 episodes gets its own draw.
  3. Train until the policy generalizes. After 40 k episodes the grasp success rate across 200 held-out random environments plateaus at 91%. The policy has learned to grip firmly enough for the heavy case and gently enough for the light case.
  4. Deploy to the real robot. The real table has μ≈0.55 and the cube weighs 112 g — both inside the training cloud. On 50 real trials the robot achieves 88% success, matching the sim holdout rate within noise.
  5. Diagnose failures to tighten the range. The 6 failures all occur when the cube is placed near the table edge (position not randomized). We add a position randomization of ±4 cm and retrain; real success rises to 94%.

02

Learned / neural simulators

robotics
the problem

Physics simulators are written by hand — a human encodes friction, contact, deformation, and fluid dynamics with explicit equations. Those equations are always approximations, and the approximation error is the gap that makes sim-trained policies fail in reality. A learned or neural simulator takes a different approach: instead of writing physics by hand, it trains a neural network to predict what the world will look like next, given what it looks like now and what action was taken. The network learns the gap implicitly from real data, so it reproduces the real system’s idiosyncrasies — soft contacts, cable flex, hydraulic lag — that no human would bother to code.

the approaches

Hand-written physics is expensive and wrong in the details; instead learn the dynamics from real rollouts, often as a world model, so the sim matches the specific robot and world.

The learned simulator becomes both the training ground and, sometimes, the policy’s predictive model.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Collect a small real dataset. We run the robot arm through 2,000 random joint trajectories and record (joint angles, torques applied, next joint angles) at 500 Hz, yielding 2 M transition tuples.
  2. Train a dynamics network. A 4-layer MLP with hidden size 512 is trained to predict Δq given (q, q̇, τ). After 200 epochs the held-out prediction error is 0.003 rad — about 0.17°, well below what a hand-coded rigid-body model achieves on this flexible arm.
  3. Roll out the learned simulator for policy search. We run 10,000 simulated episodes of a peg-insertion task entirely inside the neural model, each episode 3 seconds at 50 Hz = 150 steps. Total wall time: 8 minutes on one GPU, versus 40 hours of real robot time for the same count.
  4. Transfer the trained policy. The policy succeeds on the real peg task in 78 of 100 trials. A policy trained in the hand-coded rigid-body sim achieves only 51/100 because the rigid-body model ignores the arm’s cable-driven joint flex.
  5. Iterate by adding real data. We run the transferred policy on the real robot, collect 500 new failure trajectories, retrain the neural sim on the augmented dataset, and re-run policy search. After one round, real success rises to 89/100.

Real2Sim: capture the world, simulate in it

both
the problem

Most sim-to-real effort goes in one direction: design in sim, deploy in reality. But the hardest part is making the simulation look and behave like the specific real environment you care about. Real2Sim reverses the flow: you scan or photograph the actual scene, reconstruct it as a 3-D asset, measure the real object properties, and load everything into the simulator. Now the sim is not a generic physics sandbox — it is a digital replica of your exact lab, kitchen, or factory floor. Policies trained there transfer better because they were trained on geometry and appearance that exactly matches what the camera will see at deploy time.

the approaches

Don’t author the sim — reconstruct it from real captures (Gaussian splat, mesh, radiance field) and attach physics, so the simulator inherits real looks and geometry.

This is the seam with the perception stack: the same splat that renders the scene becomes the world you train in.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Scan the target environment. We walk an iPhone 15 Pro around a kitchen counter, capturing 400 photos from varied angles. Using photogrammetry, we reconstruct a dense mesh of 1.2 M triangles and bake a 4 K texture atlas.
  2. Measure object properties. We weigh the coffee mug (310 g), measure its diameter (8.2 cm), and estimate friction by sliding it under a known force (μ≈0.38).
  3. Import into Isaac Sim. We load the mesh, set the mass and friction in the USD physics properties, and place the robot arm at the measured base position (x=0.42 m from the counter edge).
  4. Train a grasping policy in the replica sim. After 20,000 episodes the policy achieves 94% in-sim success. Because the sim geometry matches the real counter, the policy learns the exact reach distance and camera viewpoint it will encounter in reality.
  5. Deploy and measure the gap. Real-robot trials yield 89% success on the first day — a 5-point drop compared to 35+ points when using a generic sim. The remaining failures trace to lighting differences; adding a point-light at the measured fixture position closes half that gap.

Residual & hybrid: fix the gap online

robotics
the problem

A sim-trained policy is almost right. It gets the arm to the right neighborhood, opens the gripper at roughly the right time, and closes within a few millimeters of the ideal contact point. But ‘almost right’ in robotics still means ‘fails’: a 3 mm offset on a peg-insertion task is the difference between success and wedging the peg on the rim. Residual and hybrid methods accept this: they keep the approximate sim-trained policy as a fixed base and learn only a small correction — a residual — from real experience. The base policy does 95% of the work, so the residual is tiny and can be learned from far fewer real samples than training from scratch would need.

the approaches

Pure sim-to-real leaves a residual error; learn a small correction on top — from limited real interaction or human feedback — or run fast adaptive control that rejects the mismatch live.

Physics gives the bulk of the behavior; a learned residual handles what physics missed.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Measure the base policy’s systematic error. The sim-trained policy is run on the real robot for 200 peg-insertion attempts. It fails 62% of the time. Logging the final end-effector position at failure, we find a consistent bias: the gripper undershoots in x by 2.8 mm and overshoots in z by 1.1 mm due to unmodeled joint flex.
  2. Define the residual action space. The residual network will output only (Δx, Δz) corrections in the range [−5 mm, +5 mm], applied on top of the base policy’s commanded pose. The y-axis and rotation are left unchanged (base policy gets those right).
  3. Collect 200 real trials to train the residual. Each trial records the final pose error just before the peg contacts the hole rim. This is the supervision signal: we want the residual to predict the correction that would have made the contact succeed.
  4. Train a small residual network. A 2-layer MLP (128 hidden units) maps a wrist-camera patch (32×32 pixels) → (Δx, Δz). Training on 200 samples takes 4 minutes. The network learns to read the visual gap between peg and hole and output the needed nudge.
  5. Deploy base + residual together. The residual adds at most 3.5 mm of correction per step. On 200 fresh trials, success rate rises from 38% to 91%. The residual required only 200 real trials; training from scratch on real data needed 2,000 trials to reach 87%.

the problem

vision
the problem

Train on synthetic (or a source domain) and adapt to the real target without target labels — aligning features, distilling from foundation-model ‘experts’, or self-training on unlabeled real data.

Pseudo-labeling & self-training

vision
the problem

Labels are expensive. A human annotator must look at each image, draw boxes, and click through a quality check — for robotics perception tasks that can mean $3–$10 per frame and weeks of calendar time. Pseudo-labeling offers a shortcut: train a model on the labeled data you have, then run that model on unlabeled data to generate predicted labels, and train a new model on the combined real-labels + predicted-labels dataset. The predicted labels are imperfect — hence ‘pseudo’ — but if the initial model is good enough, the pseudo-labels are right often enough to provide useful training signal and expand the effective dataset size without any human annotation cost.

the approaches

Label unlabeled real data with the model’s own predictions, filter the confident ones, and retrain — bootstrapping real supervision for free, with a verifier to stop error accumulation.

The verifier / confidence filter is the difference between improvement and collapse.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Start with 1,000 hand-labeled robot workspace images. Each image has bounding boxes for gripper, target object, and obstacles. Cost: $4,500 and 3 weeks. We also have 20,000 unlabeled images collected automatically by the workspace camera over two months.
  2. Train a teacher detector on the 1,000 labeled images. A YOLOv8-medium model reaches mAP 0.72 on a 200-image held-out test set after 50 epochs. This is our teacher.

Dataset distillation & condensation

vision
the problem

Large datasets are slow and expensive to train on. If you have 1 million demonstration trajectories, training one policy epoch means loading and processing every single one. But many of those trajectories are redundant: they show the same motion from nearly the same starting configuration. Dataset distillation asks whether you can compress a large dataset into a much smaller synthetic dataset that preserves the training signal — so a model trained on the small synthetic set learns the same behavior as one trained on the original million. The synthetic examples are not random subsets; they are constructed or selected to be maximally informative, covering the distribution of the full dataset in far fewer samples.

the approaches

Compress a huge real dataset into a tiny set of synthetic super-examples that carry the same training signal — optimized to match gradients or influence, not to look realistic.

The insight: it’s information density, not sample count, that drives learning.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Start with a large dataset of 500,000 pick-and-place demonstrations. Each demo is a 50-step trajectory of (image, action) pairs at 10 Hz. The dataset takes 2.1 TB on disk. Training one full epoch takes 14 hours on 8 GPUs — too slow for rapid iteration.
  2. Embed all trajectories into a feature space. We run a pretrained vision encoder on the first and last frame of each trajectory, concatenate the embeddings (2 × 768 = 1,536 dims), and use PCA to compress to 64 dims. All 500,000 trajectories are embedded in 4 hours.
  3. Cluster the embedding space into 5,000 bins. K-means with k=5,000 partitions the 500,000 trajectories into clusters of ∼100 each. Each cluster contains trajectories that are visually and behaviorally similar.
  4. Select one representative per cluster. From each cluster we pick the trajectory closest to the centroid. This gives 5,000 representative trajectories — 1% of the original dataset — that span the full behavioral distribution.
  5. Train on the distilled set and compare. A BC policy trained on 5,000 distilled trajectories (7 min/epoch) achieves 79% real-robot success. The same architecture trained on all 500,000 achieves 83%. The distilled set captured 95% of the performance at 1% of the training compute.
  6. Use the speed gain for faster iteration. With 7-minute epochs, the team can run 10 ablations in the time it would take to finish one full training run, enabling much faster hyperparameter search and architecture experiments.

vision
the problem

More data is not always better. A dataset of one million demonstrations sounds impressive until you discover that 400,000 of them show the robot failing, 200,000 are near-duplicates of ten easy configurations, and 50,000 have corrupted sensor readings. Training on this uncurated pile teaches the model to imitate failures, overfit to common configurations, and learn noise patterns that do not appear at test time. Data curation is the discipline of selecting and weighting data so the training signal is dense and accurate. This includes filtering failed demonstrations, deduplicating similar trajectories, reweighting rare but informative situations, and verifying label quality — all before training begins.

the approaches

Not all data is worth training on; identify the high-utility, low-redundancy samples — by influence, uncertainty, or curriculum — so a curated subset matches training on everything.

As synthetic data floods in, curation is what keeps the signal from drowning in redundancy.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Audit a raw dataset of 80,000 demonstrations. We define ‘success’ as: object reaches goal ± 1 cm, no collisions, gripper opens at the right time. Running this check on all 80,000 demos (via simulator replay) takes 4 hours and flags 31,200 (39%) as failures. We discard them, leaving 48,800.
  2. Deduplicate near-identical trajectories. We embed each trajectory and compute pairwise cosine similarity. Any pair with similarity > 0.97 keeps only one. This removes 12,400 near-duplicates, leaving 36,400 distinct trajectories.
  3. Identify underrepresented configurations. We cluster the 36,400 remaining demos into 200 bins by object start position. Bin sizes range from 12 to 890 demos. The 40 bins with fewer than 50 demos each cover edge-of-workspace positions that are hardest for the policy.
  4. Reweight the training loss. We assign each demo a weight inversely proportional to its bin size: a demo in a 12-demo bin gets weight 890/12 = 74×, while a demo in the 890-demo bin gets weight 1×. This oversamples rare configurations without collecting new data.
  5. Train and compare to the raw baseline. Policy trained on 80,000 raw demos: 71% real success, with 41% on edge-of-workspace objects. Policy trained on 36,400 curated + reweighted demos: 84% overall, 79% on edge cases. Curation turned less data into better coverage.

Test-time adaptation

vision
the problem

A policy trained in simulation or on a fixed real dataset is frozen at deployment time. But the real world is not frozen: lighting changes between morning and afternoon, surfaces wear down, actuator springs weaken over months, and the object you are asked to pick up today is slightly different from the one in your training data. Test-time adaptation lets the policy keep learning during deployment, updating a small subset of its weights based on signals it can compute without any human labels — for example, minimizing a self-supervised prediction error, matching the statistics of its own feature activations to a stored reference, or asking a critic network to score the current behavior. The adaptation happens in real time, trial by trial or frame by frame.

the approaches

Close the last of the gap at deployment: adapt the model online to the real test distribution using only unlabeled test data, no retraining, no labels.

The final, cheapest safety net when the deployment domain drifts from training.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Identify the adaptation signal. Our policy includes a masked autoencoder head: during training, we randomly mask 40% of input image patches and train the encoder to reconstruct them. The reconstruction loss is available at test time with no labels needed. When the policy encounters a new visual domain, reconstruction loss rises — a free signal that something changed.
  2. Detect distribution shift on day 1 of deployment. The robot is moved to a new factory with yellow LED lighting (training was under white fluorescent). Reconstruction loss jumps from a training baseline of 0.031 to 0.089 on the first 20 frames — a 2.9× increase. This triggers the adaptation loop.
  3. Adapt the batch normalization statistics. We update only the running mean and variance in the encoder’s 12 batch-norm layers, using exponential moving average over the live camera stream (α=0.01 per frame). No gradients are computed; update takes 0.3 ms per frame.
  4. Monitor reconstruction loss during adaptation. After 200 frames (≈7 seconds at 30 Hz), reconstruction loss drops back to 0.038 — near the training baseline. The encoder has re-calibrated its feature statistics to the new lighting without any human intervention.
  5. Measure task performance before and after adaptation. On the first 50 trials in the new factory (before adaptation): 54% grasp success. On the next 50 trials (after 200-frame warm-up): 83% success. Adaptation recovered 29 points without any new labeled data, purely from the self-supervised reconstruction signal.

Part IV

Where it stands

The data engine is what makes modern robot learning possible — and its failure modes are subtle. What is still hard:

Fake gains that don’t transfer. A policy can ace sim or synthetic benchmarks and still fail on hardware — measuring real transfer, not sim scores, is the only honest test.
Systematic sim bias. Randomization hides but doesn’t remove biases baked into the simulator or the generator; some gaps no amount of variation closes.
Generator artifacts poison data. Synthetic data inherits the generator’s errors and biases; unfiltered, they silently corrupt the student.
Contact & the hardest physics. Friction, deformation, and contact transitions are exactly where hand-built and even learned sims stay least accurate.
Forgetting the prior. Over-fitting a big pretrained model to a little synthetic data can erode the broad knowledge you started with.
How much real is enough?. No clean theory yet for the right mix of real vs synthetic, or how much real data it takes to certify a policy.
