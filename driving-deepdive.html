The big picture — connecting the dots

Strip away the acronyms and every paper on this page is chipping at a single stubborn problem: a car has to understand a world that is bigger, faster, and less certain than its sensors and its computer can comfortably handle — and being wrong means a crash. That one sentence hides four pressures that fight each other:

  • Accuracy — see what is actually there, including the sides you cannot see and the distance a flat photo does not give you.
  • Cost — do it fast enough and cheap enough to run on a car, not a server farm.
  • Honesty — know when you are unsure, so you can be careful in exactly the right places.
  • Robustness — keep working when it rains, when a camera dies, when you are in a city you never trained on.

These trade against one another. You can buy accuracy with more compute, but then you lose speed. You can bolt on sensors for robustness, but then fusing them cleanly gets hard. Every paper here is one specific trade someone found to move one pressure without wrecking the others. Grouped by which pressure they push on:

① Spend compute only where it matters. SparseDrive throws away the dense map of empty space and keeps a handful of tokens — one per real object or piece of road — so detection, tracking, mapping and planning all happen in one cheap pass. LightStereo does the same thinning for depth-from-two-cameras, shrinking the heavy 3D matching down to something a car can run live.

② Add a sensor that sees what cameras cannot — then fuse it well. Cameras are cheap but bad at distance and blind in fog; radar is cheap, sees through weather, and measures speed directly, but is noisy. HyDRa and UniBEVFusion fuse radar and camera twice — once to fix the camera's depth guess, once in the top-down map — and UniBEVFusion deliberately tests what happens when the camera fails. Talk2Radar stretches radar further, letting you point at objects with plain words. HeRCULES and PoLaRIS are the honest bookkeeping — datasets that let you measure whether any of this holds up across weathers, radar types, and even out on the water.

③ Follow things through time, and admit what you do not know. A single snapshot is not enough — you have to keep each object's identity across frames. MCTrack does that with one clean pipeline that works on several datasets, splitting the math so speed and position estimates do not corrupt each other. MonoCT faces the ugly truth that a detector trained in one place fails in another — and instead of pretending, it scores its own auto-generated labels and keeps only the ones it trusts.

④ Make the data you cannot collect. Crashes, blizzards, rare corners — too dangerous or too rare to film. AutoSplat reconstructs real driving scenes into editable 3D you can re-render from new angles, turning a handful of drives into many.

The throughline: nobody is trying to build one giant brain that does everything perfectly. Each paper finds a narrow place to be smarter — a leaner representation, a tougher sensor pairing, a way to be honest about doubt — so the whole stack becomes fast enough, accurate enough, and careful enough to trust on a real road. The hands-on run further down (SparseDrive) is bet ① taken all the way from source code to a live prediction you can watch.

The problem — in depth

A car driving itself has one job that never stops: figure out what is around it, decide what to do, and move the car safely. The sensing part — figuring out what is around it — is where most of this research lives, and it is genuinely hard.

The core difficulty is that the world around a moving car is big, fast-changing, and full of things that matter at different levels. A parked truck a hundred meters away matters. A child stepping off a curb two meters away matters more and right now. A system that tries to keep perfect track of everything — every centimeter of road, every building face, every other vehicle — burns through computing power so fast that it cannot run at the speed the car needs. And if it is too slow, it is useless, or worse, dangerous.

There is also the question of what the sensors can and cannot see. Laser scanners (called lidar) give precise 3D measurements but leave gaps — they cannot see around corners, they miss the sides of objects, and their scans are thin and sparse. Cameras see color and texture but do not directly measure distance; figuring out how far away something is from a flat image is a hard math problem. Radar bounces radio waves off objects and works in fog, rain, and darkness where cameras and lidar fail, but its output is noisier and less sharp.

All of these problems connect back to the same big challenge every robot faces: the world is unclear and always changing, computing is limited, and the cost of getting it wrong is a real crash with real people. The research in this topic is about making sensing cheaper to run, more accurate in bad conditions, more honest about what it does not know, and better connected to the decisions the car needs to make.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Drop the dense map; track only what matters

In plain termsInstead of describing every inch of the street, the car keeps a sticky note only on each thing that actually matters — this car, that lane — and ignores all the empty space in between.
The sub-problemUnderstanding a whole street means representing everything around the car — but filling a dense grid of every cell of space is far too slow to run live. So how do you understand the scene without paying for all the empty road and sky?
Sparse tokens — SparseDrive (ICRA). Represent the scene as a small set of tokens, one per object or piece of road rather than one per grid cell, and detect, track, and map them in a single pass. Because it never fills a huge grid, it runs fast enough for real roads.
Symbolic fast-paths — Chameleon (ICRA). Takes one narrow sub-problem — how lanes connect at an intersection — and runs cheap symbolic rules for the common cases, only calling a heavier model when the situation is unusual.

Work only with what matters and the computation shrinks enough to run live on the car, not just in a lab.

Mix radar with cameras to work in bad weather

In plain termsThe camera sees clearly on a good day; radar sees through fog and rain. Use both and the car is covered whichever way the weather turns.
The sub-problemCameras can't judge distance well, especially far away; lidar is precise but expensive and struggles in heavy rain; radar is cheap and sees through weather but returns a sparse cloud of dots, not a sharp image. No single sensor is enough exactly when conditions turn bad — so combine them.
Two-stage fusion — HyDRa (ICRA). First uses radar depth readings to help the camera estimate depth from its own image, then merges both streams again in a flat top-down bird's-eye-view map of the surroundings.
Fusion tested under failure — UniBEVFusion (ICRA). The same two-stage idea, but it explicitly tests what happens when the camera fails — the case that matters most for safety.
Radar from language — Talk2Radar (ICRA). Shows radar can support finding objects from spoken/written descriptions, not just plain detection.
A shared testbed — HeRCULES dataset (ICRA). Lets researchers test radar systems across weather, time of day, and two different radar types together.

A car fusing radar with cameras and lidar keeps sensing accurately in exactly the conditions where it most needs to.

Keep track of uncertainty — know what you do not know

In plain termsThe car doesn't just guess where things are — it also keeps track of how sure it is, and drives extra carefully exactly where it's least sure.
The sub-problemA car that is unsure where something is should drive more carefully near that spot — but most systems output a single answer ("this object is here") with no signal of how sure they are, so when they're wrong the car never knows to slow down.
Trust-scored labels — MonoCT (ICRA). Single-camera 3D detection loses accuracy in unfamiliar settings; MonoCT has a teacher label the new setting but scores how consistent those labels are, keeping only the ones it trusts.
Correct only the uncertain part — learned MPC (ICRA). Splits the car's motion model into the well-understood parts and the uncertain parts, using a small network to fix only the uncertain bits.
Continuous uncertainty — Ground-Optimized Radar-Inertial Odometry (ICRA). Uses Gaussian-Process regression to blend noisy sensor streams smoothly rather than at fixed time steps, so uncertainty propagates continuously.

Knowing what you don't know is what lets a car be careful in exactly the right places.

Generate data to fill gaps in training

In plain termsYou can't crash a thousand real cars to teach a robot about crashes — so you make believable fake ones on a computer, including the rare, dangerous cases you'd never want to stage for real.
The sub-problemLearning systems need mountains of labeled data, real driving data is expensive to collect, and the situations that matter most — crashes, heavy snow — are the rarest of all. You cannot collect your way to those, so generate them.
Text → lidar scenes — LidarDM (ICRA). Generates a whole driving scene from a text description, then simulates what a real lidar scanner would see inside it — realistic data without driving anywhere.
Synthetic crashes — AVD2 (ICRA). Generates realistic accident videos from written descriptions, so systems can train on crashes without any real crash footage.
Rendered picking data — Synthetica (IROS). Renders millions of robot-picking images with a high-quality renderer, so perception trains without months of real photography.
Complete the scene — DiffSSC (IROS). Starts from a sparse lidar scan and fills in the hidden geometry and object types the scanner missed.

Shift the cost from collecting the world to generating it — far cheaper, and you can make exactly the hard cases you need.

Connect vehicles so each one sees more

In plain termsOne car can only see what's in front of it. Two cars that share what they see can spot the kid stepping out from behind a parked truck that neither could have seen alone.
The sub-problemOne car only sees what its own sensors reach. Two cars sharing views can together see around corners and past obstructions — but sending data between cars has to be fast and lightweight or it creates new problems of its own.
Share only what helps — CoCMT (IROS). Instead of sending full sensor streams, it identifies the small set of features most useful to share and sends only those.
Share predictions too — Co-MTP (ICRA). Over a vehicle-to-everything (V2X) network, shares not just what each car currently sees but where it predicts nearby vehicles are going — improving everyone's forecasts.
Robust to weather — V2X-DGW (ICRA). Trains the shared perception system on artificially degraded data so it still works when real rain or fog hits.

Sending compressed, carefully chosen features rather than raw data is what makes cooperative perception practical.

Where it stands

The clearest progress is in making perception faster and more efficient: sparse representations and smarter sensor fusion have cut computing costs enough that self-driving systems can run in real time on real hardware. Mixing radar with cameras has meaningfully improved performance in bad weather. What remains open is robustness when things go truly wrong — an untrained situation, a sensor failure, an edge case no dataset covered. The systems are also still largely tested domain by domain; a detector trained in one city or weather type still struggles when moved to another, and closing that gap fully is unsolved.

SparseDrive: End-to-End Autonomous Driving via Sparse Scene Representation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A self-driving car needs to detect objects, track them over time, map the road, and plan its own path. Dense systems build a complete grid map of the entire scene around the car—expensive in compute and memory—so they can only run in labs, not on the car itself.

The approach. Represent the scene as a small set of tokens (50–100 tokens total), one per real object or road element, not one per grid cell. Run detection, tracking, mapping, and planning through one unified network that reads and refines those same sparse tokens in a single pass.

What's unique. The sparse representation itself becomes the shared backbone for all four tasks at once, so planning sees exactly what perception found—there's no lossy hand-off between separate modules, and the savings compound (10× fewer tokens means 10× faster).

ICRA 2025 · 30 citations · end-to-end driving

What it does — 2-3 plain sentences

SparseDrive is an end-to-end autonomous driving system that replaces dense grid-based scene maps with a sparse token-based representation, unifying detection, tracking, and online mapping into a single symmetric network. Instead of predicting fixed trajectories and then selecting one, it generates multiple candidate driving paths in parallel and uses a collision-aware safety scoring module to pick the safest and most feasible trajectory. This design is both faster and safer than prior work: it trains in 20–30 hours (vs. 40+ hours for dense methods) and achieves lower collision rates on the nuScenes benchmark.

Sub-approach: end-to-end (from sensors directly to driving output via a unified learned pipeline)

The method, in detail — step by step

Inputs

  • Multi-view camera images from a vehicle's front, side, and rear cameras
  • No lidar or radar; vision-only approach (making it cheaper than lidar-based systems)

Symmetric Sparse Perception Module

  • Encoding: Multi-view images are encoded into feature maps using a backbone network
  • Sparse initialization: A small, fixed set of learnable tokens (sparse instances) are created, one per detected object
  • Temporal tracking: An instance memory queue stores the state of each object across frames; new detections are matched to previous tokens via learned similarity, so the system knows which tokens correspond to the same car or pedestrian over time
  • Unified output: A single transformer-based decoder processes these sparse tokens and simultaneously outputs:

- Object bounding boxes and class labels (detection)

- Object velocity for the next few frames (tracking)

- Map elements (lane markings, road edges) also represented as sparse tokens (online mapping)

The key word is "symmetric": instead of separate detection-then-tracking pipelines, one unified architecture handles all three tasks with the same sparse token representation.

Instance Memory Queue

  • Maintains a queue of past object states (position, velocity, class)
  • Allows the network to reason about temporal patterns: "this car has been moving left, so it will continue moving left"
  • Improves trajectory prediction by grounding it in actual observed history

Parallel Motion Planner

  • Motion prediction: Using the sparse perception output and memory queue, predict 6 possible future trajectories for the ego vehicle (the driving car) over the next 6 seconds
  • Multi-modal planning: Each trajectory is a different driving option (lane change left, go straight, turn right, etc.)
  • Collision-aware rescore: Before selecting, score each trajectory with a collision-safety check:

- For each candidate trajectory, check if the ego vehicle path intersects with predicted paths of other objects (cars, pedestrians)

- Penalize trajectories that risk collision

- Rank by safety first, then by other criteria (smoothness, efficiency)

  • Selection: Pick the highest-scoring safe trajectory and output steering/acceleration commands

The Specific Trick That Makes It Work

Sparse tokens + symmetric architecture = fast and interpretable. Dense BEV methods build a complete overhead map of every location in a 200m×200m area, which is computationally expensive. SparseDrive only maintains features for detected objects and landmarks—typically 50–100 tokens vs. 100,000+ grid cells. This reduces memory and compute by 10× while keeping end-to-end differentiability. The symmetric design (one architecture for detection, tracking, mapping) eliminates the need for separate specialized heads and reduces training complexity.

Collision-aware rescore = safety. End-to-end driving normally relies on the model learning safety through examples. SparseDrive adds an explicit safety layer: if a candidate trajectory would collide with a predicted object, its score is heavily penalized *before* final selection. This is a hybrid approach: learned trajectory generation + explicit safety constraint.

Training Setup

  • Loss function: Combination of detection loss (bounding box + class), tracking loss (velocity prediction), map loss (lane detection), and trajectory loss (imitation learning from human driving data, plus a safety regularizer)
  • Datasets: Trained and evaluated on nuScenes (1,000 sequences from Boston and Singapore)
  • Hardware: GPU required (multi-GPU training, 20–30 hours for Small/Base models)

LightStereo: Channel Boost Is All You Need for Efficient 2D Cost Aggregation

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Stereo depth (judging distance from two camera views) requires matching pixels left-to-right, which creates a bulky cost volume. Shrinking it with 3D convolutions is expensive; traditional methods either run slow or lose detail where depth is uncertain (sky, distance, occlusions).

The approach. Build the cost volume with 2D operations instead of 3D: expand the channels first (add more feature maps), apply lightweight depthwise convolution to each channel independently, then project back down. This gives the refinement of bulky 3D methods without the compute cost.

What's unique. Channel boosting—expanding feature channels before refinement—replaces expensive 3D operations, making stereo depth run in 17ms on embedded hardware. Prior work used costly 4D structures; this finds that the width-expansion trick provides the same detail at a fraction of the cost.

ICRA 2025 · 16 citations · perception-fusion (stereo depth from 2D cost aggregation)

Live run · we actually ran it

Two camera images in → a depth map out

Stereo depth is how humans judge distance: two eyes see slightly different views, and the horizontal shift of each point between them (its disparity) is inversely proportional to how far away it is. LightStereo does this cheaply — the paper's whole point is an efficient 2D cost-aggregation design. We ran the authors' pretrained (KITTI-finetuned) checkpoint on their sample stereo pair on an L4: top = the left camera image; bottom = LightStereo's predicted depth, warm = near, cool = far.

Read it as a ramp: the road right in front of the car is red (near), fading through magenta to deep blue at the trees and horizon (far). The pedestrian and pole on the left, the parked cars, the curb and sign posts all come out as distinct depth shapes — from nothing but the left/right pixel shift, no depth sensor. The one messy patch (top-center) is the textureless sky gap, where every stereo method struggles because there is no detail to match between the two views.

Note: depth quality depends on matching the checkpoint to the image domain — the model's SceneFlow (synthetic-trained) weights gave a noisy result on this real photo; the KITTI-finetuned weights (shown) are clean. Same tradeoff the theme of cheap, fast, reliable seeing is all about.

What it does

LightStereo computes depth from stereo camera pairs by building a small 3D structure (cost volume) that records how well the left and right images match at different horizontal offsets, then applies a lightweight channel-boosting trick to recover the detail that traditional bulky 4D methods provide. This is a perception-fusion approach: it fuses information across the disparity (depth) dimension using 2D convolutions instead of expensive 3D convolutions, making it 17 ms fast on real-time embedded hardware while competing with slower state-of-the-art methods.

The method, in detail

Input: Stereo image pair (left and right camera views of the same scene) at 384×1280 resolution.

Step 1: Feature extraction. A lightweight MobileNetV2 backbone processes both images independently to extract multi-scale feature maps (at 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, and 1/32 spatial resolution).

Step 2: Cost volume computation. The cost volume is a 3D structure with shape (height, width, disparity_range). At each spatial pixel location (x, y), the algorithm computes a similarity score (using normalized dot-product correlation) between the left image feature and the right image feature at all possible horizontal offsets (disparities). This is the matching cost: higher cost means better match, and the offset with highest cost is the depth at that pixel.

Step 3: The channel-boost trick—the core innovation. Traditional methods would aggregate this cost using a 4D structure, which requires heavy 3D convolutions (computationally expensive). Instead, LightStereo:

  • Expands the channels: applies a 1×1 convolution to boost the cost volume from 1 channel to many (e.g., 8 or 16).
  • Processes with depthwise convolution: uses a 3×3 depthwise convolution (one filter per channel, independent) to refine each channel without mixing them, capturing local spatial patterns in the cost landscape.
  • Projects back down: applies another 1×1 convolution to return to 1 channel while retaining the learned refinements.

Step 4: Multi-scale attention (MSCA). A parallel attention module uses elongated kernels (1×7, 7×1, 1×21, 21×1) that sweep across the image horizontally to capture long-range dependencies. These learned patterns guide which cost values to trust and are combined with the cost volume via element-wise multiplication.

Step 5: Disparity regression. A soft-argmax operation converts the refined cost volume (now a dense set of similarity scores at each pixel) into a continuous disparity value (horizontal pixel offset) that directly represents depth.

Training setup: LightStereo is trained end-to-end on the SceneFlow synthetic dataset (35,454 image pairs) for 90 epochs using Smooth L1 loss (measures absolute difference between predicted and ground-truth disparity) and the AdamW optimizer with a one-cycle learning rate schedule. After 90 epochs, the model is fine-tuned on real KITTI stereo pairs for 500 more epochs. Augmentation includes random crops, color jittering, random erasing, and scale variations.

Unleashing HyDRa: Hybrid Fusion, Depth Consistency and Radar for Unified 3D Perception

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Cameras alone struggle to judge depth at distance and fail in fog or darkness. Radar sees through weather and measures speed directly but has no height information. Most fusion methods treat them as separate streams and merge late; depth errors propagate and never get corrected.

The approach. Fuse radar and camera twice: first in camera perspective view (where radar depth readings help the camera's depth guesses), then again in the bird's-eye-view map. This two-stage alignment lets radar guide depth everywhere it's weak, especially in rain and darkness.

What's unique. By aligning radar and camera early in perspective view before lifting to bird's-eye, fine-grained spatial detail is preserved. Radar's height-insensitive but precise distance measurements become a direct guide for camera depth, avoiding the usual strategy of treating radar as sparse lidar.

ICRA 2025 · 13 citations · perception-fusion

What it does

HyDRa fuses camera images and 4D radar point clouds into a unified 3D scene representation by combining them in two stages: first in the camera's original 2D perspective view (where a transformer aligns radar depth with image features), then again in a bird's-eye-view overhead map (where radar guides depth refinement). This is a perception-fusion approach—it replaces dense bird's-eye-view grids with smarter multimodal fusion to detect and track 3D objects and predict what surfaces are occupied, especially when lighting is bad or weather is harsh.

The method, in detail

Inputs and preprocessing:

The system ingests six camera images from multiple viewpoints and a 4D radar point cloud (which gives position plus radial velocity). The camera backbone extracts multi-resolution feature maps. The radar points are encoded into "pillars"—vertical stacks—each capturing height distribution at that position.

Height Association Transformer (HAT):

Radar lacks elevation information; cameras lack precise depth at range. HAT bridges this by:

  • For each image column (width position), the system extracts "discrete monocular height features" from the camera decoder
  • These are paired with radar pillar features at matching positions using self-attention and cross-attention operations
  • A zero-initialized layer norm stabilizes training; outputs are added residually to the original depth features

Result: geometry-aware depth distributions that outperform either sensor alone.

Bird's-eye-view projection:

Camera features are lifted into overhead 2D coordinates using the predicted depth distribution. Radar pillar channels are concatenated alongside. A squeeze-and-excitation layer performs lightweight per-channel fusion. Optional temporal fusion stacks the previous eight frames.

Radar-weighted Depth Consistency (RDC):

The BEV representation is refined by enforcing consistency: forward projection (image → BEV) and backward projection (BEV → image) should agree on depth. The consistency weight is computed as the scalar product between the predicted depth distribution and implicit query depth. A 3×3 convolution applied to radar-only BEV features produces attention weights. Deformable attention then samples features dynamically around projection points, weighted by both depth consistency and radar attention.

Task-specific heads:

The fused BEV features feed into separate heads for 3D object detection, multi-object tracking, and occupancy prediction. Each head uses standard losses from prior work (detection, tracking, semantic segmentation).

Training:

20 epochs on nuScenes or Occ3D with AdamW optimizer, batch size 64, and memory-efficient sequential sampling. Data augmentation follows BEVDet baseline. No test-time augmentation.

Why it works:

By deferring BEV projection until after camera-radar alignment in perspective view, the method preserves fine-grained spatial detail. Radar's height-insensitive but precise distance measurements guide camera's depth everywhere, especially in darkness or rain. The two-stage fusion (perspective + BEV) avoids treating radar as a sparse lidar—it exploits radar's unique 4D signature.

AutoSplat: Constrained Gaussian Splatting for Autonomous Driving Scene Reconstruction

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Testing self-driving algorithms requires replicating rare, dangerous, or hard-to-find scenarios (crashes, heavy snow, edge cases). You can't collect your way to these, and simulation is slow and unrealistic. You need a way to turn a few real drives into many synthetic scenarios.

The approach. Reconstruct actual driving scenes as 3D point clouds (Gaussians) that can be re-rendered from new viewpoints. Add geometric constraints so the road stays flat, the sky stays distant, and vehicles match their physical shape—not free-form splotches.

What's unique. Standard 3D Gaussian Splatting ignores physics (floats the road, scatters the sky). AutoSplat keeps geometry real by enforcing that road and sky fit expected planes, and vehicles match CAD priors or reflection consistency, turning one real drive into many photo-realistic variations.

ICRA 2025 · 9 citations · scene-sim (Gaussian splatting-based 3D scene reconstruction)

What it does

AutoSplat reconstructs driving scenes as cloud of colored point blobs called Gaussians, enabling fast photorealistic rendering and novel view synthesis for safety-critical simulation. It addresses a key limitation of standard 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS)—which fails on dynamic driving scenarios—by imposing geometric constraints to keep the background (road, sky) physically consistent across multiple viewpoints and handling moving foreground objects (vehicles) with template-based reconstruction. This is a scene-sim approach: building simulated driving environments for testing and validation rather than detecting or tracking individual objects.

The method, in detail

Input. Camera frames and LiDAR point clouds from a moving vehicle (Pandaset or KITTI datasets). The system consumes multi-view sequences with sparse depth (from LiDAR) and dense image color.

Core 3D Gaussian Splatting idea. Instead of storing a dense voxel grid or mesh, represent the scene as thousands of small Gaussian "splats"—each is a 3D ellipsoid with a position, covariance matrix (size and orientation), and learned color (via spherical harmonics). During rendering, these Gaussians are projected onto the camera image, and their contributions are summed to produce final pixel colors. The key advantage: rendering is differentiable and extremely fast (real-time).

Why standard 3DGS fails on driving scenes. Unconstrained 3DGS optimizes per-viewpoint consistency but does not enforce physical realism: road surfaces can "float," sky blobs can scatter, and foreground objects (like vehicles) lose consistency across large viewpoint changes. This ruins realism for simulation.

AutoSplat's four key tricks:

  • Geometric constraints on road and sky regions. Segment the image into semantic masks (road, sky, dynamic objects) using Mask2Former. Then add a geometric loss during Gaussian optimization: penalize Gaussians labeled "road" if they deviate from a locally-flat plane, and "sky" Gaussians if they deviate from a fixed distance. This keeps the background geometrically plausible.
  • Reflected Gaussian consistency for vehicles. Foreground objects (cars, pedestrians) are represented using 3D CAD templates or bounding-box priors. For each object, construct a constraint that ensures the visible side of the Gaussian cloud matches observations *and* the occluded (hidden) side is rendered as a mirror reflection. This forces the model to predict surfaces correctly even where cameras cannot see.
  • Temporally-dependent residual spherical harmonics for dynamic appearance. Rather than fix each Gaussian's color, learn time-dependent residual corrections to the spherical harmonics (color basis functions). This allows a single Gaussian to appear with slightly different colors and shading as the vehicle's pose or lighting changes across the sequence.
  • LiDAR initialization. Instead of using Structure-from-Motion (which fails on textureless road), initialize Gaussians at accumulated LiDAR point clouds. This grounds the scene geometry in real depth before optimization.

Training setup. Split optimization into three phases: (i) background only (30K iterations), (ii) foreground objects (5K iterations), (iii) fused refinement of both (10K iterations). Loss function is reconstruction error (L1 + SSIM on image pixels) plus geometric constraints and consistency terms. Single Tesla V100 GPU with 32 GB memory. Learning is end-to-end via PyTorch-based differentiable rasterizer.

Output. A sparse set of learned Gaussians (~1–2 million per scene) that can render novel camera viewpoints in real-time and be directly inspected for realism. Scene is immediately usable for photo-realistic simulation—e.g., rendering lane-change scenarios for testing detection or planning algorithms.

Talk2Radar: Bridging Natural Language with 4D mmWave Radar for 3D Referring Expression Comprehension

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Finding a specific object in a 3D scene using radar is hard—radar returns a sparse point cloud with no image context. You can ask a human "find the car to my left," but a machine needs to ground language in raw millimeter-wave data, which lacks color, texture, or orientation cues.

The approach. Encode the radar point cloud and text prompt separately, then fuse them with attention: for each radar point, learn which text tokens matter ("car", "left", "moving fast"), and score whether that point belongs to the target object. Regression predicts the 3D box.

What's unique. Gated graph fusion lets radar points selectively attend to language semantics rather than processing all radar points equally. Deformable sampling adapts to sparse radar instead of forcing it into dense grids—radar's native irregularity becomes an asset, not a problem.

ICRA 2025 · 7 citations · multimodal perception (perception-fusion)

What it does

Talk2Radar lets a robot or car find a specific object in 3D space using spoken or typed descriptions ("find the car to my left") by processing 4D millimeter-wave radar point clouds combined with natural language. It is a multimodal perception system that performs 3D Referring Expression Comprehension, bridging language and radar data for interactive scene understanding—a sub-approach of perception-fusion, where two sensor modalities (text and radar) are fused to ground language in 3D geometry.

The method, in detail

Inputs: 4D millimeter-wave (mmWave) radar point clouds (dense 3D point sets with height information and Doppler velocity) and natural language text describing a target object (e.g., "the small red car moving fast to the right").

Architecture – T-RadarNet:

  • Point Cloud Feature Extraction (Deformable-FPN)

The sparse and irregular radar point cloud is processed by a Deformable Feature Pyramid Network (FPN). Unlike fixed sampling grids, Deformable-FPN learns adaptive, learnable sample locations to extract multi-scale features from the point cloud. This produces hierarchical feature representations at different spatial scales, capturing both fine local details and broad scene structure.

  • Text Embedding

The natural language prompt is encoded into a dense embedding vector using a pre-trained text encoder (typically BERT or similar), capturing semantic meaning.

  • Cross-Modal Fusion (Gated Graph Fusion)

The point cloud features and text embedding are aligned and merged using a Gated Graph Fusion module. This module computes attention-based interactions between text features and radar point features, using gating mechanisms to weight which radar points are most relevant to the language query. The fusion operates on a graph where nodes are radar detections and edges represent spatial and semantic relationships.

  • 3D Bounding Box Regression

The fused features are passed to a regression head that predicts a 3D bounding box (center, size, orientation) for the object matching the description.

Training Setup:

Supervised learning on the Talk2Radar dataset (8,682 prompt–object pairs with 20,558 labeled objects). Loss combines classification loss (does this object match the prompt?) and box regression loss (is the predicted 3D box accurate?).

The trick that makes it work: The Gated Graph Fusion mechanism allows the model to selectively weight radar points based on language semantics, avoiding the problem of naive concatenation where irrelevant radar features could mislead the model. Deformable sampling adapts to sparse, irregular radar data instead of forcing it into dense grids.

MCTrack: A Unified 3D Multi-Object Tracking Framework for Autonomous Driving

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A tracker needs to link detected objects across frames—but linking boxes based on distance works poorly when depth estimation is wrong. Most trackers struggle across datasets (KITTI, nuScenes, Waymo), each requiring dataset-specific tuning.

The approach. Use three separate Kalman filters instead of one: position tracking handles (x, y, z) with motion model; size tracking watches (width, length) without drift; heading tracking blends perceived angle with velocity direction. Link detections to tracks using a hybrid cost that combines overlap (IoU) and heading alignment, then refine mismatches in two passes (bird's-eye-view first, then depth-refiner second).

What's unique. Decoupling the Kalman filters prevents numerical instability when mixing different units and scales. Two-stage matching (BEV then range view) catches depth errors that single-pass linking misses. One format (BaseVersion) unifies all datasets, so the algorithm works unchanged on KITTI, nuScenes, and Waymo.

IROS 2025 · 7 citations · detection-tracking approach

What it does

MCTrack is a 3D object tracking system that follows cars, pedestrians, and cyclists across multiple video frames in autonomous driving scenes. Instead of building separate tracking solutions for each dataset (KITTI, nuScenes, Waymo), it uses one unified pipeline that works equally well on all three, and it introduces new metrics to measure whether the estimated motion (speed and direction) is correct for downstream planning tasks.

Sub-approach: detection-tracking — takes pre-computed 3D bounding boxes (detections) from a perception model and links them across frames to maintain consistent object identities.

The method, in detail

Input: 3D bounding boxes from any detector, organized by frame number. Each box contains position, size, and orientation in world coordinates (not camera view).

BaseVersion Format: Before tracking starts, all datasets are converted to a common format that stores detection results as: scene ID, frame sequence, position in 3D space (x, y, z), dimensions (length, width, height), heading angle (yaw) plus velocity and acceleration. This eliminates dataset-specific preprocessing—researchers can focus purely on tracking logic.

Three Separate Kalman Filters (the key trick): Most trackers put all variables into one filter and run into numerical issues when mixing different units and coordinate systems. MCTrack decouples them:

  • *Position filter* (x, y, z coordinates): models constant acceleration motion
  • *Size filter* (length, width): uses constant velocity model
  • *Heading filter* (yaw angle): tracks both the perceived heading and the heading implied by velocity direction, then blends them

This separation makes the math cleaner and more stable across diverse driving scenarios.

Ro_GDIoU Cost Function: To link detections to existing tracks, you need a similarity score. MCTrack uses a hybrid metric that combines two ideas: intersection-over-union (IoU, how much boxes overlap) and center-distance measures (how close box centers are). The formula incorporates heading angle too, so it penalizes boxes that point in different directions. This catches cases where depth estimation is wrong by 10 meters or more.

Two-Stage Matching: Linking detections to tracks happens in two passes:

  • *Stage 1 (Bird's-Eye View)* — Project boxes onto the flat top-down plane and match them using the Hungarian algorithm with the Ro_GDIoU cost. This handles most matches because depth is the hardest thing to estimate.
  • *Stage 2 (Range View)* — For any detections or tracks left unmatched, project them onto a depth-distance plane and greedily re-match. This catches linking failures caused by bad depth estimates.

Each stage also blends forward (predicting where a track should be) and backward (looking at detections from the previous frame) predictions to reduce ID switches.

Training: The method is offline—no neural network training. It processes pre-computed detections, applies filtering and NMS (non-maximum suppression) to remove low-confidence or overlapping boxes, then runs Kalman filtering and matching. Different object types (car, pedestrian, cyclist) get different lifecycle parameters (how long to keep a track alive with no detection, when to kill a bad track).

Motion Metrics (new contribution): Beyond standard tracking scores (MOTA, HOTA), the paper proposes five new metrics to measure motion accuracy:

  • VAE (Velocity Angle Error) — is the estimated velocity direction correct?
  • VNE (Velocity Norm Error) — is the speed magnitude correct?
  • VDE (Velocity Delay Error) — does the velocity lag behind or lead the actual motion?
  • VSE (Velocity Smoothness Error) — is the velocity estimate noisy?
  • VIR (Velocity Inversion Ratio) — how often is velocity pointed the wrong way?

These matter because planning modules need accurate motion to predict where objects will be in 2–3 seconds.

MonoCT: Overcoming Monocular 3D Detection Domain Shift with Consistent Teacher Models

The big idea — first principles

The problem. A single-camera 3D detector trained in one environment (highway, city) fails when deployed in another (traffic camera, drone, rain), because depth-from-2D is brittle. Retraining on new data is expensive, and labels from a new domain are unreliable.

The approach. Train five independent teachers on the source domain, each predicting 3D boxes with 48 different depth estimates per object. Merge those noisy depth guesses using kernel density estimation to get one robust depth per object. Score candidate boxes on a new domain using depth confidence, network class confidence, and 3D overlap; keep only consistent pseudo labels. Train a student on the high-confidence pseudo labels.

What's unique. Merging 48 independent depth signals (from corners, 2D box geometry, projection) produces robust depth that survives domain shift. Rotating predictions are upweighted for diversity—preventing collapse to axis-aligned boxes—by geodesic distance on rotation manifolds. No target-domain labels needed.

ICRA 2025 · 6 citations · detection-tracking

What it does

MonoCT solves the problem of monocular 3D object detection breaking when switched between different cameras, environments, or viewpoints (cars vs. traffic cameras vs. drones). It uses an unsupervised domain adaptation approach where multiple teacher models vote on high-quality pseudo labels to train a student detector, ensuring the network generalizes across domain shifts without needing labeled target data. This is a detection-tracking sub-approach that focuses on robust 3D object detection across heterogeneous camera setups.

The method, in detail

The core insight is that accurate depth estimation bridges domain gaps. MonoCT works in five steps:

Step 1: Ensemble Depth Estimation (GDE Module)

Train five identical teacher models (DLA-34 backbone) with different random seeds on source domain data (NuScenes, Lyft, synthetic PreSIL). Each model predicts 2D bounding boxes and 2D corner keypoints. From each model, extract 48 auxiliary depth estimates: 16 from keypoint positions, 32 from 2D box dimensions and 3D projection geometry. Merge these 48 noisy estimates using kernel density estimation (KDE) with maximum likelihood to produce one robust depth per object.

Step 2: Pseudo Label Scoring (PLS Module)

For target domain images with no labels, each teacher generates candidate 3D boxes. Score them using three signals: (a) class confidence from the network, (b) KDE standard deviation (lower = more teacher agreement on depth), (c) 3D IoU overlap with consensus 2D/3D boxes. Only keep pseudo labels with high composite scores, filtering out inconsistent or weak predictions.

Step 3: Diversity Maximization (DM)

Rotation predictions naturally cluster toward simpler orientations (axis-aligned). To avoid this bias, measure geodesic distance on SO(3) rotation manifolds and upweight pseudo labels with unique rotations that differ from the majority. This prevents the student from learning a collapse to one orientation family.

Step 4: Student Self-Training

Train a student model on the filtered, diverse pseudo labels from all five teachers. Use AdamW optimizer (lr 2.25×10⁻⁴, weight decay 10⁻⁵), cosine learning rate decay, virtual focal length of 700 pixels.

Step 5: Evaluation Across Domains

Test on six benchmarks: KITTI (default), Rope3D (side-view traffic cameras), CDrone (aerial drone views), and KITTI variants with synthetic fog and rain to verify generalization.

UniBEVFusion: Unified Radar-Vision Bevfusion for 3D Object Detection

The big idea — first principles

The problem. Cameras misjudge distance, especially far away. Radar sees through weather but is sparse and lacks texture. Most fusion methods treat radar as fake lidar and drop it into a sparse point cloud; this wastes radar's actual strength—direct millimeter-wave ranging and material properties (RCS).

The approach. Encode camera and radar separately into features. Feed radar's range and RCS (radar cross-section) directly into the camera's depth prediction, enriching what the camera "thinks" it sees. Lift that radar-guided depth into bird's-eye-view, then fuse both modalities adaptively with learned per-channel weights.

What's unique. Early fusion—injecting radar range into depth estimation before lifting to BEV—lets radar guide camera depth everywhere it's weak, rather than competing in a dense grid later. RCS (radar's native material/shape signature) becomes a learned feature, not treated as noise. Tests robustness by adding noise to the camera stream, measuring graceful degradation.

ICRA · 6 citations · perception-fusion

What it does

UniBEVFusion combines radar and camera data to detect and locate 3D objects in autonomous driving scenes. Instead of treating radar like a sparse version of laser scanning, it uses radar's natural depth measurements (millimeter-wave ranging) to improve how camera images are converted into a flat bird's-eye-view (BEV) map of the scene. This is a perception-fusion approach: it fuses multimodal sensor data (camera + 4D radar) into a unified spatial representation for object detection.

The method, in detail

Inputs: Camera images and 4D millimeter-wave radar point clouds (each radar point includes x, y, z position, Radar Cross-Section value, and optionally velocity or angle).

Step 1: Dual encoding

  • Image encoder: Uses a pre-trained SwinTransformer (vision foundation model) to extract hierarchical camera features at multiple scales.
  • Radar encoder: Applies PointPillar-based architecture (PillarFeatureNet + SECOND backbone + SECONDFPN pyramid) to convert sparse radar points into dense feature maps.

Step 2: Radar Depth Lift-Splat-Shoot (RDL) module (the trick)

Prior methods waste radar information by treating it like sparse lidar. UniBEVFusion instead:

  • Concatenates radar coordinate data and RCS values directly into the camera's depth prediction module, performing early fusion.
  • RCS (radar cross-section) encodes object shape, material, and size—radar-native information that cameras cannot sense.
  • This radar-enriched depth estimate is then "lifted" (projected to 3D), "splatted" (scattered into voxel grid), and "shot" (merged into BEV features) by a lifting-splatting-shooting operation, creating a depth-enhanced BEV map.

Step 3: Unified Feature Fusion (UFF) module

Rather than naively concatenating BEV features from both sensors, UniBEVFusion applies four learned operations:

  • Channel Unifier: Uses 1×1 convolutions to align feature dimensions across modalities.
  • Shared Feature Encoder: A common transformer-like encoder that normalizes representations.
  • Softmax Concatenation Fusion: Learns per-channel weights to combine camera and radar BEV features adaptively (not a simple concatenation).
  • Fused Feature Encoder: Residual blocks that refine the combined representation.

Step 4: Detection head

Standard 3D object detector applied to the final fused BEV feature map, outputting bounding boxes, classes, and scores.

Training: End-to-end supervised learning on VoD or TJ4D datasets (4D radar + camera frames with bounding box labels). Loss includes 3D detection loss (L1 on box coordinates + focal loss on classification).

Robustness innovation: The paper introduces a "Failure Test" (FT) ablation—injects Gaussian noise into the camera stream (noise level ρ ∈ [0.5, 0.7, 0.9]) to simulate vision failure, measuring whether the fused system gracefully degrades or collapses. This exposes when a multi-modal fusion is too camera-dependent.

@DETECTORS.register_module()
class SparseDrive(BaseDetector):
    def __init__(self, img_backbone, head, img_neck=None, depth_branch=None, ...):
        self.img_backbone = build_backbone(img_backbone)  # ResNet50
        self.img_neck = build_neck(img_neck)              # FPN
        self.head = build_head(head)                      # SparseDriveHead
        if use_grid_mask:
            self.grid_mask = GridMask(...)                # Augmentation

    def extract_feat(self, img, return_depth=False, metas=None):
        # img: (bs, num_cams, H, W, 3)
        bs, num_cams = img.shape[:2]
        img = img.flatten(end_dim=1)  # (bs*num_cams, H, W, 3)
        
        if self.use_grid_mask:
            img = self.grid_mask(img)
        
        feature_maps = self.img_backbone(img)  # Multi-scale features
        feature_maps = list(self.img_neck(feature_maps))  # FPN pyramid
        
        # Reshape back to multi-view: (bs, num_cams, C, H', W')
        for i, feat in enumerate(feature_maps):
            feature_maps[i] = torch.reshape(feat, (bs, num_cams) + feat.shape[1:])
        
        return feature_maps  # or (feature_maps, depths)

    def forward_train(self, img, **data):
        feature_maps, depths = self.extract_feat(img, True, data)
        # Pass to SparseDriveHead
        model_outs = self.head(feature_maps, data)
        output = self.head.loss(model_outs, data)
        return output

Key insight: Raw images → multi-view backbone → FPN features. No dense grid is built yet. The sparse magic happens in the head.

class InstanceQueue(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, queue_length=4, ...):  # queue_length=4 means history+current
        self.queue_length = queue_length
        # Storage for previous frames:
        self.instance_feature_queue = []  # List of (bs, num_det, 256)
        self.anchor_queue = []            # List of (bs, num_det, 11)
        self.period = None                # (bs, num_det) age of each token
    
    def get(self, det_output, feature_maps, metas, batch_size, mask, anchor_handler):
        # Temporal matching: which token at t-1 is the same object at t?
        if self.period is not None:
            instance_id = det_output['instance_id']
            match = instance_id[..., None] == self.prev_instance_id[:, None]
            # Propagate matched features
            for i in range(len(self.instance_feature_queue)):
                self.instance_feature_queue[i] = (match * self.instance_feature_queue[i][..., None]).sum(dim=2)
        
        # Append current frame
        self.instance_feature_queue.append(instance_feature.detach())
        self.anchor_queue.append(det_anchors.detach())
        self.period += 1
        
        # Keep only last queue_length frames
        if len(self.instance_feature_queue) > self.queue_length:
            self.instance_feature_queue.pop(0)
            self.anchor_queue.pop(0)
        
        # Stack into temporal tensor
        temp_instance_feature = torch.stack(self.instance_feature_queue, dim=2)  # (bs, 50, 4, 256)
        temp_anchor = torch.stack(self.anchor_queue, dim=2)                      # (bs, 50, 4, 11)
        
        return ego_feature, ego_anchor, temp_instance_feature, temp_anchor, temp_mask

Summary: the sparse-centric trick

Dense BEV baseline: Image → 2D CNN → 200m×200m grid (100,000+ cells) → detect all cells → track → plan.

SparseDrive: Image → 900 learnable anchors (K-means) + GNN + deformable attention → detect offsets for each anchor → track via InstanceQueue → motion/planning via MotionPlanningHead with collision-aware rescore.

Why faster? 900 tokens vs. 100,000+ cells, and one unified transformer instead of task-specific pipelines.

Why safer? Explicit collision checking instead of pure end-to-end learning.

Why symmetric? Detection, tracking, mapping, and planning all use the same sparse token representation—no task-specific conversions, no redundant feature extraction.

What the repo is / how you'd run it (entry scripts, config, envs)

MCTrack is a CPU-only tracking pipeline that takes 3D bounding box detections (from any detector) and links them across frames using Kalman filtering and cost-based data association. No GPU required—pure NumPy computation.

Entry point & CLI

File: main.py

Entry-point signature:


parser.add_argument("--dataset", type=str, default="kitti",

Typical usage:

The script loads pre-formatted detection results from JSON, spawns a multi-processing pool to process each scene independently, and saves results per dataset.

Main tracking loop (Base3DTracker)

class Base3DTracker:
    def __init__(self, cfg):
        self.all_trajs = {}        # Active track_id → Trajectory
        self.all_dead_trajs = {}   # Terminated tracks
        self.track_id_counter = 0  # Global ID generator

    def track_single_frame(self, frame_info):
        """
        Main frame-by-frame tracking loop.
        1. Predict: extrapolate all active tracks forward
        2. Assign: match predicted tracks to detections
        3. Update: fuse detections into tracks
        4. Lifecycle: remove dead tracks
        """
        self.predict_before_associate()  # Step 1
        
        trajs = self.get_trajectory_bbox(self.all_trajs)
        match_res, cost_matrix = match_trajs_and_dets(
            trajs, frame_info.bboxes, self.cfg
        )  # Step 2 (Bird's-Eye View stage)
        
        # Process matched pairs
        for i in range(len(trajs)):
            if i in match_res[:, 0]:  # traj i matched to some detection
                matched_det_idx = match_res[match_res[:, 0] == i, 1][0]
                self.all_trajs[trajs[i].track_id].update(
                    frame_info.bboxes[matched_det_idx], cost_matrix[...]
                )  # Step 3
        
        # If IS_RV_MATCHING enabled, re-match unmatched detections/tracks
        # in Range View (range-distance projection)
        if self.cfg["IS_RV_MATCHING"]:
            match_res_rv, _ = match_trajs_and_dets(
                unmatched_trajs, unmatched_dets, self.cfg,
                frame_info.transform_matrix, is_rv=True
            )
            # Secondary matching (greedy, 2D cost)
        
        # Initialize new tracks from unmatched detections
        for i in unmatched_det_indices:
            self.all_trajs[self.track_id_counter] = Trajectory(
                track_id=self.track_id_counter, 
                init_bbox=frame_info.bboxes[i], 
                cfg=self.cfg
            )
            self.track_id_counter += 1
        
        # Prune dead tracks (status_flag == 4)
        for track_id in list(self.all_trajs.keys()):
            if self.all_trajs[track_id].status_flag == 4:
                self.all_dead_trajs[track_id] = self.all_trajs[track_id]
                del self.all_trajs[track_id]
        
        return self.get_output_trajs(frame_info.frame_id)

def match_trajs_and_dets(trajs, dets, cfg, transform_matrix=None, is_rv=False):
    """
    Matches trajectories to detections using cost matrix + Hungarian/Greedy solver.
    
    is_rv=False: Bird's-Eye View (BEV) matching (stage 1, primary)
    is_rv=True:  Range View (RV) matching (stage 2, for failed BEV matches)
    """
    if len(trajs) == 0 or len(dets) == 0:
        return np.empty((0, 2), dtype=int), np.empty((0, 2), dtype=int)
    
    # Compute cost matrix: shape (N_trajectories, N_detections)
    cost_matrix, trajs_category, dets_category = cost_calculate_general(
        trajs, dets, cfg, transform_matrix, is_rv
    )
    
    # Hungarian algorithm solver (from 'lap' package)
    if cfg["MATCHING"][match_type]["MATCHING_MODE"] == "Hungarian":
        m_det, m_tra, um_det, um_tra, costs = Hungarian(
            trans_cost_matrix,
            cfg["THRESHOLD"][match_type]["COST_THRE"]  # Max cost threshold
        )
    else:
        m_det, m_tra, um_det, um_tra, costs = Greedy(...)
    
    # Return: matched pairs as (traj_idx, det_idx)
    matched_indices = np.column_stack((m_tra, m_det))
    return matched_indices, costs

Ro-GDIoU cost function (BEV stage)

File: tracker/cost_function.py

The rotation-aware Generalized Diou combines IoU and geometric distance:


def cal_rotation_gdiou_inbev(box_trk, box_det, cfg, cal_flag=None):
    """
    Computes Ro-GDIoU cost for matching in bird's-eye view.
    Lower cost = better match.
    """
    # Get poses (x, y, z, l, w, h, yaw) from either predicted or fused trajectory state
    if cal_flag == "Predict":
        pose1 = box_trk.bboxes[-1].global_xyz_lwh_yaw_predict
    else:
        pose1 = box_trk.bboxes[-1].global_xyz_lwh_yaw_fusion
    pose2 = box_det.global_xyz_lwh_yaw
    
    # Project to BEV by extracting (x, y, l, w) and converting yaw to degrees
    box1 = np.array([pose1[0], pose1[1], pose1[3], pose1[4], pose1[6]*180/pi])
    box2 = np.array([pose2[0], pose2[1], pose2[3], pose2[4], pose2[6]*180/pi])
    
    # Compute intersection over union using OpenCV rotated rectangle
    iou, inter_area = cal_rotation_iou_inbev(pose1, pose2)
    
    # Compute enclosing rectangle
    union_points = np.concatenate([bev_corners1, bev_corners2])
    rect = cv2.minAreaRect(union_points)
    universe_area = rect[1][0] * rect[1][1]
    
    # Compute volume ratio (3D size penalty)
    volume_ratio = max(vol1, vol2) / min(vol1, vol2)
    
    # Compute orientation similarity (heading angle penalty)
    angle_ratio = orientation_similarity(pose1[6], pose2[6])
    
    # Final score: IoU - penalty for distance - penalty for size mismatch
    w1, w2 = cfg["THRESHOLD"]["BEV"]["WEIGHT_RO_GDIOU"][category]["w1/w2"]
    ro_gdiou = (
        iou 
        - w1 * (universe_area - inter_area) / universe_area  # Distance penalty
        - w2 * box_center_distance / union_distance           # Center penalty
    )
    return ro_gdiou

class EKF_CV(KF_Base):
    def f(self, x):
        """State transition: x_k = F @ x_{k-1}"""
        x_fil = np.array(x)
        x_fil[0] = x[0] + self.dt * x[2]  # x += vx * dt
        x_fil[1] = x[1] + self.dt * x[3]  # y += vy * dt
        return x_fil
    
    def getF(self, x):
        """Jacobian of state transition"""
        F = np.matrix([
            [1.0, 0.0, self.dt, 0.0],      # x = x + vx*dt
            [0.0, 1.0, 0.0, self.dt],      # y = y + vy*dt
            [0.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0],          # vx = vx
            [0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0]           # vy = vy
        ])
        return F

Prediction step (called once per frame before matching):


def predict(self):
    """Extrapolate all three filters forward"""
    predict_state = self.kalman_filter_pose.predict()    # [x_pred, y_pred, vx, vy]
    predict_yaw = self.kalman_filter_yaw.predict()       # [yaw_pred, yaw_rate]
    predict_size = self.kalman_filter_size.predict()     # [l_pred, w_pred, ...]
    
    # Construct predicted BBox
    predict_xyz = predict_state[:2].tolist() + [z_from_fusion]
    predict_yaw_val = predict_yaw[0]
    predict_lwh = predict_size[:2].tolist() + [h_from_fusion]
    
    self.bboxes[-1].global_xyz_lwh_yaw_predict = predict_xyz + predict_lwh + [predict_yaw_val]

Update step (when detection matched):


def update(self, bbox, cost):
    """Fuse detection into filters"""
    measure_pose = np.array([bbox.global_xyz[0], bbox.global_xyz[1], 
                             bbox.global_velocity[0], bbox.global_velocity[1]])
    measure_yaw = np.array([bbox.global_yaw, 
                            np.arctan2(bbox.global_velocity[1], bbox.global_velocity[0])])
    measure_size = np.array([bbox.lwh[0], bbox.lwh[1]])
    
    self.kalman_filter_pose.step(measure_pose)
    self.kalman_filter_yaw.step(measure_yaw)
    self.kalman_filter_size.update(measure_size)
    
    self.bboxes.append(bbox)  # Record detection

Why decoupling works

Mixing position (meters), heading (radians), and size (meters) in one filter causes numerical instability—covariance matrices become ill-conditioned. By separating them:

  • Position filter: learns motion dynamics (acceleration, velocity) at meter scale
  • Yaw filter: learns rotation (blends pose heading with velocity-implied heading)
  • Size filter: learns length/width changes slowly

Each filter can tune its own Kalman gains (P, Q, R) per object class independently.
