01

Learning by watching demonstrations

the problem
Learning robot policies from human demonstrations is direct supervision without reward engineering, but distribution shift happens when the robot encounters scenes the demonstrator never showed it.
the approach
Behavior cloning, diffusion models, transformers that map observations to actions. Use vision foundations, data augmentation, world models, online correction, and multi-modal fusion.
why it helps
Demonstrations provide immediate supervision. Real-time policy learns because it sees new scenes by using rich visual features from pre-training, data augmentation to simulate variation, or world models to adapt on the fly.

The most direct way to teach a robot a skill is to do it yourself, a few dozen times, while the robot records what it saw and what you did — then it copies the mapping. No reward to design, no physics to model: the demonstrations are the answer.

The catch is subtle and fatal. You only ever showed the robot successes. The first time it wobbles slightly off the path you demonstrated, it is looking at a scene no demonstration ever covered, and it has no idea what to do. That small error puts it somewhere even stranger, and the mistake compounds until the whole thing falls apart.

So almost all the 2024 work here circles that one weakness. Gather demonstrations more cheaply and from more sources — human video, teleoperation rigs, other robots. And patch the drift directly: let the robot try, catch where it strays, and add a correction for exactly those states it wandered into.

02

Diffusion policies: many possible moves

the problem
Diffusion models handle multimodal action distributions (multiple valid ways to reach a goal), but are slow for real-time control and require careful conditioning on observation.
the approach
Train diffusion models to denoise actions conditioned on observations. Variations: 2D/3D scene conditioning, language/goal conditioning, action chunking, integrating with MPC or sampling-based planning.
why it helps
Diffusion models naturally represent uncertainty over multiple outcomes; denoising steps gradually refine actions. Instead of picking one deterministic action, generate a distribution. Real-time variants use fewer denoising steps or efficient architectures.

Here is a problem that sounds like nothing and breaks everything: most tasks have more than one right answer. You can grab the mug from the left or the right; both are fine.

Train an ordinary network to output the action and it does something absurd — it averages the two good options and reaches for the empty space between them. The more flexible the task, the worse this gets.

Diffusion policies borrow the trick behind image generators to fix it. Instead of predicting one action, they learn to start from pure noise and gradually clean it up into a whole action sequence. Because the cleanup starts from fresh randomness each time, the policy can produce the left grab or the right grab — never the blurred average of both. It turns “pick one of several good moves” from a failure mode into the normal behaviour.

03

Hands that handle tricky contact

the problem
Multi-finger hands coordinate fingers, contacts shift dynamically, and small pose changes cause task failure. Learning policies for this is much harder than simple grasping because of contact discontinuities.
the approach
Combine simulation, tactile sensing, reward shaping, and diffusion models. Use contact-aware dynamics, in-hand object tracking, tactile feedback, coordinated multi-finger planning.
why it helps
Contacts break and re-establish discontinuously. Papers handle this by: learning tactile models to predict when contact breaks, using world models to forecast contact state, or shaping rewards to encourage stable grasps before manipulation.

Watch where CoRL's papers pile up and it is hands — fingers, grippers, in-hand manipulation. There is a physical reason.

A robot moving through empty air lives in smooth, predictable math: you can model it and plan. The instant a finger touches an object, that smoothness falls off a cliff. Contact starts and stops abruptly, forces spike, a millimetre of error flips a firm grip into a dropped object. Hand-writing rules for that discontinuous mess is close to hopeless.

Learning handles it better precisely because a network can soak up the messy relationship straight from examples, instead of someone having to derive it. That is why touch — tactile sensing, contact-rich control, dexterous in-hand moves — is where learning-for-robots is really being fought out, not walking or flying.
