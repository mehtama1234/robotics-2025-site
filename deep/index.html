The one problem

The one problem

Strip away the details and every robot is trying to do one thing: act in the real world on its own, without a person steering it the whole time. You tell it what you want ("clear the table," "drive me home," "put the needle here," "fly down this pipe and check it"), and it has to work out the rest by itself and get it right.

That is hard for one reason: the real world does not hold still and does not tell you what is in it. A robot cannot see everything. Light changes, weather changes, things move, some things are see-through or shiny, water is murky, and the robot only gets a noisy, partial picture through its cameras and sensors. On top of that, touching things is messy — when a hand grips an object or a needle presses on tissue, the forces are hard to guess ahead of time. And whatever the robot decides to do, it has to do it *now*, using the small, cheap computer bolted onto its body, not a giant server farm somewhere else.

So the one problem is: take in an unclear, changing picture of the world, decide what to do, and move a physical body to do it — reliably, quickly, safely, and cheaply. Both meetings, and nearly all 3,589 papers, are chipping away at some piece of that. The two meetings do not disagree about the problem. They split the work up in almost the same way. Where they differ is in how much weight they put on each piece, and I will point that out as we go.

Every term the papers lean on, defined from first principles. Skim it once, or dip back when a word bites.

SLAMSimultaneous Localization and Mapping — the robot builds a map of a place while at the same time working out where it is inside that map. A chicken-and-egg problem it has to solve on the fly.
Gaussian SplattingA way to store a 3D scene as thousands of tiny colored blobs of light instead of a solid mesh. Fast to draw, looks photo-real, and small enough to run live — so it's become a shared 3D format for mapping and simulation.
VLA — Vision-Language-Action modelTake an AI that can look at a scene and describe it in words, and retrain it so its output is robot movements instead of words. The robot's 'brain' that turns a camera view + an instruction into actions.
VLM — Vision-Language ModelAn AI that takes an image and text together and reasons about both — e.g. 'is the cup empty?' Used as a planner that reads a scene and proposes steps.
Diffusion policyA trick borrowed from image generators: start from random noise and repeatedly clean it into a good result. Pointed at robot motion, it keeps several equally-good options apart instead of averaging them into one jerky compromise.
Sim-to-realTrain the robot inside a fast, free computer copy of the world, then get that learned skill to still work on the real machine — where friction, delays, and wear break things the simulator ignored.
Imitation learningTeach the robot by showing it a human doing the task, and have it copy. Simple and powerful, but it needs many good demonstrations — collecting those is the field's real bottleneck.
Reinforcement learning (RL)The robot learns by trial and error, getting a reward when it does well. No demonstrations needed, but it can take millions of tries — which is why so much of it happens in simulation.
MPC — Model-Predictive ControlLook a short way into the future, imagine several ways the body could move, score each, and take the first step of the best one — then redo it a moment later. Planning by constantly re-planning.
Control Barrier Function (CBF)A math guardrail that sits on top of whatever the robot wants to do and blocks any command that would cross a safety line — like a hard fence the controller physically cannot step over.
Optical flowMeasuring how each pixel moves from one video frame to the next — the raw signal for understanding a moving scene.
Pose estimationWorking out not just where an object is but exactly which way it's facing — its full position and rotation in 3D space.
Sensor fusionCombine sensors whose weaknesses differ — camera plus radar, say — so when one fails (darkness, rain, glass) another covers for it.
Sampling-based controlInstead of solving the hard 'best move' math exactly, try many random short-future motions, score them, and blend the good ones. Fast enough to run live.
ComplianceLetting the robot choose how firm or gentle to be from moment to moment, and read force directly — so it can push a peg into a hole or wipe a surface, jobs pure position control can't do.
Foundation modelOne big AI pre-trained on a huge pile of general data, then adapted to many specific jobs — the same idea that powers chatbots, brought into robotics as a shared starting point.
Whole-body controlCoordinating every joint of a legged or humanoid robot at once — legs, torso, arms — so balance, walking, and manipulation don't fight each other.
TAMP — Task and Motion PlanningTwo planning problems stitched together: the high-level order of steps ('pick up cup, then pour') and the low-level joint motions that physically achieve each step.

01

Cheap, fast, reliable seeing

Sense · 21 papers in this theme

21 papers · sense — the first part of the loop: taking in the world

The problem — in depth

Every robot starts with the same task: figure out what is around it. Before a robot can plan a route, pick up an object, or avoid hitting a person, it has to understand the scene in front of it. That understanding comes from sensors — cameras, depth sensors, radar, microphones — and from software that turns raw sensor readings into useful information: where things are, what they are, how fast they are moving, and what they are about to do next.

This sounds straightforward. It is not.

The first difficulty is speed. A robot moving through the world cannot wait several seconds for its computer to figure out what it sees. A drone avoiding a wall, a car stopping for a pedestrian, or a factory arm reaching for a moving part all need answers in well under a second. But the programs that give accurate answers are usually large and slow. Running them fast enough requires either a powerful computer — which is heavy, expensive, and drains batteries — or a smaller, cheaper computer that cannot keep up with the big programs. Most robots today use the smaller computers. That tension between accuracy and speed is at the center of this topic.

The second difficulty is the gap between clean lab conditions and the real world. In a lab, lighting is good, objects sit still, and sensors work as expected. In the real world, lights are dim or shifting, objects move fast and blur, glass surfaces reflect and confuse depth sensors, rain scatters radar and camera signals, and important things — a small flower on a plant, a shuttlecock in flight, a cable on a cluttered bench — are tiny or partially hidden. Programs trained to work in clean conditions often fail in these conditions.

The third difficulty is that no single sensor covers everything. A camera gives rich color detail but cannot measure distance well and fails in the dark. A depth sensor (called a lidar or RGB-D camera) measures distance but fails on transparent or shiny surfaces. Radar works in rain and through walls but gives only a rough scatter of points — too sparse to understand a scene. Each sensor has gaps, and a robot that relies on just one will fail whenever that sensor hits its limits.

Together these three difficulties create a hard constraint: the robot needs to see the world accurately, fast, and under a wide range of conditions, all on hardware that fits in a small machine with limited power. This is the sensing problem, and it sits at the entrance to everything else a robot does. If the robot sees wrong, every downstream decision — where to go, what to pick up, how to avoid danger — is built on a faulty foundation.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Make programs leaner so they can run on small hardware

The most direct answer to the speed-versus-accuracy problem is to redesign the program itself so it does less unnecessary math. This is called model compression or lightweight design, but the underlying idea is simple: cut out everything in the program that does not contribute much to the final answer.

NeuFlow-V2 does this for measuring motion between video frames (called optical flow). Measuring how pixels move from one frame to the next is a key input for understanding a moving scene, but existing methods are either accurate or fast, not both. NeuFlow-V2 redesigns the math model so it reaches more than 20 frames per second on cheap edge hardware without giving up much accuracy. The key move is identifying which parts of the computation actually drive the answer and keeping only those.

Context-aware Sparse Spatiotemporal Learning (CSSL) takes a similar approach for event-based cameras — sensors that fire only when something in the scene changes, rather than capturing full frames. These sensors can run on very low power on brain-inspired chips, but pairing them with useful software has been hard. CSSL solves this by setting activity thresholds per individual sensor cell based on what that cell actually sees, rather than one global rule. The result is a program that stays sparse (does little work when the scene is quiet) but stays accurate when things move.

Combine sensors so gaps in one are covered by another

A second major approach is not to fight any single sensor's limits but to pair sensors whose weaknesses are different. When one sensor fails, another covers for it. This is called sensor fusion.

The paper on mmDEAR illustrates this well. Radar works through walls and in darkness, but it gives only a sparse scatter of points — far too few to reconstruct the shape of a human body. The researchers solve this by training a network on camera images and radar together, so the network learns to fill in the missing radar points using knowledge of what human bodies look like. At test time, no camera is needed — the network has learned to make the sparse radar data act like the denser camera data.

Estimating User Engagement using a Dynamic Bayesian Network (DBN) takes the same logic into human-robot interaction. Deciding whether a person is engaged with a robot requires reading many signals at once: where their eyes are pointing, how their head is tilted, what expression is on their face, and even their skin temperature. No single one of these is reliable alone. The DBN combines a color camera and a thermal camera into a single model that tracks all of these signals and how they change over time, giving a much more stable read of engagement than any one sensor could.

Learn to handle hard visual conditions directly

A third approach targets specific failure cases — the situations where cameras and depth sensors break down — and trains programs to handle them explicitly rather than hoping they generalize.

Depth Estimation Through Translucent Surfaces addresses one of the most common and least-discussed failure modes: glass. Depth sensors work by bouncing signals off surfaces. Glass lets the signal partly through and partly reflects it, producing a confused mixed reading. The paper adapts a learning-based depth model to recognize and handle this mixed signal, recovering useful depth estimates even when the scene includes semi-transparent material.

The Complete Corruption-Aware Retinex Framework tackles low-light image improvement (called enhancement). The standard approach to dark images is to brighten them — but brightening a dark image amplifies noise and can introduce blurring. This paper treats the brightening and the cleanup as a single problem, decomposing the image into a light layer and a shadow layer, improving both, and then removing the distortions that the brightening process itself introduced.

YO-CSA-T addresses fast-moving small objects — specifically a badminton shuttlecock, which moves so fast it blurs into the background. The system uses two cameras to get a 3D position, pays attention to context around where the shuttlecock should be, predicts where it will go next, and fills in frames where it disappears entirely.

Estimate position and orientation without depth sensors

Many tasks require knowing not just that an object is present but exactly where it is and which way it is facing — its full position and rotation in space. This is called pose estimation. The standard approach uses a depth sensor to get 3D information directly. A growing set of papers shows how to do this from a plain color camera alone, which is cheaper and lighter.

RGBTrack tracks the exact position and rotation of an object in real time with no depth sensor. It renders many views of a known 3D model and uses a binary search to find which view best matches what the camera sees — a method called render-and-compare. It pairs this with a tracker that follows the object between frames and a statistical filter (Kalman filter) to smooth out noisy estimates.

RCGNet and CIPE take a learning approach: they train networks that absorb knowledge of what 3D shapes look like and then apply that knowledge to infer position and rotation from color images alone.

Use non-visual sensing for special environments

Some environments where robots operate simply defeat cameras. Underwater, murky water scatters light. Behind walls, cameras cannot see at all. In these cases, researchers turn to other signals entirely.

The two underwater gesture-recognition papers (One-Shot Gesture Recognition and Action Recognition for Underwater Gesture Communication) both deal with water's effect on visual signals — blur, shadow, shifting light. One uses mathematical shape descriptors that describe a gesture by its overall form rather than pixel-by-pixel detail, making it robust to visual noise. The other tracks how a gesture changes across many frames rather than relying on any single frame, which lets it ignore individual bad frames.

The 3-D Sound Source Localization paper uses five microphones and timing differences between them to compute where sounds are coming from in three dimensions, bypassing cameras entirely.

Where it stands

Robots today can see well in controlled, predictable settings, and the programs to do it have gotten leaner and faster in recent years. Combining sensors has become a practical tool for filling in each sensor's gaps. The hard cases — glass, darkness, rain, tiny fast objects, through-wall sensing — are solvable one at a time, but no system handles all of them robustly at once. Doing all of this on the small computers that fit inside actual robots, without sacrificing accuracy or speed, remains an open problem with steady but incomplete progress.

One shared way to store the 3D world

Sense · 447 papers in this theme

447 papers · sense — how the robot takes in and holds a picture of the world around it

The problem — in depth

Every robot needs to know what the world around it looks like before it can do anything useful. That sounds simple, but it is not.

A robot's sensors — cameras, laser scanners, motion chips — pour out raw streams of numbers: brightness values, distance readings, tiny acceleration signals. None of that is a "map." The robot has to turn those raw numbers into some stored structure that tells it where things are, what they look like, and how they relate to each other. That stored structure is what researchers mean when they talk about a scene representation — a way the robot holds the world in memory.

For decades, different jobs used completely different structures. If you wanted to track where the robot is while it builds a map (a job called simultaneous localization and mapping, or SLAM), you used one format. If you wanted to render a photo-realistic view of the scene for training, you used a different one. If you wanted a planner to reason about what objects are near each other, you used yet another. Each format was a silo: data captured for one job could not easily feed another. You had to scan the scene, store it three different ways, and keep three systems running.

This is wasteful. Memory is tight on a robot. Processing time is tight. And the seams between the different systems are where bugs and errors creep in.

The deeper problem is that robots operate in the real world, which is messy. Lighting changes. People walk through the scene. Outdoor spaces have no walls to bounce a laser off cleanly. Underwater, light scatters in ways that make every color look wrong. Featureless hallways give a camera almost nothing to anchor on. Any representation that only works in a clean, static, well-lit lab is not good enough.

All of this connects to the central challenge every robot faces: take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do it fast, cheap, safe, and general. A scene representation sits at the very start of that chain. If the stored picture of the world is wrong, noisy, too slow to build, or too large to fit in memory, every step after it — the decisions, the movements — goes wrong too. Getting the representation right is not a detail; it is the foundation.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Building the scene from tiny colored blobs

The single biggest idea running through this group of papers is a method called 3D Gaussian Splatting. The name is technical, but the idea is this: instead of storing the scene as a mesh of flat triangles or a grid of boxes, you describe it as a large collection of small, soft, colored blobs — each blob has a position, a size, a shape, and a color. When you want to see what the scene looks like from a new camera angle, you project all the blobs onto the image plane, and the result looks strikingly realistic. The key advantage is speed: rendering with blobs is fast enough to do live, and the format is flexible enough to support training, mapping, and planning all at once.

Papers in this group use Gaussian Splatting as the common language that connects different jobs. SplatSim shows that if you scan a real workspace and build a blob-based model of it, you can drop that model into a physics simulator and train robot manipulation skills in it. The simulated images look close enough to real ones that the trained skill runs directly on the real robot without retraining — a problem the field calls the simulation-to-real gap. Robo-GS goes further: it ties each blob to a nearby point on a geometric mesh, so that when the robot arm moves, both the visual appearance and the physical geometry update together, giving you a replica that is both realistic and physically accurate. SemGauss-SLAM bakes object labels — "this blob is part of a chair" — directly into the blob data, so the map understands what things are, not just what they look like.

Making maps while moving — and staying on track

Several papers use the blob format specifically for SLAM: building a map of an unknown space while simultaneously figuring out where the robot is in that map. This is hard because the two jobs depend on each other: you need a map to know your position, and you need your position to build the map correctly.

CGS-SLAM attacks one of the biggest practical problems with blob-based SLAM: these maps grow very large very fast because the system keeps placing new blobs everywhere. The paper trims the map by discarding blobs that have not been seen recently, then compresses the remaining ones by sharing shape data across groups — cutting memory use sharply without losing visual quality. GARAD-SLAM and Dy3DGS-SLAM both tackle moving objects: people or vehicles that cross the scene corrupt the map because the system tries to record them as permanent structure. Both papers detect movement — one by checking which pixels flow against the expected camera motion, the other by watching for depth changes — and mask those pixels out before updating the map. RGB-Only Gaussian Splatting SLAM removes the need for a depth sensor entirely, building the blob map from plain color images alone by using a network that predicts consistent 3D positions across frames.

Shrinking and speeding up for real hardware

A representation that needs a server-grade graphics chip or gigabytes of memory is useless on a robot that runs on a battery. A strong thread in this group is about making representations small and fast enough to run live on modest hardware.

NGD-SLAM achieves 60 frames per second on a regular laptop — no graphics chip — by running two tasks at the same time instead of in sequence: one thread tracks camera position while another masks out moving objects, so neither waits for the other. CGS-SLAM's compression work fits here too. Kinematic-ICP takes a different angle: instead of a blob-based scene, it uses a classic laser-scan alignment method (ICP — a technique that takes two clouds of 3D points and finds the shift and rotation that makes them match best), but adds the robot's known motion rules as hard limits on what position estimates are allowed. This cuts drift without any extra memory. MambaGlue speeds up the step of matching visual features across camera frames by replacing the standard attention mechanism — which slows down as the number of features grows — with a newer sequence model (Mamba) that scales better.

Handling hard environments and long time scales

Some papers push the representation to work in conditions that earlier work ignored. SeaSplat takes the blob format underwater: it learns, at the same time as building the 3D scene, how water absorbs and scatters light at different depths, so it can undo those effects and recover the true color of objects. GaussianRoom handles plain, textureless indoor walls — surfaces that give the blob optimizer almost nothing to anchor on — by training a second model alongside it that estimates the true surface geometry and tells the blobs where they should and should not grow.

Other papers deal with time: a map built once will go stale. Ephemerality Meets Lidar-Based Lifelong Mapping assigns each part of a laser scan a score for how temporary it is — a score that captures both short-term changes within one mapping session and longer-term changes across many sessions. LiLoc keeps a single master map that absorbs new scan data from each new visit to a space, using a coarse-then-fine position search to start each session and a local graph that fuses motion, laser, and inertial data to keep estimates tight over months.

Giving maps meaning so robots can reason about them

A map that only stores where surfaces are cannot answer the question "find the cup near the red bowl." Beyond Bare Queries (BBQ) builds a map where each object is a node that carries a text description of what it is and where it sits. A large language model then reasons through a step-by-step chain over those nodes to answer spatial questions in plain language. ReMEmbR extends this to long time horizons: a robot that has been running for hours stores past observations — with times, positions, and image descriptions — in a searchable database. When asked a question about something it saw earlier, it retrieves the relevant entries and reasons over them.

Where it stands

Gaussian Splatting has moved quickly from a graphics novelty to a practical common format for robot sensing, and the compression and real-time work has made it usable on modest hardware. Systems that handle moving objects, plain surfaces, and tricky lighting environments now exist. What is still open: running a full blob-based map on very small, cheap hardware without a GPU remains difficult; merging maps across many robots in real time is unsolved at scale; and making the semantic layer — the part that knows what objects are — work reliably across diverse, uncontrolled environments is an active area with no settled answer yet.

Self-driving cars, done leaner and safer

Sense · 428 papers in this theme

428 papers · serves the sense part of the loop — taking in the world around the car

The problem — in depth

A car driving itself has one job that never stops: figure out what is around it, decide what to do, and move the car safely. The sensing part — figuring out what is around it — is where most of this research lives, and it is genuinely hard.

The core difficulty is that the world around a moving car is big, fast-changing, and full of things that matter at different levels. A parked truck a hundred meters away matters. A child stepping off a curb two meters away matters more and right now. A system that tries to keep perfect track of everything — every centimeter of road, every building face, every other vehicle — burns through computing power so fast that it cannot run at the speed the car needs. And if it is too slow, it is useless, or worse, dangerous.

There is also the question of what the sensors can and cannot see. Laser scanners (called lidar) give precise 3D measurements but leave gaps — they cannot see around corners, they miss the sides of objects, and their scans are thin and sparse. Cameras see color and texture but do not directly measure distance; figuring out how far away something is from a flat image is a hard math problem. Radar bounces radio waves off objects and works in fog, rain, and darkness where cameras and lidar fail, but its output is noisier and less sharp.

All of these problems connect back to the same big challenge every robot faces: the world is unclear and always changing, computing is limited, and the cost of getting it wrong is a real crash with real people. The research in this topic is about making sensing cheaper to run, more accurate in bad conditions, more honest about what it does not know, and better connected to the decisions the car needs to make.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Drop the dense map; track only what matters

The oldest approach to scene understanding was to build a thick grid of the space around the car and fill in every cell with information. That works but is expensive. A newer idea is to represent the scene as a small set of important tokens — each token stands for one object or one piece of road, not one grid cell — and update only those as the car moves. This is called sparse scene representation.

SparseDrive (ICRA) does exactly this: it tracks a small number of scene tokens for detecting objects, following them across time, and building a map of the road, all in one pass. Because it is not filling in a huge grid, it runs fast enough for real roads. Chameleon (ICRA) takes a similar idea into one specific sub-problem — figuring out how lanes connect at intersections — and runs fast symbolic rules for common cases, only calling a heavier model when the situation is unusual.

The gain is real: when you only work with what matters, the computation shrinks enough to run live on the car, not just in a lab.

Mix radar with cameras to work in bad weather

Cameras on their own cannot tell you how far away something is with much precision, especially at long range. Lidar is precise but expensive and suffers in heavy rain. Radar is cheap, works through weather, and directly measures velocity — but its output looks like a sparse cloud of dots, not a sharp image.

The field has moved toward fusing (combining) radar and camera data, and the 2025 papers show several smart ways to do it. HyDRa (ICRA) merges them at two stages: first it uses radar depth readings to help the camera estimate depth from its own image, then it merges both sensor streams again in a flat top-down map of the car's surroundings (called a bird's-eye-view map). UniBEVFusion (ICRA) follows a similar two-stage idea, and it explicitly tests what happens when the camera fails — important for safety. Talk2Radar (ICRA) goes further and shows that radar can support object finding from language descriptions, not just detection. The HeRCULES dataset (ICRA) gives researchers a way to test radar-based systems across different weather, times of day, and two different radar types together.

The practical result: a car that uses radar alongside cameras and lidar can keep sensing accurately when conditions are bad, which is exactly when a self-driving car most needs to know what is around it.

Keep track of uncertainty — know what you do not know

A self-driving car that is confused about where something is should drive more carefully near that spot. But many systems just output a single answer — this object is at this position — with no signal about how sure that answer is. When those answers are wrong, the car has no way to know it should slow down or take a different path.

Several papers address this directly. MonoCT (ICRA) is about 3D object detection from a single camera; when the detector is moved to a new place or new camera it does not know, accuracy drops. MonoCT uses a teacher model to generate labels for the new setting, but — crucially — scores how consistent those labels are, only keeping the ones it trusts. The learned MPC paper (ICRA) does something similar at the control level: it splits the car's motion model into the parts that are well understood and the parts that are not, then uses a small network to correct only the uncertain parts. Ground-Optimized Radar-Inertial Odometry (ICRA) uses a math method called Gaussian Process regression to smoothly blend two noisy sensor streams rather than combining them at fixed time steps, which lets the system propagate its uncertainty continuously.

Knowing what you do not know is not a nice-to-have; it is what lets a self-driving car be careful in exactly the right places.

Generate data to fill gaps in training

Machine learning systems need large amounts of labeled data, and real driving data is expensive to collect — and some situations, like accidents or heavy snow, are rare. A fast-growing approach is to generate fake but realistic data to fill these gaps.

LidarDM (ICRA) generates entire driving scenes from a text description, then simulates what a real lidar scanner would see from inside that scene, producing realistic sensor data without driving anywhere. AVD2 (ICRA) generates realistic accident videos from written descriptions using a generative model, so developers can train on crashes without needing real crash footage. Synthetica (IROS) generates millions of images of robots picking objects using a high-quality computer renderer, so robot perception systems can be trained without months of real-world photography. DiffSSC (IROS) generates complete 3D scenes with labels starting from a sparse lidar scan, filling in the hidden geometry and object types that the scanner missed.

This approach shifts some of the cost from collecting data in the world to generating it on a computer — which is far cheaper and lets researchers create exactly the hard cases they need.

Connect vehicles so each one sees more

A single car can only see what its own sensors reach. Two cars sharing what they see can together see around corners and past obstructions. This sharing is called cooperative or collaborative perception, and it appears in several papers.

CoCMT (IROS) is about doing this efficiently: instead of sending full sensor streams over the network, it identifies the small set of features that are most useful to share and sends only those. Co-MTP (ICRA) uses a similar vehicle-to-everything (V2X) network to share not just what each car currently sees, but each car's prediction of where nearby vehicles are going — so every car in the network gets better trajectory forecasts. V2X-DGW (ICRA) addresses what happens when the weather is bad: it trains the shared perception system on artificially degraded data so it still works when real rain or fog hits.

The challenge with cooperative perception is bandwidth and latency — sending data between cars has to be fast and lightweight or it creates new problems. The papers here show that sending compressed, carefully selected features rather than raw data makes this practical.

Where it stands

The clearest progress is in making perception faster and more efficient: sparse representations and smarter sensor fusion have cut computing costs enough that self-driving systems can run in real time on real hardware. Mixing radar with cameras has meaningfully improved performance in bad weather. What remains open is robustness when things go truly wrong — an untrained situation, a sensor failure, an edge case no dataset covered. The systems are also still largely tested domain by domain; a detector trained in one city or weather type still struggles when moved to another, and closing that gap fully is unsolved.

Language models that plan the job

Decide · 54 papers in this theme

54 papers · the decide part of the loop — turning a spoken goal into a safe, ordered set of robot steps

The problem — in depth

Every time you hand a robot a task in plain words — "clean up the kitchen" or "find my keys" — you are asking it to do something remarkably hard. The robot must figure out what you mean, break that meaning into concrete physical steps, check that each step is actually possible in the room it is in right now, and do all of this without hurting anyone or breaking anything.

Older planning systems handled this by having a person write out every rule by hand in advance: if the cup is on the table and the hand is empty, then pick up the cup. This works for narrow, fixed jobs, but it falls apart the moment something unexpected happens or the goal changes. A human cannot write rules for every possible kitchen layout, every possible object, every possible instruction.

The breakthrough idea here is to use the same kind of AI that powers conversational chatbots — systems trained on enormous amounts of text so that they understand language the way a person does — to do the step-making instead. These systems, called large language models (LLMs), can take "clean up the kitchen" and produce a reasonable list of steps without anyone pre-writing the rules. That is genuine progress.

But it brings new problems. These AI systems were trained to produce text that sounds right, not steps that are physically possible. They invent steps the robot cannot carry out. They do not know where the robot is, what the room looks like, or what rules the site has about safety. They forget what happened earlier in a long task. They plan each task as if it is the only task the robot will ever do, which leads to choices that are cheap now but costly later.

These problems matter because they sit at the exact center of what makes robots hard. A robot must take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do all of that fast, cheap, safe, and across many situations — not just the ones it was trained on. The decide step is where the plan either holds together or falls apart. If the plan is wrong, everything downstream — every move the robot makes — is wrong too.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Giving the language model a memory of the world

The most direct problem is that a language model knows language but not the specific room the robot is in right now. Several papers fix this by building and maintaining a structured record of what the robot has seen, then feeding relevant pieces of that record to the language model when it needs to plan.

KARMA keeps two separate logs: a persistent 3D map of the whole space that does not reset between tasks, and a short-term log of recent changes — an object moved, a drawer opened — that updates constantly. The language model reads from both when choosing the next step. E2Map goes further: it tags every location in the robot's map with a score recording how well the robot's own past attempts there went. The language model plans around places where things went poorly before. L3M+P stores everything the robot knows in a structured web of facts (a knowledge graph) that updates whenever the robot sees or hears something new; when a task arrives, the language model reads the relevant parts and a separate classical planner turns that into an executable sequence.

The common insight: the language model supplies flexible understanding of the goal, while the structured memory supplies the grounding the model lacks on its own.

Checking that each step is actually possible before acting

Even with good world knowledge, a language model can write steps that look plausible but cannot be carried out. These papers add a checking layer that tests each planned step against real physical conditions before the robot moves.

ConceptAgent pairs the language model with a tree search process — it tries many possible action sequences in a simulated forward search (Monte Carlo Tree Search), and at each node it checks whether the required conditions for that step are actually present. If a step turns out impossible, the search backtracks and tries a different path. The paper on Enhancing Robustness in Language-Driven Robotics wraps a smaller on-board language model in a checking system that tests each step for sense before the robot commits to it, then detects and corrects mistakes on the fly when they occur anyway.

This approach treats the language model as a first draft generator, not a trusted authority. The checking layer catches what the model gets wrong.

Writing plans in a form that can be verified and executed reliably

A related but distinct idea is to make the language model produce its plan in a structured, checkable form from the start — not free text, but code or formal logic that a separate system can test for correctness.

SayComply organizes a site's operations manuals and safety rules into a tree-shaped database and retrieves relevant passages before the language model writes each step, so the plan is grounded in actual written rules, not just the model's general knowledge. LTLCodeGen sidesteps the language model's tendency to write incorrect formal logic by using code generation rules that guarantee the output is syntactically correct; that correct code then feeds directly into a motion planner. FrameCRL (Controlled Robot Language with Frame Semantics) avoids large language models entirely at plan time: it parses each spoken instruction using a structured set of linguistic meaning units (FrameNet frames) to extract precise goals, then passes those to a formal task planner. Path Planning Using Instruction-Guided Probabilistic Roadmaps uses a language model to convert a person's stated travel preferences into a cost map — a number assigned to every possible location indicating how much the robot should prefer or avoid it — and the robot's path planner reads that map directly.

The through-line: get the output of the language step into a form that does not depend on the model being right, because it can be checked or executed mechanically.

Adapting the plan to the specific robot and environment

A general plan written by a language model may be fine in the abstract but wrong for this robot in this room with these constraints. Several papers address this mismatch by grounding general plans against specific knowledge of the actual setup.

AdaptBot generates a general plan with a language model, then checks every step against a structured knowledge graph of known facts about the specific environment. When a step still does not fit, the robot asks a targeted question to a nearby human and stores the answer for next time. Multi-Floor Zero-Shot Object Navigation Policy extends an existing single-floor search system to reason about when to switch floors in a multi-story building, using visual and language-based understanding to guess which floor likely has the target — without task-specific training. Language-Guided Object Search in Agricultural Environments asks a language model which objects tend to appear near which other objects, then uses those relationships to plan a search route that covers the most probable spots first in an unfamiliar farm setting.

Planning ahead across many tasks, not just the current one

Most planners optimize for the task in front of them. But a robot used all day needs to make choices that keep future tasks easy too.

Anticipatory Planning for Performant Long-Lived Robot in Large-Scale Home-Like Environments uses a graph neural network — a type of network that works on connected webs of objects and relationships — to estimate how hard future tasks are likely to be given the current state of the room. The robot then chooses actions that balance finishing the current task well and keeping the room in a state where future tasks stay easy. It combines this estimate with a search process that samples many possible actions before committing.

Where it stands

Language models have made robots genuinely more flexible at the decide step: they can now accept open-ended goals in plain words and produce reasonable starting plans without hand-coded rules. The main open problems are reliability and trust — these models still invent impossible steps, still fail to account for safety rules they were not shown, and still struggle when a task runs long enough that memory and context matter. The checking and grounding techniques in these papers reduce failure rates but add complexity, and most have been tested only in specific domains or simulated settings. Making this whole pipeline fast, safe, and general enough to run on any robot in any environment is the work still ahead.

Turning seeing-and-talking AI into doing AI

Decide · 85 papers in this theme

85 papers · decide — how the robot chooses what to do next

The problem — in depth

In the last few years, AI models learned to look at a photo, read a caption, and answer questions about the scene in plain sentences. They can say "there is a red cup on the table to the left of the laptop." That is genuinely impressive. But saying something and doing something are not the same thing.

A robot arm that needs to pick up that red cup has to know, in real numbers, how far away the cup is, what angle to approach it from, how wide to open its fingers, and how much force to use. None of that comes out of a model that was trained to produce words. The model's whole training reward was "did the next word match the target?" There was no signal for "did the arm land in the right spot?" So the words come out fine but the movements are absent.

There is a second, related gap. These big models were trained on flat photos from the internet. A flat photo has no reliable depth information. The model cannot tell whether two objects are five centimeters apart or five meters apart, which means it cannot reason about whether the robot can reach something, or whether one object is in the way of another. Space, in the physical sense, is nearly invisible to these models.

Together, these two gaps mean that even a very powerful AI that can describe a kitchen scene in perfect prose is still, in the practical sense, blind to three-dimensional space and mute when it comes to motor commands.

This matters because every robot faces the same core challenge: take in an unclear, changing world; decide what to do; move a real body; and do it fast, cheap, safe, and in general situations no one wrote rules for. The deciding step is where these big models were supposed to help — they carry enormous common-sense knowledge about the world, knowledge that is very expensive to build from scratch. But that help only arrives if the model can cross the gap from words to actions and from flat images to real space.

The 85 papers in this topic are all working on that crossing. They differ in which part of the gap they attack and how.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Rewiring the model's outputs to produce movements, not words

The most direct fix is to take a model that already understands scenes and words and add a new layer that produces robot movements instead of — or alongside — text. These are called vision-language-action models (VLAs). The model still reads the camera and the instruction, but instead of typing an answer, it outputs the joint angles or the end-effector position for the next fraction of a second.

"Improving Vision-Language-Action Model with Online Reinforcement Learning" (iRe-VLA) tackles the problem that copying expert demonstrations only gets you so far. The robot needs to try things and learn from outcomes, the way a person does. But running trial-and-error training on a huge model is unstable. The paper's answer is to alternate: one phase where the robot collects experience by trying, followed by a phase of supervised learning on that experience to keep the model stable — then repeat. This cycling makes self-improvement practical.

"PD-VLA: Accelerating Vision-Language-Action Model Integrated with Action Chunking via Parallel Decoding" deals with a different problem: speed. These models predict a whole short block of future actions at once (called action chunking). Doing that one step at a time is too slow for real robot arms. By treating the block as a parallel math problem rather than a sequential one, the system speeds up without any retraining.

2. Teaching the model to understand 3D space

If the model only sees flat images, it guesses about depth and often guesses wrong. Several papers add depth information directly to the model's training or its input, so spatial reasoning becomes a first-class skill rather than an afterthought.

"SpatialBot: Precise Spatial Understanding with Vision Language Models" fine-tunes a vision-language model on depth camera images alongside normal color images. The team also built two new question-and-answer datasets — SpatialQA and SpatialQA-E — where every question is about depth or 3D layout. Training on this dataset forces the model to actually answer questions like "how far is the chair from the wall?" rather than treating depth as decoration.

"Towards Generalizable Vision-Language Robotic Manipulation: A Benchmark and LLM-Guided 3D Policy" (3D-LOTUS++) builds a policy that reads the 3D positions of objects in the scene rather than working from a flat image. A language model then plans the multi-step task, and a vision-language model names objects it has never seen before. The paper also introduces a benchmark, GemBench, with four levels of novelty to check whether this actually generalizes.

"Language-Guided Object-Centric Diffusion Policy for Generalizable and Collision-Aware Manipulation" pulls out the relevant object's full 3D shape from a point cloud (a cloud of measured 3D points), trains a movement-generation model on that shape, and then adds collision-avoidance as a penalty during movement generation so the robot steers around obstacles without needing to be retrained on each new scene.

3. Splitting planning and acting into two separate jobs

A single model that must both figure out what to do and produce the exact movements is asked to do two very different jobs at once. Many papers split those jobs: a large language or vision-language model handles the "what to do" part, while a smaller, faster system handles the "how to move" part.

"Guiding Long-Horizon Task and Motion Planning with Vision Language Models" is a clean example. The language model breaks a long task into sub-steps in plain words. A traditional motion planner then turns each sub-step into actual movements. When the motion planner finds a sub-step physically impossible, it sends that back to the language model, which tries a different way. Neither system has to do the other's job.

"BUMBLE: Unifying Reasoning and Acting with Vision-Language Models for Building-wide Mobile Manipulation" extends this to a very long task — finding and handling objects across an entire multi-floor building. The vision-language model reads the situation and picks from a library of ready-made skills. Two layers of memory let it recall what it saw earlier in a task that can last fifteen minutes.

"Local Policies Enable Zero-Shot Long-Horizon Manipulation" makes each individual skill work in coordinates measured relative to the object being acted on, not the room. This means a skill trained in a test environment works in the real world even when the robot's exact location is different. A large model then chains these local skills in sequence.

4. Letting the robot recover when things go wrong

Most training data shows tasks going right. Real robots encounter things going wrong: an object not where expected, a grip that slips, a door that doesn't open. Several papers specifically train for recovery.

"Closed-Loop Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation with GPT-4V" (COME-robot) uses a large model that reads both images and text to check, after each action, whether the action actually worked. If not, it traces back through its own recent steps to find where things went wrong, then replans from that point. This makes failure a normal, handled event rather than a crash.

"RACER: Rich Language-Guided Failure Recovery Policies for Imitation Learning" takes a different route: it adds recovery moves to the training data itself. An automated pipeline takes existing expert demonstrations, adds the kinds of mistakes a robot would make and the moves needed to fix them, then attaches plain-language descriptions of what went wrong and what to do. At run time a vision-language model watches the robot and gives it correction instructions in plain speech.

"LERa: Replanning with Visual Feedback in Instruction Following" keeps it simple: if something goes wrong, the system looks at the camera feed, forms a plain-language explanation of what happened, and rebuilds the plan from there.

5. Learning from humans without requiring robot-specific data

Robot demonstrations are expensive to collect. Human video is abundant. Several papers bridge the gap.

"Motion Tracks: A Unified Representation for Human-Robot Transfer in Few-Shot Imitation Learning" represents both human hand movements and robot end-effector movements as short sequences of 2D pixel positions over time. Because both are in the same format, a policy trained on human video and just a few robot demonstrations can then convert those pixel tracks to real 3D robot movements at run time.

"R+X: Retrieval and Execution from Everyday Human Videos" goes further: given a plain-language instruction, a vision-language model searches a large collection of unlabeled first-person human videos and pulls out the most relevant short clips. A separate method makes the robot copy the key moves from those clips with no additional training on robot data.

"ICRT: In-Context Imitation Learning via Next-Token Prediction" uses a large transformer that predicts the next sensor reading or action from a sequence of past ones. At test time, a few example demonstrations of a new task are dropped in as a prompt — the model then does the new task with no retraining at all, the same way a language model answers a new question when you give it a few examples.

Where it stands

Systems that split planning from acting are reliable enough to run on real robots in real rooms today, and the ability to describe a failure in plain words and replan around it is now routine in research demos. What remains hard is the full loop: a single model that perceives 3D space accurately, reasons about a novel task, produces correct movements at the speed the arm needs, and does all of this on the onboard computer a real product can afford. Each paper solves one piece; no single system yet closes all the gaps at once.

Getting several robots to work together

Decide · 145 papers in this theme

145 papers · decide — how a team of robots picks who does what and stays coordinated

The problem — in depth

A single robot already has a hard job. It must read its sensors, figure out where it is, decide what to do next, and then move its body — all in real time, in a world that keeps changing. Now multiply that by ten, or a hundred, or ten thousand robots that must all share a single goal.

The new difficulties are not just more of the same. They are different in kind.

First, the team may be made of different robot types. A drone can fly over a wall; a wheeled robot cannot. A robot arm can pick up a box; a drone cannot. When a shared task needs several of these steps in order — fly here, pick this up, carry it there — someone or something must figure out who does which part. Do it wrong and the whole job stalls.

Second, each robot can only see a small patch of the world at once. What one robot learns — an obstacle just appeared, a path is blocked — the others do not automatically know. The team must share information, but passing everything to everyone all the time is too slow and uses too much radio bandwidth.

Third, when a task has many steps across many robots, the number of possible orderings and assignments grows extremely fast. A plan that looks fine at the top level can turn out to be physically impossible once you check the details: the robot assigned to step four is already busy with step three and cannot be in two places at once.

Fourth, real robots fail. A battery dies. A sensor glitches. A drone falls. The rest of the team must notice this and keep going — without a person stepping in every time.

All of this connects directly to the central problem every robot faces: the world is messy and incomplete, time is short, and bodies are fragile. One robot managing its own slice of reality is already hard. A team that must coordinate slices is harder by an order of magnitude, because a mistake by one robot can cascade and ruin the work of every other.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Using a language model to divide and assign work

Several papers take a large language model (LLM) — the same kind of system behind chat tools — and use it as a coordinator that reads a plain-language mission description and breaks it into smaller jobs, one for each robot. The key insight is that LLMs already know a great deal about tasks and sequences from the text they were trained on, so they can produce sensible splits without being programmed with every possible rule.

The difficulty is that language models do not automatically know what is physically possible. Left unchecked, they produce plans that sound fine but cannot be carried out — a robot assigned to lift something it cannot reach, or two robots sent to the same place at the same time. The field's answer is to pair the LLM with a second system that checks feasibility. In COHERENT, the LLM proposes a plan, each robot tries its part, and reports back what worked and what did not, so the LLM can revise. In LaMMA-P, the LLM hands its assignments to a logic planner (PDDL) that finds step-by-step actions only if they are actually valid. DELTA does something similar: the LLM sets sub-goals one at a time while a classical planner fills in the low-level moves, keeping the LLM away from physics it cannot reliably reason about. YOLO-MARL takes a different angle — ask the LLM once before training starts to write out strategies and helper functions, then let the robots train using those written rules without calling the model again during the whole learning process, which is much faster.

Moving many robots without collisions

Getting dozens or thousands of robots to move through a shared space without hitting each other is its own hard problem, separate from who does what. Each robot's path must not cross another's at the same moment, and when plans conflict the system must resolve the conflict fast.

SILLM trains a neural network to copy the move decisions of a classical search-based planner on many scenarios at once, adds a way for nearby robots to share information, and runs on a graphics processor so it can plan for ten thousand robots in parallel — something classical methods cannot do at speed. SIGMA uses a branch of mathematics (sheaf theory) that describes how each robot's local view of the world connects to every other robot's view; a neural network then lets each robot act as though it can see the bigger picture, even though it can only see a small area. Work Smarter Not Harder takes a simpler path: a small neural network learns from a good classical planner, and a lightweight collision-fixer checks and corrects any one-step conflict in the network's output before it actually happens — and this simple setup outperforms far more elaborate learned methods.

Holding a formation and reshaping it

A group of robots often needs to move as a unit — a triangle, a line, a ring — while still fitting through narrow gaps and avoiding obstacles. The formation shape that works in open ground may be wrong for a corridor. Coordinated Multi-Robot Navigation with Formation Adaptation handles this with two layers of control: one layer uses a graph neural network to decide when and how to reshape the group, while each robot independently steers itself and avoids collisions at the lower layer. A spring-damper model (borrowed from physics) makes the shape change smooth rather than abrupt. ZeroCAP handles the related task of placing robots into a shape that fits a spoken command — "form a ring around that object" — by combining a vision-language model that understands the scene with a language model that interprets the instruction, then computing target positions for each robot without any task-specific training.

Splitting jobs across a mixed team including people

Some teams are not all robots. A person and several robots must share a workspace and divide tasks in real time. When someone on the team is slower, or unavailable, or the task changes mid-way, the assignment must be updated. Adaptive Task Allocation in Multi-Human Multi-Robot Teams uses a two-level learning system: the top level assigns tasks based on who is best at what; the bottom level watches for changes and reassigns when needed. Hypergraph-Based Coordinated Task Allocation models robots, people, and task locations all together as a hypergraph — a kind of graph where one connection can link several nodes at once — and trains the robots to pick tasks and avoid people at the same time, using a single shared learning process. TriHRCBot focuses on the very specific case of a robot holding an object for two people simultaneously, watching both with cameras and continuously moving the object to a spot where both can reach it.

Running the plan when the network or a robot fails

A robot team that needs a central server to function will stop the moment that server is unavailable. Several papers remove that single point of failure. Cooperative Distributed Model Predictive Control has each robot solve the shared control problem on its own chip and trade partial answers with neighbors using a mathematical splitting method (ADMM) until all robots agree — no central computer needed. FogROS2-PLR handles unreliable networks by sending each computation request to several cloud servers over several paths at once and using whichever answer arrives first, throwing the slower ones away.

Where it stands

Language models have made it much easier to write a sensible first plan for a mixed robot team from plain instructions, especially when paired with a logic checker that catches impossible steps. Moving many robots without collisions has improved sharply, with learned methods now approaching classical planners in quality and beating them in speed at large scale. Formation control and reshaping in real environments works in tested settings. What remains hard: teams that must reorganize themselves fully after multiple failures at once, teams that are much larger than tested so far (the jump from a hundred to ten thousand is still not smooth for many methods), and coordination in dynamic spaces where the environment changes faster than any plan can keep up with.

Teaching legs and humanoids to move

Move · 271 papers in this theme

271 papers · move — how the robot uses its body to travel through the physical world

The problem — in depth

Walking looks easy. Humans do it without thinking. But when you try to build a machine that walks, you run into a set of hard problems fast.

A walking robot is always about to fall. At any moment, one or more feet are off the ground. The robot has to push against the floor with just the right amount of force, at just the right time, to keep its body from tipping. That force changes every fraction of a second. Miss the timing and the robot goes down.

Terrain makes this worse. Real ground is not flat. There are steps, gravel, wet surfaces, slopes, and random objects. The robot cannot see the whole path ahead. It has to feel what the ground is doing under each foot and adjust on the fly. A small surprise — a patch of ice, a loose stone — can end a walk that was going fine.

Energy is a second hard problem. Moving a heavy body on legs burns a lot of power. The legs on a well-built robot have a natural swing to them, a pendulum-like motion that carries energy from one step into the next for free. Getting a computer-trained controller to find and use that natural swing, rather than fighting it, is not automatic. Most controllers trained by trial and error in a computer spend energy in ways that would exhaust a real machine quickly.

The third problem is the gap between the computer world and the real world. The standard method for teaching a robot to walk is to practice inside a physics simulator — a computer program that pretends to be the physical world. Simulation is fast, cheap, and safe. You can run millions of training steps in hours with no risk of damage. But the simulator is never quite right. It gets joint friction slightly wrong, motor delays slightly wrong, the give of the floor slightly wrong. A walking controller trained entirely in a fake world often fails the moment it meets the real one. This gap is called the sim-to-real gap, and closing it is one of the central puzzles in the field.

On top of all that, legged robots are now being asked to do more than walk. Humanoid robots need to carry things, open doors, and operate in spaces built for people. Four-legged robots need to climb ladders, push heavy boxes, and navigate rough outdoor ground. A separate hand-crafted controller for each task is too slow and too expensive to build. The field wants one controller that can handle many tasks.

All of this connects to the one big problem every robot has: take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do it fast, cheap, safe, and general. Legged locomotion sits squarely at the "move" part of that loop, but it reaches back into sensing (the robot must read its own body and the terrain) and forward into decisions (which skill to use, when to switch, how to combine walking with arm work). A robot that cannot walk reliably cannot do anything else reliably either.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Training in simulation, then moving to the real robot

The most common approach is to teach the robot inside a computer and then transfer the skill to real hardware. The key trick is to vary the conditions during simulator training — changing floor friction, motor strength, body weight, and other settings randomly — so the learned controller is wide enough to still work when the real world turns out to be slightly different from what the simulator assumed. This is called domain randomization.

"Berkeley Humanoid" is a clean example. The team built a mid-size humanoid with simple joints that a simulator could model accurately, trained a walking controller in Isaac Gym (a physics simulator), applied only mild condition randomization, and transferred directly to the real robot with no extra tuning. "Robust Ladder Climbing with a Quadrupedal Robot" used the same philosophy: train on a wide range of ladder angles and rung sizes in simulation, add hooks on the feet for grip, and move to real hardware without retraining. These papers show that when the robot hardware is designed to be easy to simulate, the transfer becomes much cleaner.

Building one controller for many tasks

A dedicated controller for each skill is not scalable. The field is pushing toward single networks that handle a wide range of commands and movements.

"HOVER" does this for humanoids by first training a teacher policy on a large library of full-body movements, then training a single student policy to match the teacher while accepting different command types — velocity, position, or individual joint angles — through one shared process. The result is a single controller that can switch between walking, carrying, and reaching.

"AllGaits" solves the same problem for four-legged robots. Instead of one controller per gait style, a single network controls a rhythm generator (a system that produces repeating beat patterns for the legs, called a Central Pattern Generator) whose settings select among nine different leg-beat patterns. One network, all known walking styles.

"MOVE" trains a single network to handle climbing, jumping, crawling, and stair-climbing for a cheap four-legged robot with only a small forward camera. It uses both camera images and body-sensor data together, and a technique called contrastive learning to let the robot infer what it cannot directly see from how its body is moving.

Closing the sim-to-real gap by modeling real hardware

Some papers attack the gap directly by making the simulator more accurate, rather than papering over inaccuracy with randomization.

"Cycloidal Quasi-Direct Drive Actuator Designs" is a good example. Cycloidal gears — a type of compact, high-force gear — behave in messy, hard-to-predict ways. The team trained a small neural network called an Actuator Network to learn the actual input-output behavior of their motor from real data, then used that learned model inside the simulator. This made the simulator match the real hardware much more closely.

"Legged Robot State Estimation with Invariant Extended Kalman Filter Using Neural Measurement Network" addresses a related gap: knowing where the robot is and how fast it is moving when the ground is slippery or soft. A small neural network watches sensor readings and estimates the robot's speed and foot contact, and feeds those estimates into a mathematical tracking filter (invariant extended Kalman filter). The math handles the overall tracking; the neural network handles the parts the math cannot model cleanly.

"Physically-Consistent Parameter Identification of Robots in Contact" fills another gap. Real robots have slightly wrong mass and inertia values in their simulators. This method figures out the true values using only motor data, without needing force sensors at the feet, by mathematically canceling out the effects of floor contact.

Using natural motion and passive physics to save energy

One of the cleanest insights in legged locomotion is that legs, when built right, want to swing at a certain rhythm for free. Fighting that rhythm costs energy. Using it saves energy.

"The Duke Humanoid" is built around this idea. The team designed the leg proportions so the natural pendulum swing matches a useful walking speed, then trained the controller to walk in a way that rides that swing rather than overriding it.

"Adaptive Energy Regularization for Autonomous Gait Transition" takes a simpler approach: add a single reward term during training that penalizes energy used per meter traveled, and let the robot figure out by trial and error how to move at any speed. The robot discovers on its own that switching between slow walking and fast trotting saves energy, without anyone writing rules about when to switch.

"Zippy," the smallest known power-autonomous two-legged robot, takes this to an extreme. It uses rounded feet and one motor, relying entirely on physical shape to stay balanced, with no sensors and no feedback. The passive structure of the body does the work.

Combining walking with arm work

Humanoid robots need to walk and use their arms at the same time. This is harder than it sounds because the legs and arms share the body's balance, and training one policy to handle both tends to hurt the precision of the arms.

"Mobile-TeleVision" solves this by training the legs and arms separately. A model trained on human teleoperation data learns typical arm motion patterns, and a separate walking policy learns to stay balanced while those patterns play out. The two are combined at run time.

"Real-Time Whole-Body Control of Legged Robots with Model-Predictive Path Integral Control" (MPPI) takes a different route. It runs thousands of random action sequences in a simulator on multiple processor cores in parallel, scores them by how well they achieve the goal, and picks the best — all fast enough to run on the real robot in real time. No separate training for walking versus reaching; the planner handles both at once.

Where it stands

Legged robots can now walk over rough ground, climb ladders, switch between gaits, and do basic arm tasks — all using controllers trained mostly in simulation and moved to real hardware. The sim-to-real gap is narrowing, and single controllers are getting broader. What is still open: robots that can sustain useful work for hours in unpredictable outdoor settings, humanoids that can match a person's arm speed and walking smoothness at the same time, and methods for quickly teaching a robot a new movement skill without retraining from scratch.

Planning motion in real time

Move · 115 papers in this theme

115 papers · move — this is the part of the loop where the robot's decisions become actual physical movement

The problem — in depth

Every robot that moves has to answer one question over and over, many times each second: given where I am right now, what should my joints do next? This sounds simple, but it is deeply hard.

The right answer is to solve a precise math problem: find the exact sequence of joint commands that gets the robot to its goal as fast and safely as possible, while avoiding every obstacle and respecting every limit the hardware has. That problem, solved exactly, takes far too long to compute. A robot arm reacting to a moving ball, or a mobile robot weaving through a crowd, cannot wait. It needs an answer now, then again in a fraction of a second, then again. So the field is always chasing the same tradeoff: the more correct the answer, the slower it arrives; the faster the answer, the rougher the motion.

This gets harder still because the world keeps changing in ways the robot did not expect. A person shifts direction. A crane load starts to swing. Wind pushes a drone off course. A leaf moves. An object turns out to be heavier than expected. The math the planner used a moment ago now describes a world that no longer exists.

There is also a structural problem that shows up across many of these papers. A robot is not a point in space — it is a body with links and joints, and each link can collide with something. Checking whether a proposed path is safe requires testing the whole body's shape against every nearby obstacle, and doing that for millions of candidate paths per second is expensive. In tight spaces, where nearly every path grazes something, the planner's job is even harder: it must thread through gaps that may be barely wide enough, or find that the only way through is to move something out of the way first.

These problems connect to the one big challenge every robot faces. A robot must take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do it fast, cheap, safe, and general. Motion planning sits right at the junction of decide and move. A plan that arrives too late is useless. A plan that ignores the body's limits cannot be followed. A plan that ignores disturbances will drift. The 115 papers in this topic are all trying to close that gap.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Trying many paths in parallel and scoring them

The most common approach is to stop trying to find the one exact best path and instead try a large number of short candidate paths very quickly, score each one, and pick the winner. This is called sampling-based control or, in its most common modern form, model predictive path integral control (MPPI).

The key insight is that modern processors — especially graphics chips (GPUs) — can evaluate thousands of candidate paths at the same time. Each path is a possible short future, lasting maybe a second or two. The system scores each one by how close it gets to the goal, how safe it is, and how smooth it is. The winner, or a weighted blend of the good ones, becomes the robot's next action. Then the whole process repeats.

"Trajectory Planning with Signal Temporal Logic Costs Using Deterministic Path Integral Optimization" is a direct example: the team used this sampling approach to plan paths that satisfy time-based rules — such as "be in zone A between seconds 3 and 7" — without having to smooth or rewrite those rules into a form standard solvers can handle. "High Speed Robotic Table Tennis Swinging Using Model Predictive Control" shows the same idea applied to a physical task that demands fast re-planning: every time the robot gets a new estimate of where the ball will land, it re-solves the best swing from scratch. "Take Your Best Shot: Sampling-Based Planning for Autonomous Photography" applies it to a camera robot choosing where to stand for a good shot.

Handling tight spaces by breaking them into simpler pieces

When the space the robot must move through is narrow or cluttered, the most common approach fails because most sampled paths collide with something. Several papers tackle this by restructuring the problem so the planner never confronts a hard collision all at once.

"Narrow Passage Path Planning Using Collision Constraint Interpolation" is the clearest example: it splits the environment into simple convex regions and feeds the collision-avoidance rules into the solver one at a time, solving an easier version of the problem before adding the next rule. This keeps the solver from getting stuck at the start. "Improving Efficiency in Path Planning: Tangent Line Decomposition Algorithm" takes a different angle — it breaks the full multi-obstacle problem into a chain of single-obstacle steps, each solved by a simple tangent line, so the search space shrinks dramatically. "Search-Based Path Planning in Interactive Environments Among Movable Obstacles" (PAMO*) deals with rooms full of objects by noticing that most objects are far from the robot's path and can be ignored during search, focusing attention only where it matters.

Building a model of how the body and world behave — and using it

Rather than planning blindly, many systems first learn or derive a model of how the robot's body or the world responds to commands, then use that model to make smarter guesses.

"iKap: Kinematics-Aware Planning with Imperative Learning" bakes the math of how the robot's joints translate into movement directly into the training of a neural network, so the network never proposes a path the hardware cannot follow. "SEB-Naver: A SE(2)-based Local Navigation Framework for Car-like Robots on Uneven Terrain" derives the math for how a car-like robot steers on sloped ground and uses it to plan smooth paths that respect real steering limits. "Dynamic End Effector Trajectory Tracking for Small-Scale Underwater Vehicle-Manipulator Systems" builds a full model of how a robot arm mounted on an underwater vehicle interacts with the water around it and uses that model to design a controller. "Near Time-Optimal Hybrid Motion Planning for Timber Cranes" models the hydraulic flow limits of a forestry crane and uses that model to plan fast motions that the pump can actually supply.

Canceling disturbances and model errors directly

Even the best plan drifts when the real world does not match the model. A separate family of approaches focuses on detecting that drift and correcting it in real time, without waiting for the planner to generate a new path.

"Collision Avoidance in Model Predictive Control Using Velocity Damper" adds a safety rule to the planner that considers not just how far the robot is from an obstacle but how fast it is closing in — catching unsafe situations that a distance-only rule misses. "Robust Orientation Control of Robot Manipulator Using Orientation Disturbance Observer" rebuilds a standard disturbance-canceling tool so it works directly with 3D rotation math, letting the arm resist outside forces trying to knock its tip off direction. Handling things by feel, not just by looking

Move · 112 papers in this theme

The problem — in depth

When you press a key on a keyboard, your fingers feel resistance and stop pushing before you break anything. When you peel a carrot, your hand adjusts grip strength ten times a second in response to how the peel pulls back. A robot that only tracks where its hand is in space — without any sense of how hard it is pressing — cannot do either of those things. It will snap the key, gouge the carrot, or fumble the peel entirely. This is the core problem: many real tasks are defined not by where you move but by how much force you apply, and the force changes in ways you cannot predict ahead of time.

This matters because the gap between robots that work in labs and robots that work in homes or factories is largely a gap in contact handling. Lab demos pick up objects sitting still on clean tables. Real jobs — plugging in a cable, wiping a counter, screwing a cap, separating stuck panels, caring for a person — all require the robot to push, slide, peel, or press against objects that push back. The object's resistance changes as the task goes on. A pure position controller (one that only tracks "move hand to point X") has no way to respond to that feedback. It either pushes too hard and breaks something, or not hard enough and fails.

The deeper issue is that vision alone cannot fill this gap. A camera can tell you roughly where an object is, but it cannot tell you whether the grip is slipping, how much a part is flexing, or whether a connector is seated. The hand itself blocks the view. Contact happens at sub-millimeter scales that cameras cannot resolve. And contact events happen faster than any vision-based loop can respond.

This connects directly to the one big challenge every robot faces: taking in an unclear, changing world, deciding what to do, and moving a real body — all fast, cheap, safe, and general. Touch and force close the loop between the body and the world in a way that vision cannot. Without them, the "move" part of the loop is open: the robot acts but does not truly feel what is happening. With them, the robot has a signal it can react to, a way to detect when something is going wrong, and a basis for deciding whether to push harder, ease off, or stop.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Recording force during human demonstrations, then teaching the robot to replay it

The most direct fix is to capture what a person does — including how hard they press — and train the robot to copy it. The key challenge is that standard motion-capture setups record where the hand goes but not the forces involved.

ForceMimic solves this with a wearable glove-like device (ForceCapture) that a person wears while doing the task, recording both motion and force without a robot present. A controller (HybridIL) then trains the robot to produce both a target position and a target force at each step. Learning Visuotactile Skills With Two Multifingered Hands (HATO) takes a similar path for two-handed dexterous tasks: they built a low-cost system from off-the-shelf parts and touch-sensing prosthetic hands, recorded human operators, and trained the robot to replay both the visual and touch signals. Force-Conditioned Diffusion Policies for Compliant Sheet Separation Tasks trains on expert recordings that include force data, then uses a generative model called a diffusion policy — a model that learns to build up actions step by step from noise — to produce actions that respond to live force readings at run time.

2. Building sensors that are cheap, robust, and easy to replace

A second major thread is hardware: making touch sensors that work at all, hold up to repeated use, and do not require a complete software overhaul when they wear out.

AnySkin separates the soft sensing skin from the electronics so the skin peels off and snaps back on without tools or recalibration, and skills trained on one skin still work on a fresh one. PolyTouch packs a camera-based touch surface, a microphone, and a small outward-facing camera into one finger-shaped unit, feeding all three signals into a single control policy. ORCA is an open-source, tendon-driven hand with 17 joints and built-in touch sensors, with self-tightening joints and automatic cable tensioning so it survives long training runs without breaking — designs and instructions are fully public. HumanFT fits a tiny camera, pressure circuit, and vibration circuit into a fingertip-sized soft shell small enough for humanoid hands.

3. Blending touch with vision so each fills the other's gaps

Neither cameras nor touch sensors are sufficient alone. Cameras are blocked by the robot's own hand; touch sensors only measure what is already in contact. Combining them lets each cover the other's weak spots.

ViTa-Zero figures out the exact position and angle (pose) of a held object without any touch training data: it starts from a camera-only pose estimate, then uses touch sensor readings to pull the estimate toward the contact point and joint angle readings to push it away from impossible positions. Adaptive Visuo-Tactile Fusion with Predictive Force Attention automatically turns up the weight on whichever sensor is more useful at each moment — it watches force readings as the task unfolds and shifts attention without any human labels. FusionSense rebuilds the 3D shape of transparent or very dark objects — things cameras cannot see well — by first building a rough shape from images, then using touch readings to fix the fine surface details. Beyond Sight (FuSe) takes a general robot AI trained mostly on camera data and fine-tunes it to also use touch and sound by training it to describe what each sensor is reading in plain language.

4. Learning to control force and softness directly

Rather than just sensing force, some work teaches the robot controller to actively manage how firm or soft it should be at each moment — a property called compliance (how much the arm gives way when pushed).

Adaptive Compliance Policy learns from human demonstrations how much the arm should give at each point in space and time — a compliance profile — then uses a diffusion policy to turn that profile into smooth motions that avoid large contact forces. TacDiffusion trains a generative model on recordings of the three-dimensional forces and torques felt during successful peg insertions, so the model learns to generate good force sequences for new insertions the robot has never seen, including new part shapes. Efficient Online Learning of Contact Force Models fits a simple force model to sensor data in real time on a GPU, fast enough to update during the task itself, which matters for precise connector insertion where even small errors in the force model cause failure.

5. Sharing knowledge across sensors and making pre-trained features

Many labs use different touch sensors, which means data collected in one lab cannot be used in another. Several papers tackle this transfer problem directly.

ACROSS converts each sensor's signals into a shared 3D shape — a mesh showing how the sensor surface bends under contact — so old data from a discontinued sensor can be reused to train models for a newer one. Contrastive Touch-to-Touch Pretraining trains a model on two different sensors touching the same objects, teaching it to map both sensors' signals to the same shared internal space. Canonical Representation and Force-Based Pretraining of 3D Tactile for Dexterous Visuo-Tactile Policy Learning takes raw 3D touch data, converts it to a simpler standard form, then pre-trains a feature extractor by teaching it to predict forces from touch data alone before fine-tuning on specific tasks — reducing the number of demonstrations needed.

Where it stands

Force and touch sensing in robots has moved from a mostly hardware question to a mostly learning question. Researchers can now build usable, low-cost touch sensors and record human demonstrations that include force. Diffusion-based policies that condition on force have shown clear results on precise tasks like peg insertion and connector plugging that pure position control cannot handle. The remaining hard parts are reliability over long runs, generalization to surfaces and objects very different from training, and making touch signals transfer cleanly across sensor types and robot hands — progress on all three is active but not complete.

One flexible tool for both seeing and acting

12 papers · move — how the robot turns plans into physical actions

The problem — in depth

A robot arm reaching for a cup does not face one clear best path. There are dozens of paths that all work. A human picks one without thinking. A robot needs a method that can hold all those valid options in mind and then commit to one of them smoothly.

The oldest approach is to compute an average: if a robot has learned from many examples of humans doing a task, it can try to pick the action that sits in the middle of what humans did. That works when there is one clear answer. When there are many equally good answers, averaging collapses them into something that is none of them — like averaging a vote of "go left" and "go right" and ending up going straight into a wall. The robot freezes, twitches, or follows a shaky in-between path that makes real tasks fail.

The broader problem runs deeper. A robot has to do four things at once: take in a messy, changing world through cameras and sensors; build a picture of what is happening; decide what to do; and move a physical body to do it — all in real time, on limited power, without breaking anything or anyone nearby. Each of those four steps has its own hard problems. This topic sits at the crossing of all four.

The specific hard point here is that the world the robot must model and act in is full of gaps. Cameras only see surfaces. Sensors go noisy. Hidden things — the weight of an object, the slip of a floor, the position of a person who just walked in — change the physics of the task. A robot that builds an internal model of what will happen next (called a world model) can plan ahead without touching the real world, but only if that model is honest about what it does not know and can update fast when something new appears.

The field has spent years watching robots fail at tasks they seemed to understand in training and break completely the moment something small changed — a different table surface, a slightly heavier object, a new room layout. The root cause is almost always the same: the robot's internal picture of the world is wrong, and it has no good way to know that it is wrong or fix it.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Building and using an internal model of the world

Several papers train the robot not just to act but to predict what happens next — to build a model inside its own computing that simulates the effects of its actions before it takes them. This internal model (a world model) lets the robot plan in its head, practice situations it has never seen in real life, and catch bad plans before they cause real damage.

LUMOS uses a world model to let a robot practice tasks in simulation that never appeared in the original recorded data, using language and image goals to label that experience in new ways. LIMT extends the same idea across many tasks at once: a single world model absorbs language instructions so the robot can share what it learned in one task to help with another. X-MOBILITY trains a world model to understand environments from large amounts of unlabeled data, then adds a smaller set of human examples just for the control step, so it can handle new environments without retraining from scratch.

The payoff is that a robot with a good world model is not limited to situations it has practiced. It can reason forward and reject bad options before they happen.

Catching hidden changes and unfamiliar situations

A world model is only trustworthy inside the situations it was trained on. When something new appears — a heavier load, a slippery surface, a person behaving unexpectedly — the model's predictions go wrong and the robot does not know it.

Privileged-Dreamer addresses this by having the robot watch its own recent history of sensor readings and actions and use that to estimate hidden variables (things it cannot measure directly, like payload or friction). Those estimates feed both the robot's decision and its internal model, keeping both aligned with what is actually happening. Bounding Distributional Shifts takes a different angle: before the robot commits to a plan, a small detector checks whether the plan would take the robot into situations it has never seen. If it would, the plan is discarded. These two approaches — estimate the hidden thing, or detect when you are lost — together let the robot stay honest about the limits of its knowledge.

Reducing the need for labeled data

Training a robot usually means recording thousands of examples that log exactly what action the human took at every moment. That is expensive and slow to collect.

Imitation Learning with Limited Actions via Diffusion Planners and Deep Koopman Controllers splits the job in two: a diffusion model (a method that works by starting from noise and gradually refining it into a good output) plans over states that the robot can observe, without needing action labels. A second, smaller controller — trained on only a handful of labeled examples — translates that plan into actual motor commands using a compact mathematical structure (Koopman operator) that stays well-behaved. The result is that the expensive labeled data requirement drops sharply.

Planning by imagining future pictures

Rather than reasoning in abstract numbers, some approaches plan by generating pictures of what the world should look like at each step of a task, then steering the robot toward those pictures.

RoboEnvision generates a short video of a task in key snapshots, fills in the motions between them, and then reads off the joint angles the robot needs. Chain-of-Imagination uses a language model to break a long instruction into steps and an image generator to create a visual target for each step, so the robot always has a concrete picture to move toward. This approach makes long, multi-step tasks easier to handle because each step becomes a clear local target rather than a vague direction.

Letting curiosity guide what the robot learns

SR-AIF and Curiosity-Driven Imagination both address the case where the robot only gets a signal when it actually succeeds at a task — which happens rarely when it is first learning. SR-AIF uses a method called active inference, which drives the robot to act in ways that reduce its own uncertainty about the future, pushing it naturally toward goal states even without frequent rewards. Curiosity-Driven Imagination adds a curiosity module that points the robot toward situations it does not understand yet. When it finds something new, a high-level planning system adds a new rule to its knowledge and generates a training signal to teach the robot the motion that goes with it.

Where it stands

World models and diffusion-based planning have moved from research curiosities to methods that beat older approaches on real robot tasks, especially when the supply of labeled data is limited or the environment changes. The main gaps that remain are speed — world models are still expensive to run on small onboard computers — and reliability outside the situations they were trained on. Detecting when a model's predictions have gone stale is a solved problem only in narrow cases. Combining fast models, good uncertainty awareness, and broad coverage of real-world variation in a single system is still open work.

Grasping and dexterous hands

Move · 242 papers in this theme

242 papers · move — the physical act of touching, gripping, and handling objects

The problem — in depth

Pick up a mug. Your hand knows, without thinking, where to close around it, how hard to squeeze so it doesn't slip, and how to hold it if you are about to drink versus about to hand it to someone. You do this in a pile of dishes, for mugs you have never seen before, for ceramic or plastic or paper cups, and you adjust the moment the mug starts sliding. A robot does none of this naturally.

The core difficulty is that every object in the world is different. Shape, weight, slipperiness, and fragility all change how the hand should close. Most robot hands today use simple two-finger grippers that either pinch or suction. They work in warehouses, where every item is the same, in the same place, every time. Move them to a kitchen, a hospital, or a grocery aisle and they fail constantly. Objects are piled on each other, tucked behind other things, or made of glass, cloth, or foam that the sensors cannot read clearly.

Transparent objects — glass bottles, plastic cups — are a special trap. The cameras that measure depth do so by bouncing light off surfaces. Glass and clear plastic let light through or bend it in unpredictable ways. The robot sees the object but gets a wrong or missing measurement of where it actually sits in space. It reaches, and misses, or crushes.

Then there is the question of intent. Picking up a knife by the blade is wrong even if it is technically graspable. The right grip depends on what comes next — cutting, passing to a person, storing. A robot that only asks "where can I close my fingers?" will choose the wrong grip for the job. This connects to the deep problem every robot faces: it is not enough to move the body; the movement must serve a purpose that the robot understands.

Multi-fingered hands, which look like human hands and can do the fine work humans do, have existed in labs for decades. The mechanical design is hard, the control is harder, and they break often. They cost far more than simple grippers and require engineers to maintain them. This is why they almost never leave the lab, even though fine tasks — folding clothes, peeling vegetables, opening jars — require them.

Collecting training data is its own problem. You cannot program a multi-fingered hand to fold a shirt by writing down the exact finger positions step by step. The motion is too complex and too sensitive to small differences in the shirt's shape. Researchers instead have humans perform tasks and record the motions so the robot can learn from the recordings. But teleoperation of two hands with many fingers at once is very hard to do well, and getting thousands of recordings takes months.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Reasoning about purpose to choose the right grip

A robot that only looks at shape will pick a grip that is easy to reach but wrong for the job. Several papers here teach the robot to first ask "what is this object for, and what is the task?" and use that answer to pick where and how to grasp.

AffordGrasp uses a large language model to work out what the user wants, then finds the right part of the object to grip before computing the hand position. RTAGrasp stores a memory of how humans grip objects in video, finds the most similar stored example for the given task, and maps that grip onto the new object — all without any task-specific training. Task-Oriented 6-DoF Grasp Pose Detection in Clutters built a large labeled dataset of over 2 million grasps across 6 task types and 198 objects, and shows that when you train with task labels, the robot picks hand positions that actually serve what comes next. PhyGrasp goes a step further and adds material properties — weight, slipperiness — as text alongside the object's 3D shape, so the grip accounts for physics, not just geometry.

Seeing through clutter and transparent materials

Before a hand can go anywhere, the robot needs to know exactly where the object is. In messy bins and with see-through materials, that is far harder than it sounds.

DA-Fusion runs color and depth images through separate processing streams, then a flexible attention step (a part of the network that learns to weight each pixel by how useful it is) merges the two streams automatically. This beats either channel alone for pulling objects out of cluttered bins. TransDiff takes the noisy depth that a sensor gives for a transparent object, combines it with edges, surface shapes, and outlines extracted from the color image, and feeds the whole thing to a model that gradually fills in a correct depth map. Monocular Depth Estimation and Segmentation for Transparent Objects with Iterative Semantic and Geometric Fusion runs two branches — one for shape, one for depth — that correct each other in several passes, rather than making one prediction and stopping. vMF-Contact adds a confidence score to every predicted grasp point and distinguishes between "I am uncertain because the sensor was noisy" and "I am uncertain because I have never seen this type of object," which helps the robot know when to trust itself and when to ask for help.

Learning from human demonstrations at scale

The motions needed for fine grasping are too complex to write by hand. The field's answer is: watch humans do it and learn. But getting enough recordings is the bottleneck.

EgoMimic has a person wear glasses with built-in cameras, records that person doing tasks, aligns the person's hand motion to the robot's arm geometry, and mixes those recordings with a small set of actual robot recordings — dramatically increasing training data at low cost. Unified models that work across objects and hand types

Training a separate model for each object, each task, or each robot hand does not scale. Several papers here build single systems that generalize.

D(R,O) Grasp learns one model that takes the hand's joint layout and the object's 3D shape as inputs and outputs where and how to grip — the same model then runs on hands with different numbers of fingers without retraining. GraspSAM takes a large image-segmentation model, adds small extra layers, and turns it into a grasp predictor that accepts a point, a box, or a word as a user prompt and works on objects it was never trained on. UniAff builds one system that covers both tools and jointed objects (drawers, scissors), using a large multimodal model to read images and say where to touch and how to move — replacing two separate, incompatible pipelines with one. Robot Utility Models trains five manipulation skills on large, high-quality demonstration sets, then deploys them in new homes without any retraining, using a language model to watch the robot and call for a retry when something goes wrong.

Fine multi-step manipulation with multi-fingered hands

Some tasks require more than a single grip: repositioning an object between the fingers, peeling, folding, catching.

Vegetable Peeling designs the repositioning step and the final holding step together, so the hand ends in a grip suitable for the next action rather than any stable grip. DROP skips pre-training entirely and searches for good finger movements in real time at each step, guided by a camera estimate of the object's current pose. Catch It! trains a whole-body controller — moving base, arm, and twelve-finger hand — in simulation across many thrown object shapes and throw angles, then moves it directly to a real robot. General-Purpose Clothes Manipulation with Semantic Keypoints identifies named points on garments from a depth camera and lets a language model plan what sequence of steps to take, reaching well beyond fixed-shape objects to soft, deformable ones.

Where it stands

The field has made real progress on several hard pieces: robots can now pick unfamiliar objects from cluttered bins more reliably, transparent objects are no longer invisible to the pipeline, and task-aware grasping is moving out of theory into working systems. The biggest remaining gaps are in hands that are cheap and tough enough for real use outside a lab, in collecting enough training data for complex two-handed fine tasks without enormous human effort, and in combining all the pieces — seeing, reasoning about purpose, and executing precise finger motion — into one system that handles the full range of objects a person deals with every day.

Making training examples cheaper

Across everything · 506 papers in this theme

506 papers · a rule across everything (sense / decide / move) — cuts the cost of teaching robots

The problem — in depth

Every robot that learns to do something by watching people needs a large set of recorded examples. A person physically guides the robot through the task, or the person does the task themselves while sensors record every movement. Then those recordings are fed to a learning algorithm that builds a policy — a set of rules for what to do in each situation.

The core trouble is that this process is slow, expensive, and does not scale. A single arm task, like picking up a cup and placing it on a shelf, might need hundreds of good recordings before a robot can do it reliably. Getting those recordings means finding willing people, setting up equipment, running the session, checking the recordings, and throwing away the ones that are noisy or wrong. Scale that to dozens of tasks and multiple robot bodies, and the cost becomes the main thing blocking the whole field from moving forward.

Why is collecting examples so hard? Three reasons compound each other.

First, robots are clumsy to operate. Moving a robot arm by hand, or through a controller, is tiring and unintuitive. The person giving the demonstration does not move the way they would move their own hand, so the motion they produce is often jerky or imprecise. A learning algorithm trained on bad motion data learns bad habits.

Second, the person giving the demonstration has no feedback while they are doing it. They cannot see whether their movement was clean enough, or whether the sensor data it produced will actually be useful for training. They find out the data was bad only after the session ends and someone checks the files. That means repeating the whole session.

Third, examples collected from one robot body usually cannot directly teach a different robot body, and examples from a person's bare hands usually cannot teach a robot at all without extra conversion steps. So even if you have thousands of examples, you may not be able to reuse them across the robots you have.

This matters because it connects directly to the one thing every robot must do: take in a world it does not fully understand, decide what to do, and move a physical body to carry that action out — all quickly, cheaply, and without failing. The example-collection bottleneck slows all three parts. Without enough examples, the robot does not learn to sense the world well. It does not learn to make good decisions. And it does not learn the precise physical motions that actually work. Every other advance in robot learning — better algorithms, bigger models, faster computers — hits a ceiling if there are not enough good examples to learn from.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Better rigs for recording demonstrations

One approach is to make the act of recording a demonstration easier and higher quality — better tools so each example a person gives is more likely to be usable.

ARCap takes this direction directly: an augmented reality headset shows the person live visual signals while they are demonstrating. The signals tell them in the moment whether their motion is clean enough for training. This means the person can adjust on the fly instead of discovering the recording was bad afterward. The result is fewer wasted sessions and cleaner data from each one.

The paper "How to Train Your Robots?" takes a more analytical approach: the researchers recorded the same set of tasks using three different methods — moving the arm by hand, using a VR controller, and using a joystick — then trained and tested robot policies from each set. Understanding which method produces data that leads to better learning is itself a way to stop wasting effort on the wrong recording approach.

Diff-DAgger (from "Diff-Dagger: Uncertainty Estimation With Diffusion Policy for Robotic Manipulation") attacks a related problem: when to ask a human to step in and give more examples during live operation. Most systems ask too often, which is tiring, or too rarely, which means the robot stays stuck. Diff-DAgger measures how uncertain the robot's own internal process is at each moment and calls for a human only when that uncertainty is genuinely high. This means human effort is spent only where it is most needed.

2. Making one demonstration go further

A second approach keeps the number of demonstrations small and instead finds ways to squeeze more learning out of each one.

SPOT ("SE(3) Pose Trajectory Diffusion for Object-Centric Manipulation") makes demonstrations more portable. Instead of recording what the robot's joints did, it records how the target object moved in 3D space. That description of the task is body-independent — it can teach a different robot, or even learn from videos of a person's bare hands, with as few as eight demonstrations. This dramatically widens the pool of possible recordings.

The Human-Agent Joint Learning paper takes a related approach during collection itself. A trained helper agent shares control of the robot with the human operator, gradually taking over more of the work as it improves. The operator does less and less over time while still producing useful demonstrations. Each hour of human effort produces more training signal than it would if the person were working alone.

"From Imitation to Refinement" addresses what happens when the examples run out and the policy still is not good enough. Instead of collecting more demonstrations, it freezes the policy learned from the existing demonstrations and trains a second, lighter policy that watches the robot in action and makes small corrections. The corrections are learned through trial and error, not more human demonstrations. This extends the value of the original data.

3. Generating fake training data in simulation

A third direction bypasses real-world collection entirely: build a computer simulation that is realistic enough to generate useful training examples automatically, at almost no cost per example.

The Stonefish simulator and OceanSim both do this for underwater robots, a domain where real collection is especially expensive. Stonefish adds realistic sensor models — including cameras that report only changes rather than full frames — and automatic labeling of every simulated frame. OceanSim runs on graphics cards and models how underwater cameras and sonar physically work, creating realistic sensor data without ever getting a real robot wet.

"Domain Randomization for Object Detection in Manufacturing Applications" applies the same idea to factory robots. It generates synthetic training images by randomly varying material appearance, lighting, camera angle, and background — a technique called domain randomization. A detector trained only on these generated images then works on real factory scenes. The paper systematically tests which random variations matter most, giving future researchers a guide for building their own synthetic datasets.

"Learning to Swim" and AnyCar both use simulation to train control policies that transfer to real robots. The underwater vehicle paper trains in a fast parallel physics simulator with randomly varied conditions, then deploys the policy directly on a real vehicle with no extra tuning. AnyCar trains a single neural network on many different virtual vehicles with different physics, then fine-tunes it to a real vehicle using only a small number of real examples.

4. Refining policies after the examples run out

A fourth approach trains an initial policy from whatever demonstrations exist, then improves it further through trial and error — so the robot keeps getting better without needing more human recordings.

FLaRe takes a general robot policy trained by copying demonstrations and continues training it using reinforcement learning (trial-and-error learning) driven by sparse signals — just a signal for whether the task succeeded or not, with no detailed instructions. This extends the value of the original demonstrations by letting the robot practice on its own.

ExploRLLM uses a large language model to write an initial control program and simplify what the robot senses, giving the trial-and-error learner a far better starting point than random exploration. The result learns faster and transfers to a real robot without extra training.

Where it stands

The field has made real progress on all four fronts: better recording tools reduce the cost of each human session, portability tricks like object-centric representations let one demonstration teach more than one robot, simulation closes gaps in domains where real collection is dangerous or slow, and trial-and-error fine-tuning extends the life of the examples that do exist. What is still open is whether any of these approaches can scale to the full range of tasks a general robot needs — most systems are still tested on a narrow set of table-top tasks, and none have shown that a single example-collection strategy covers everything from precise assembly to outdoor navigation to helping people eat. The combination of cheaper recording and cheaper simulation is narrowing the gap, but the gap remains large.

Safety and being sure it won't fail

Across everything · 267 papers in this theme

267 papers · a rule that cuts across all parts of the loop: sense, decide, and move

The problem — in depth

Every robot has to do four things at once: take in what the world looks like right now, decide what to do, move its body, and do all of this fast, cheap, and without hurting anyone. Safety is not one of those four steps — it is the condition that has to hold during every single one of them, at every moment, without exception.

That sounds simple, but it is very hard in practice. The world is not predictable. People walk out from behind doors. Maps are wrong. Sensors fail. Other drivers do unexpected things. A robot arm can drift a centimeter off its planned path. Any one of these can turn a normal action into a dangerous one.

The problem gets worse because modern robots are no longer built from simple rule-following code. They use learned systems — networks trained on data — that can generalize to new situations but cannot give you a written-out proof of what they will do. They also now use large language models, the same kind that power chatbots, to understand spoken instructions and write out plans. These are powerful, but they have no built-in sense of the physical world. A language model does not know that telling a robot to drive faster into a crowd is dangerous. It knows that sentence as text, not as a physical consequence.

So the field now faces a gap: flexible, capable learned systems on one side, and the need for hard, provable safety on the other. You want the robot to be smart enough to handle new situations, but guaranteed enough that you would let it near people. Bridging that gap is what this whole topic is about.

There is also a second, less obvious problem: even when you write down safety rules, making sure the robot actually follows them at every step — not just most of the time — is extremely difficult. A robot doing a ten-step task has to stay safe at every step. One unsafe step is enough.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Hard math limits that block unsafe actions

The most direct approach is to put a mathematical wall around the robot that it cannot cross, no matter what the rest of the system tells it to do. These walls are called control barrier functions (CBFs). The idea is this: you write down a rule — a math formula — that says "as long as this number stays above zero, you are safe." The safety layer then filters every command the robot receives and changes it just enough to keep that number above zero. The robot's main controller still works, but it can never actually produce an unsafe outcome.

Papers like "Multi-Agent Obstacle Avoidance Using Velocity Obstacles and Control Barrier Functions" apply this to groups of robots that have to avoid each other — an older method suggested good directions to move, but had no proof; pairing it with CBFs added the proof. "Certificated Actor-Critic" uses the same math limits to shape how a learning agent is trained, so the agent learns to stay safe from the start rather than learning recklessly and hoping it works out. "Safe Navigation in Uncertain Crowded Environments Using Risk Adaptive CVaR Barrier Functions" goes further: in crowds, you do not know exactly where people will be, so the safety wall is made to grow and shrink based on how uncertain the situation is.

The weakness is that CBFs need a fixed setting — how tight is the wall — and one fixed setting cannot be right everywhere. "Learning to Refine Input Constrained Control Barrier Functions via Uncertainty-Aware Online Parameter Adaptation" and "Soft Actor-Critic-Based Control Barrier Adaptation for Robust Autonomous Navigation" both address this by having the robot learn to adjust the tightness of the wall in real time as it moves, based on what it is seeing.

Translating plain-language rules into formal checks

A different problem arises when humans give robots instructions in plain language — "go to the red chair before entering the kitchen" or "do not pick up sharp objects when children are nearby." A language model can read these, but reading is not the same as being guaranteed to follow them. Researchers have started converting these plain-language rules into a formal logical language — a kind of math for sequences of events — that a separate system can check and enforce.

"SELP: Generating Safe and Efficient Task Plans for Robot Agents with Large Language Models" converts safety rules into formal logic (a system called Linear Temporal Logic), then directly constrains the language model's output so it can only produce plans that satisfy those rules. "Verifiably Following Complex Robot Instructions with Foundation Models" does something similar for navigation: translate the spoken instruction into a formal spec, identify the named objects in the real world, and generate a path that is mathematically guaranteed to satisfy the spec. "Safety Aware Task Planning via Large Language Models in Robotics" takes a layered approach: a second AI watches the first AI's plan and flags any unsafe steps, while a CBF layer enforces limits at the hardware level below that.

Testing by deliberately trying to break the system

You cannot know where the weak points are unless you look for them on purpose. A growing set of papers deliberately tries to make robots fail — not to cause harm, but to find the holes before deployment does.

"Jailbreaking LLM-Controlled Robots" built an algorithm called ROBOPAIR that automatically searches for text inputs that cause a language-model-controlled robot to ignore its safety rules and take physically dangerous actions. They ran it against three real robot systems. The findings show that the safety rules built into current language models are not enough to protect against a determined attacker — or even an accidental one. "Generating Out-of-Distribution Scenarios Using Language Models" uses a language model to write unusual driving scenarios that real data almost never contains, then builds those scenarios in a simulator to test whether self-driving systems can handle them. "Realistic Extreme Behavior Generation for Improved AV Testing" takes a different angle: it starts from real recorded driving data and uses a learned model of driver behavior to generate synthetic crashes by slightly changing what each driver would have done.

"Robust Deep Reinforcement Learning in Robotics via Adaptive Gradient-Masked Adversarial Attacks" does this for learned robot controllers specifically: it builds an adversary that learns which parts of the robot's input matter most, then focuses its attack there — revealing exactly which inputs the controller is fragile to.

Knowing when you do not know

Several papers focus on a quieter kind of safety: the robot should know when it is not sure, and act more carefully when it is not. This matters because a robot that acts confidently on bad information is more dangerous than one that slows down when uncertain.

"UncAD: Towards Safe End-to-end Autonomous Driving via Online Map Uncertainty" adds a module that tracks how confident the system is in each part of its predicted map, and rejects path options that depend on parts of the map that might be wrong. "Evidential Uncertainty Estimation for Multi-Modal Trajectory Prediction" gives the prediction system a way to output not just a predicted path for other vehicles but also a number that says how confident that prediction is — and it learns to be better calibrated by giving harder training cases more weight. "Dynamic Tube MPC: Learning Tube Dynamics with Massively Parallel Simulation for Robust Safety in Practice" runs thousands of simulations to learn how much a real robot will drift from its planned path, and uses that learned drift to plan paths that still fit safely in the available space even in the worst case.

Faults, crowds, and multi-robot coordination

A robot operating alone is hard enough to make safe. Groups of robots add new failure modes: one robot's bad sensor reading can mislead the others; one robot going offline can leave a gap in coverage; signal interference can corrupt the shared information the group depends on.

"Distributed Fault-Tolerant Multi-Robot Cooperative Localization in Adversarial Environments" proves that its localization system keeps working even when some robots' sensors are hacked or radio signals are jammed. "CoDynTrust: Robust Asynchronous Collaborative Perception via Dynamic Feature Trust Modulus" handles the case where shared sensor data arrives late or out of order — it estimates how uncertain each piece of incoming data is and weighs it accordingly before merging it with local readings. "Decentralized Safe and Scalable Multi-Agent Control Under Limited Actuation" shows that hundreds of robots can each run a small learned safety layer locally, with no central coordinator, and still avoid collisions and permanent standstills.

Where it stands

Hard math safety limits (control barrier functions) now work reliably for fairly simple physical situations, and the field has gotten good at adapting them to learned systems. The harder open problems are at the language-model end of the pipeline: there is no reliable way yet to guarantee that a language-model-controlled robot will refuse a genuinely dangerous instruction under all conditions, as the jailbreaking work makes clear. Testing systems under deliberate attack and unusual conditions is gaining serious momentum, which is a healthy sign. What remains open is a clean, general method for connecting plain-language human intent all the way down to provably safe physical motion — the gap between what a robot is told and what it is guaranteed to do.

Soft and body-inspired machines

Across everything · 250 papers in this theme

250 papers · a rule across everything — how the body itself is built shapes what the robot can sense, decide, and do

The problem — in depth

Every robot body is a bargain. Make it rigid and you get strength, repeatability, and easy math. Make it soft and you get something else: the ability to squeeze through gaps, cushion a fall, stretch around odd shapes, and touch a person or a piece of fruit without leaving a bruise.

The hard bargain is that the real world does not care which choice you made. A robot sent to pull weeds in a greenhouse must bend between plant stems. A robot helping a surgeon must navigate slippery, shifting tissue without tearing it. A search robot squeezed into rubble must change its shape as the gap changes. A robot arm helping a person who has lost muscle strength must push and pull without ever pressing too hard.

Rigid metal arms and legs, which almost all industrial robots have used for decades, were designed for factories where every part sits in a fixed spot and the job never changes. When the space is uneven, the object is fragile, or the task demands contact with a living body, rigid machines either break what they touch or cannot reach it at all.

The deeper problem is that stiffness is not always good or bad — it depends on the moment. When you pick up a coffee cup, your hand is soft enough to wrap around the glass but stiff enough not to drop it. When you grip a door handle, you stiffen up more. Living muscles do this automatically. A rigid robot joint cannot. A fully soft joint cannot either, because it has no stiffness at all. The real target is a body that can change how stiff it is on demand — soft when it needs to give, firm when it needs to push.

This connects to the one big problem all robots face: taking in an unclear, changing world, deciding what to do, moving a real body, and doing it fast, cheap, safe, and general. Soft body research touches every one of those pieces. A soft fingertip with a built-in sensor solves sensing and safety at once. A robot that grows from its tip through a narrow pipe (called a vine robot, though none of these papers are on that specific design) solves motion in constrained spaces. A body that cushions contact makes the robot safer near people. A body that can be printed or assembled from modules lowers cost and raises generality.

The field is not one idea. It is a loose collection of groups — engineers, biologists, materials scientists, and control theorists — each attacking a different piece of the same goal: bodies that handle the world's messiness instead of demanding the world be clean.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

1. Muscles made from electricity, air, and fluid

Living muscles contract when a nerve fires. Researchers are building artificial muscles that do something similar using electricity, air pressure, or pressurized fluid.

One line of work uses a material that stretches when you apply voltage — called a dielectric elastomer (a stretchy electrical insulator that squishes when charged). The problem is that these need very high voltage, far more than a battery can give directly. "Portable, High-Frequency, and High-Voltage Control Circuits for Untethered Miniature Robots Driven by Dielectric Elastomer Actuators" solved this by stacking many small voltage steps in series to reach 1,800 volts on a board weighing only 2.5 grams — light enough for a crawling robot to carry itself.

A second type of artificial muscle pairs electricity with fluid. Called HASEL (hydraulically amplified self-healing electrostatic) actuators, these squeeze a pouch of fluid using electric charge, generating force. The catch is that one such muscle can only pull, not push. "Stretchable Electrohydraulic Artificial Muscle for Full Motion Ranges in Musculoskeletal Antagonistic Joints" dealt with this by pairing two of these muscles on opposite sides of a joint — one to pull, one to let go — plus electric clutches to coordinate the pair, reaching over three full cycles per second.

Air pressure is the oldest approach: pump air into a bendable tube and it curves or stiffens. "Anisotropic Stiffness and Programmable Actuation for Soft Robots Enabled by an Inflated Rotational Joint" used a clever geometry: an inflatable tube is shaped so that only part of it wrinkles when pressurized. The wrinkled region is softer than the smooth region, so the tube bends toward the wrinkled side, and the bend direction and stiffness can both be tuned by controlling which part wrinkles.

2. Cables and tendons pulling through a body

Many soft or semi-soft robots use cables — thin wires or cords run through the body and pulled by motors — the same way tendons work in a hand. The advantage is that motors can sit at the base, keeping the moving parts light.

The main challenge is that when cables share a path through multiple joints, pulling one joint can accidentally drag on another. "D3-ARM: High-Dynamic, Dexterous and Fully Decoupled Cable-Driven Robotic Arm" re-routed the cables so each motor connects to exactly one joint with no cross-talk, then added a tensioning mechanism to keep cables from going slack. This is a basic but hard engineering problem — the geometry of how cables run determines whether control is clean or messy.

For exosuits (soft, wearable frames that add muscle power to a person's limbs), cable routing is even more important because the cables bend as the person moves, and bending wastes energy through friction. "A Comparative Study of Pulley and Bowden Transmissions in a Novel Cable-Driven Exosuit, the Stillsuit" measured exactly how much energy is lost when cables run through flexible tubes versus over pulleys, finding that tubes waste 40 to 60 percent of the motor's energy just to friction. Routing over pulleys instead dramatically reduces that waste.

Cables also let you program how a gripper gives way. "Dexterous Assembly Using a Planar Hand Having Programmable Passive Compliance" uses opposing tendons on each finger so that the direction in which the finger yields can be set before a task by adjusting how tight each tendon is, with no sensors needed during contact.

3. Bodies that move like animals

Several papers take direct shape or motion ideas from living creatures and build robots around them.

Worms, fish, and snakes all move by sending a wave of bends along their body, which lets them push through dense or narrow spaces that a wheeled or legged robot cannot enter. "AquaMILR+" sends wave-like bends down a limbless body to swim in open water, and can stiffen or soften on command so it deforms around obstacles rather than getting stuck. A built-in buoyancy system — a syringe-screw that changes the robot's density — controls depth the way a fish's air bladder does.

"Leader-Follower Formation Enabled by Pressure Sensing in Free-Swimming Undulatory Robotic Fish" takes a swimming fish body and trains a small neural network (a type of learned controller) to steer just using water-pressure readings on the sides of the follower fish, with no camera. The network was trained by recording what a human controller did and then correcting the network each time it drifted — a training method called DAgger.

"Adaptive Concertina Locomotion of a Robotic Snake Through Narrow Uncertain Channels" mimics how a snake grips the sides of a tunnel to inch forward (concertina motion) and adjusts the wave pattern in real time by reading how hard the motors are working — a proxy for how tight the tunnel is.

At even smaller scales, "Feedback Control of a Single-Tail Bioinspired 59-mg Swimmer" built a robot lighter than a grain of rice that uses a shape-memory alloy tail — metal that bends when heated and springs back when cooled — to swim and steer itself.

4. Sensing inside a soft body

A soft robot that cannot sense what it is touching or how it is bent cannot be controlled precisely. This is a hard problem because most sensors are rigid or bulky, and putting them inside a soft body often makes that body stiffer or weaker.

"A Piezoresistive Printable Strain Sensor for Monitoring and Control of Soft Robotic Links" printed a sensor directly into a soft robot arm: a rubber base is printed, then silver ink is drawn onto it to make conductive traces that change electrical resistance when bent. The sensor is part of the arm, not glued to it.

"Milli-Scale AcousTac Sensing Using Soft Helmholtz Resonators" avoids electronics at the fingertip entirely. A tiny air-filled soft pocket on the fingertip shifts the pitch of a sound when pressed. A microphone elsewhere on the robot hears the pitch change — no wires at the contact point.

"A Magnetic-Actuated Vision-Based Whisker Array for Contact Perception and Grasping" uses eight thin flexible whiskers around a soft pad, moved by magnets. A camera watches how each whisker bends, and a small neural network reads those bend patterns to identify objects and choose a grip.

5. Simulation and math that keeps pace with soft physics

Controlling a soft robot requires knowing its shape at every moment, but soft bodies have nearly infinite ways to bend and compress, making them very hard to simulate. Two papers address this from different angles.

"Physics-Informed Split Koopman Operators for Data-Efficient Soft Robotic Simulation" builds a linear (straight-line math) model of a soft robot's behavior by combining two kinds of observations and baking in known physical laws, so the model stays accurate while needing far less data than a purely learned model.

"A Synergistic Framework for Learning Shape Estimation and Shape-Aware Whole-Body Control Policy for Continuum Robots" trains two neural networks together: one estimates the current shape of a flexible tube-like robot, the other uses that shape to decide how to move. Each network trains using the other's output, letting them improve together.

Where it stands

Soft and body-inspired robots can now move through narrow spaces, sense touch without rigid electronics, and change their stiffness on command — things rigid machines cannot do. Cable-driven arms and printable sensors are close to practical use. Full artificial muscles that match the strength, speed, and efficiency of living muscle are not yet there. Controlling soft bodies reliably is still the central open problem: the math is hard, the sensors are limited, and simulation is slow. The gap between a proof-of-concept lab robot and one that works outside the lab, without careful tuning, remains large.

Medicine and surgery

Across everything · 340 papers in this theme

340 papers · a rule across every part of the loop: sense, decide, and move all apply, but each must be rethought for the body

The problem — in depth

Every robot faces the same challenge: take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do, move a real body, and do all of that fast, cheap, safe, and well enough to work in new situations. Medical robots face the harshest version of this challenge, because the "world" they work in is the inside of a living person.

The body is not a fixed space. Tissue stretches, shifts, and breathes. An organ that was in one place a moment ago is somewhere slightly different now. A needle meant to reach a precise layer in the retina of the eye will miss if the tissue moves even a fraction of a millimeter during insertion. That is not a small error — it can blind someone. In most robot settings, the world is relatively stable. In surgery, it never is.

Getting information about the world inside the body is also far harder than it sounds. The main tool is a tiny camera mounted on a thin tube that a surgeon feeds through a small cut (an endoscope). The view is cramped, the lighting is uneven, and the tissue glistens with fluid that causes false reflections. Building a 3D picture of what the camera sees — figuring out how far things are, what is moving, where the tool is — is genuinely difficult. Other ways of seeing inside the body, like X-ray or ultrasound, each have their own gaps and distortions. Before the robot can do anything useful, it has to assemble a good-enough picture of a scene that keeps changing and is hard to look at clearly.

Movement inside the body must be gentle above all else. A robot that presses too hard on the spine or the wall of a blood vessel can cause injury that is worse than doing nothing. Standard robot tools — rigid metal arms driven by powerful motors — are too blunt for this. The anatomy is curved in ways that a straight tool cannot follow: the pelvis curves, the arteries curve, the spinal canal curves. Getting a tool to the right place without damaging what is around it demands a different class of robot body altogether.

The margin for error is also much smaller than in almost any other setting. In a factory, a robot that grips slightly wrong drops a part. In surgery, the same mistake can cost a life. And unlike a factory, every patient is different. The same procedure done on ten people involves ten different anatomies. The robot cannot rely on the world being the same every time.

All of this means that the standard sense-decide-move loop still applies, but every single step of it must be rebuilt with these constraints in mind.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Tracking moving tissue in real time

The core sensing problem in many surgical settings is that the tissue the robot is trying to reach or avoid does not stay still. Researchers have built systems that watch the tissue constantly and update the robot's picture of where things are on the fly.

One approach represents the scene as a collection of small flat discs floating in 3D space — a method called Gaussian Splatting — and updates their positions as the tissue moves. "Advancing Dense Endoscopic Reconstruction with Gaussian Splatting-Driven Surface Normal-Aware Tracking and Mapping" uses this to build a consistent 3D map from endoscope video by forcing each disc to agree with its neighbors on which direction it faces. "Deformable Gaussian Splatting for Efficient and High-Fidelity Reconstruction of Surgical Scenes" extends this by tracking whether each point in the scene has appeared, moved, or disappeared — removing old points and adding new ones as the tissue changes.

For procedures requiring extreme precision, "Real-Time Deformation-Aware Control for Autonomous Robotic Subretinal Injection Under iOCT Guidance" builds a live 3D picture of the retinal layers from scan slices at real-time speed, tracks a target point that moves with the tissue, and adjusts the needle depth automatically to stay on target even as the eye shifts.

Robots built to bend and grow into the body

When the path to a target curves — through a blood vessel, around the pelvis, down the spine — a rigid straight tool cannot follow it. A large group of papers builds robots whose bodies are designed to curve, grow, or change stiffness mid-procedure.

"Towards Design and Development of a Concentric Tube Steerable Drilling Robot for Creating S-shape Tunnels for Pelvic Fixation Procedures" builds a drill that bends in two places to match the curve of the pelvis, letting surgeons place screws along stronger bone paths. "Vine4Spine: A Steerable Tip-Growing Robot with Contact Force Estimation for Navigation in the Spinal Subarachnoid Space" builds a robot that extends from its tip — like a vine growing — and has force sensors so it knows when it is pushing too hard against the spine. "Variable-Stiffness Nasotracheal Intubation Robot with Passive Buffering" lets doctors change how stiff the tool is during a procedure, with built-in shock-absorbing sections so that any unexpected contact is cushioned. Each of these designs accepts that the path cannot be rigid and builds flexibility directly into the tool.

Simulation and synthetic data to train safely

Training a robot to do anything dangerous through trial and error in the real world is not acceptable in medicine. Researchers solve this by building detailed simulations where robots can practice or where data can be generated without putting anyone at risk.

"Sim4EndoR: A Reinforcement Learning Centered Simulation Platform for Task Automation of Endovascular Robotics" builds a physics-based model of blood vessels and uses it to train an agent — a program that learns by trying things and getting rewards — to guide a wire through arteries. The agent learns clinically correct behavior without ever touching a real patient. "CRESSim-MPM: A Material Point Method Library for Surgical Soft Body Simulation with Cutting and Suturing" simulates the actual act of cutting through soft tissue and stitching it back together, tracking each tiny piece of material through the deformation. For perception, "Polyp-Gen: Realistic and Diverse Polyp Image Generation for Endoscopic Dataset Expansion" generates realistic fake endoscopy images with accurate boundaries around growths (polyps), giving machine-learning systems more data to train on without needing more patients.

Connecting perception to robot action

A robot that can see is only useful if it can turn what it sees into a physical action at the right moment. Several papers focus specifically on closing this loop between looking and doing.

"RoboNurse-VLA: Robotic Scrub Nurse System based on Vision-Language-Action Model" connects a language model that understands what a surgeon is asking for with a vision model that finds the right tool in the camera image, then drives the robot arm to pick it up and hand it over. "MEDiC: Autonomous Surgical Robotic Assistance to Maximizing Exposure for Dissection and Cautery" figures out where to grip and how to pull soft tissue to give the surgeon the clearest possible view, using a simulation it can compute gradients through — meaning it can mathematically find the best pull rather than guessing. "Ultrasound-Guided Robotic Blood Drawing and In Vivo Studies on Submillimetre Vessels of Rats" uses ultrasound images in real time to find a vein less than one millimeter wide and guide a needle into it precisely enough to draw blood reliably.

Reading the body from outside the skin

Not every medical robot operates by cutting. Some control devices already inside the body from the outside, or help people whose bodies do not work as they should. "Intraoperative 3D Shape Estimation of Magnetic Soft Guidewire" figures out the full 3D curve of a flexible wire inside a blood vessel by reading the magnetic field the wire's tip produces — no extra imaging hardware needed, just physics worked backward from field measurements. "Force Myography Based Torque Estimation in Human Knee and Ankle Joints" measures how a muscle bulges through the skin using pressure sensors, then uses that to estimate how hard the joint is working — information needed to control an exoskeleton. "Continuous Wrist Control on the Hannes Prosthesis" watches the target object with a camera and automatically rotates a prosthetic wrist to the right angle for the grab, so the user does not have to compensate with their shoulder.

Where it stands

The field has made real progress on the individual pieces: robots that bend into curved anatomy, systems that track moving tissue well enough to guide a needle, simulators that can train agents safely, and tools that connect seeing to acting. What remains hard is putting all of these pieces together reliably in a real operating room, with all the noise, time pressure, and variation across patients that brings. Each paper solves one specific problem well; the gap is a full system that handles many problems at once, adapts to the patient in front of it, and does so with the safety and consistency medicine demands.

Flying, underwater, and outdoor field robots

Across everything · 293 papers in this theme

293 papers · a rule across everything: the same core sense-decide-move loop, but each setting breaks a different part of it

The problem — in depth

Every robot must do three things: take in what is around it, decide what to do, and move its body to do it. In a lab, that is hard enough. Put the robot in the air, underwater, or out in a field, and each of those three steps gets harder in its own way — often all at once.

A drone flying through a forest faces wind that shoves it off course and gaps between trees that are barely wider than itself. Its camera picks up small objects at odd angles and distances that a network trained on regular photos has never seen. Its battery lasts minutes, not hours. GPS stops working inside buildings or under thick canopy. And if the drone hits anything, it falls.

Underwater robots face murky water that eats light and sonar signal both. The water itself pushes back. Depth makes electronics fragile. There are almost no large training sets of underwater images, so learning-based methods that depend on massive data fall apart before they start.

Outdoor ground robots deal with rough, unpredictable terrain. They carry science gear, or pull cables, or inspect pipelines, and must do all of that with limited power in places where no one can quickly come to fix them.

All of these settings connect to the same core problem every robot faces: how do you take in a world that is unclear and changing, figure out what to do, move a real body, and do it fast, cheap, safe, and reliably enough to be useful? In the lab you can control some of these — good lighting, flat floors, reliable networks. In the field, none of that holds. The field is the hardest test of whether robots actually work.

The 293 papers in this topic do not propose a single new idea. They adapt what already works — learned control, safety filters, path planning, object detection — to the specific ways each environment breaks it. That is what makes field robotics a distinct research area: not a new theory, but the disciplined work of making existing methods survive contact with the real world.

The approaches — how the field actually tackles it

Learning to fly without hand-coded rules

The old way to control a drone is to write out the physics by hand: how fast the rotors spin, how that creates lift, how wind shifts the body, and so on. This works when everything matches the model. When reality diverges — a gust of wind, a heavier-than-expected payload, a rotor that is slightly off — the hand-coded model falls apart.

Several papers replace part or all of that hand-coded model with a learned one. In "DroneDiffusion," a model learns the drone's motion as a range of possible futures rather than a single fixed prediction, which means it stays useful even when the drone is loaded with different payloads or flying in variable wind. In "Learning Quadrotor Control from Visual Features Using Differentiable Simulation," a simulator is built so that the effect of each decision on the final flight outcome can be computed exactly and used to update the learned policy — a technique called differentiable simulation — cutting training from hours to minutes. "GRaD-Nav" uses the same differentiable-physics trick combined with photorealistic image rendering to teach a drone to fly through gates using only a camera, without falling apart on real hardware.

The common thread: when the real world does not match your model, let the model learn from data instead of being written by hand.

Designing controllers that resist outside forces

Even a well-trained drone goes wrong when something pushes on it unexpectedly. The papers here do not try to learn a better model of the disturbance — they measure it in real time and subtract it out.

"ProxFly" deals with a specific problem: when multiple drones fly very close together, the downward air from one rotor disturbs the others. A small add-on module trained through trial and error sits on top of the main controller and sends tiny corrective commands based only on what the drone's own body is doing — no wireless signals between drones needed. "NDOB-Based Control of a UAV with Delta-Arm" handles the shaking that happens when a robot arm is mounted on a drone: a disturbance observer measures the unwanted coupling forces between the arm and the drone body and cancels them in real time. "DR-MPC" does the same for pipeline inspection: a planner looks a short time ahead, and a state estimator inside it continuously measures outside wind forces and feeds the correction back into the plan before the drone goes off course.

These approaches all share a structure: a main controller that works in calm conditions, plus a watchdog that estimates what is going wrong right now and adjusts.

Flying through clutter using only cameras

A drone that can only carry a small camera — no heavy laser scanner — must still navigate dense spaces safely and quickly. This is a hard problem because camera images are noisy, small objects are easy to miss, and the time between seeing an obstacle and hitting it is very short at high speed.

"Vision Transformers for End-to-End Vision-Based Quadrotor Obstacle Avoidance" trains a model that splits each camera frame into patches and learns relationships across the whole image at once (this class of model is called a vision transformer), then maps those images directly to flight commands. The drone learns in simulation and transfers to new environments without retraining. "Flying on Point Clouds with Reinforcement Learning" uses 3D laser scans instead, simplified enough to run at 50 commands per second, so the drone can fly through a forest with no path planner in the loop at all. "Whole-Body Control Through Narrow Gaps from Pixels to Action" adds another layer: the drone must tilt its whole body to squeeze through a gap barely wider than itself, and the learning algorithm is started from positions along known good paths so it can actually find solutions to this very hard problem.

"UAV-DETR" addresses a related but distinct failure mode: standard object-detection networks miss small objects in drone photos because they were designed for regular, ground-level images. Three targeted upgrades — combining features at many scales, keeping fine detail during downsampling, and connecting features across processing paths — fix this for drone imagery specifically.

Saving power and extending mission time

Drones drain their batteries in minutes. This limits how far they can go, how long they can monitor, and what tasks they can carry out. Several papers address this directly by giving drones ways to stop flying temporarily.

"Adaptive Perching and Grasping by Aerial Robot with Light-Weight and High Grip-Force Tendon-Driven Three-Fingered Hand Using Single Actuator" designs a three-fingered claw driven by one motor that can grab objects from any approach angle and hold on with no power once latched. "Tendon-driven Grasper Design for Aerial Robot Perching on Tree Branches" builds a claw that grips a tree branch and locks in place using tendons — no power needed to stay on. A camera finds a good branch and guides the drone to land. "RaccoonBot" solves the same problem for a wire-crawling robot that monitors forests: it grips the wire without power so it cannot fall, then moves along the wire to wherever the sun hits best, recharging as it goes.

All three are engineering solutions to a power-budget problem that no amount of better learning or planning can fully solve — the physics of flight just cost energy.

Building safer flight with real-time safety filters

A learned or planned trajectory can still steer the drone into a wall. Safety filters (the technical term is control barrier functions, or CBFs) add a layer that watches the planned motion and blocks any command that would bring the drone too close to an obstacle, regardless of what the main controller decided.

"A Control Barrier Function for Safe Navigation with Online Gaussian Splatting Maps" runs this filter at 15 times per second directly on a map made of hundreds of thousands of tiny 3D blobs (Gaussian splats) that are still being built in real time. "Safety-Critical Control for Aerial Physical Interaction in Uncertain Environment" applies the same idea to a flying robot arm: the safety filter tracks estimation errors in real time and adjusts the arm's motion just enough to keep all motor thrusts inside safe bounds.

Where it stands

Drone flight in controlled or semi-controlled conditions — racing, gate navigation, indoor obstacle avoidance — is now good enough to demonstrate reliably in research settings. Perching and battery extension hardware has been proven on real robots. What remains hard is generalizing across conditions: a controller trained for calm air fails in high wind; a grasper designed for tree branches may not handle pipes or wires; a detection network tuned for daytime drone imagery breaks at night. Underwater and outdoor ground field robots are still limited mainly by the lack of large training datasets and the difficulty of testing in the field at scale. The core challenge has not changed — surviving the gap between what was trained and what the real world delivers.

UAV-DETR: Efficient End-to-End Object Detection for Unmanned Aerial Vehicle ImageryIROS70 cites
ProblemA standard object detection method designed for regular photos doesn't work well on drone pictures because small objects get lost and detail gets blurred.
ApproachThey enhance the standard method with three upgrades: a module that combines features at many sizes using frequency tricks, a downsampling step that keeps details sharp, and a module that glues features together across different paths.
ContributionA DETR-based UAV object detection framework with frequency-domain feature enhancement that improves AP by 3.1% and AP50 by 4.2% over baseline on the VisDrone dataset.
Vision Transformers for End-to-End Vision-Based Quadrotor Obstacle AvoidanceICRA9 cites
ProblemFlying a drone fast through cluttered spaces using only a camera feed requires a controller that reacts quickly and still works in places it has never seen, which standard convolutional camera-to-control networks fail to do at high speeds.
ApproachThey train vision transformer models (ViT) — which split images into patches and learn relationships across the whole image at once — to map depth camera images directly to flight commands, testing speeds up to 7 m/s in simulation.
ContributionFirst use of vision transformers for end-to-end vision-based quadrotor control, showing ViT outperforms CNNs at high speeds and generalizes better to unseen environments, with recurrence further reducing energy cost.
Whole-Body Control Through Narrow Gaps from Pixels to ActionICRA9 cites
ProblemFlying a drone through a gap just barely wider than itself requires precise tilting of the whole body, and learning this from raw camera images is very hard because there are very few successful paths for the learning algorithm to discover.
ApproachThey first train a control policy using 3D point-cloud inputs (which are easier to learn from), then transfer that knowledge to a camera-image policy by having the camera policy mimic the point-cloud policy's behavior during flight; to help the learner find good paths, it is reset to positions along trajectories generated by a physics-based planner.
ContributionCombines model-based trajectory initialization with RL-to-pixel distillation to learn whole-body narrow-gap traversal entirely from pixels without hand-crafted reward shaping.
NDOB-Based Control of a UAV with Delta-Arm Considering Manipulator DynamicsICRA8 cites
ProblemWhen a robot arm is mounted on a flying drone, the arm's motion shakes the drone and the drone's motion disturbs the arm, making precise arm positioning very difficult.
ApproachA disturbance observer (NDOB) measures and cancels the unwanted coupling forces between the arm and drone in real time, while a high-pass filter separately boosts fast arm commands so the arm can move quickly without fighting the drone controller.
ContributionAchieves millimeter-level end-effector accuracy on a fully autonomous delta-arm aerial manipulator by jointly compensating for dynamic coupling and selectively amplifying fast manipulator motions.
Learning Quadrotor Control from Visual Features Using Differentiable SimulationICRA7 cites
ProblemTraining a drone to fly using only camera images with standard trial-and-error (reinforcement learning) requires enormous amounts of simulated flight time and takes hours or days.
ApproachA differentiable simulator lets the learning algorithm compute exact gradients of the flight outcome back through the physics model to the policy. The drone learns to fly from raw visual features in minutes instead of hours by following these gradients directly.
ContributionDemonstrates that differentiable simulation with a surrogate model trains vision-based quadrotor control orders of magnitude faster than model-free RL, bridging from state-based (seconds) to vision-based (minutes) learning.
Robust Self-Reconfiguration for Fault-Tolerant Control of Modular Aerial Robot SystemsICRA7 cites
ProblemWhen a drone made of detachable modules loses a rotor or a module, it needs to rearrange itself, but the steps taken during that rearrangement can put the craft in a shape it cannot actually fly in.
ApproachA planning algorithm works out a step-by-step order to detach and reattach modules, checking after each step whether the craft in its current shape can still be controlled and flown stably before moving on.
ContributionA reconfiguration planning algorithm that maximizes controllability margin at every intermediate stage, enabling fault-tolerant self-reconfiguration with improved trajectory tracking and fewer assembly steps.

The whole picture, in one paragraph

The whole picture in one paragraph

Put both meetings together and they are one effort: build robots that can take in an unclear, changing world, decide what to do about it, and move a real body to do it — fast enough for a small onboard computer, learned from as few examples as possible, safe around people, and general enough to carry to new robots and new jobs. The seeing themes make the picture clearer and cheaper; the deciding themes turn plain-language goals into checked, doable plans, alone or in teams; the moving themes make bodies act well by feel and in real time; and the cross-cutting themes fight the real limits — the cost of training data, the need for safety, and the demands of hard settings. The two meetings agree on all of this. ICRA tilts a little more toward the newest AI-brain ideas and toward naming the dangers (it holds the biggest headline citation counts on self-driving and touch, and the sharpest safety warning). IROS tilts more toward the body and the world — flying, medicine, soft machines, energy, and making the computer generate its own data — and it puts the "make the plan safe, and make it run on cheap hardware" worry even more front and center. Two big things are still not solved at either meeting. First, many of the best methods are accurate but too slow or too costly to run live on the robot's own computer — "right but too late" is the most common problem the papers name. Second, the work on being able to *promise* a robot won't fail is falling behind how fast these learned and language-driven systems are being put to work.
