Hauling ore

An autonomous haul truck loads to target, matches its speed to the grade, saves its brakes with the retarder, drives fuel-optimally, and slows for the bends. Five stages of heavy-haulage physics — each a plain rule, run and measured, with the failure it prevents.

Each stage is one link in the haul cycle: load the right tonnes, hold a speed the brakes can survive, keep the brakes cool, spend the least fuel, and stay upright through the bends.

1 load → 2 grade → 3 brakes → 4 fuel → 5 bend
1

Load to the target

Hardware

The shovel loads ore into the tray until the truck is at its target payload. If the loader just counts buckets — five passes and go — the truck leaves randomly heavy or light, because every bucket is a different weight. Come in over the overload limit and the payload is illegal and pounds the frame apart; come in light and you wasted a whole cycle hauling air.

Load to the measured payload from the suspension-strut pressures, not a fixed bucket count; buckets vary by tonnes, so counting them lands the truck randomly over or under target and overloads it often enough to crack the frame.
payload error — count buckets14.2 t
payload error — strut-measured1.2 t
overload freq — count vs strut1% → 0%

Loading to a 200 t target, counting a fixed 5 buckets misses by 14.2 t on average and blows past the 240 t overload limit 1% of the time. Measuring the strut pressures cuts the error to 1.2 t and overloads 0% of the time.

2

Speed for the grade

Control & MPC

Loaded, the truck heads down a steep ramp out of the pit. Gravity pulls the whole weight down the slope and the brakes have to hold it. Pick a fixed haul-road speed and ignore the grade, and on a steep enough descent the braking the truck needs to hold that speed is more than the brakes can deliver — the descent becomes an unstoppable runaway.

Set the descent speed from the brake/traction limit, not a fixed haul-road speed; on a steep grade gravity's pull must be held within brake capacity, and a speed that demands more braking than available is an unstoppable runaway.
brake demand — naive speed158% (runaway)
brake demand — safe speed100%
max safe descent speed8.8 m/s

On the 10° descent with a 575 t load, a naive 14 m/s demands 158% of brake capacity — a runaway. The max safe speed is 8.8 m/s, which sits right at 100% of capacity.

3

Save the brakes

Safety & guarantees

That long descent has to be held for minutes, not seconds. Every bit of gravity's energy the friction brakes soak up turns into heat. Hold the whole descent on the friction brakes alone and they climb past their temperature limit and fade — losing force exactly where the ramp is steepest and there is nothing left to stop with.

Dump the descent energy into the engine/electric retarder and keep the friction brakes as a reserve; friction-only brakes heat past their fade limit on a long grade and lose the force you need at the bottom.
peak brake temp — friction only894°C (fade)
peak brake temp — retarder168°C
brake capacity left — friction vs retarder0% → 100%

Holding 10 m/s down the 8° grade, friction-only brakes peak at 894°C — well past the 350°C fade limit, leaving 0% of their capacity. With the retarder taking the energy, the brakes peak at 168°C and keep 100%.

4

Drive the fuel-optimal line

Control & MPC

The same load has to move the same distance every cycle — the only choice is how. Run flat-out and the truck burns fuel fighting drag that grows with speed squared, then sits idling in the shovel queue it raced to reach. The clock barely moves but the fuel gauge does.

Drive a steady fuel-optimal speed and coast the downgrades instead of flat-out-then-idle; drag grows with speed squared and racing to idle in the shovel queue burns fuel for no real cycle-time gain.
fuel/cycle — aggressive561 L
fuel/cycle — eco339 L
fuel saved / extra time40% / +198 s

Over a 5 km haul, driving flat-out at 14 m/s burns 561 L per cycle. The eco profile at 9 m/s with coasting burns 339 L — 40% less — for only 198 s more.

5

Slow for the bend

Safety & guarantees

The haul road bends around the pit, and a loaded truck rides tall and top-heavy. Carry haul-road speed into a tight curve and the sideways pull to hold the turn passes the point where the truck tips — one bend, on its side, load spilled across the road.

Slow for the bend below the rollover speed v_max = sqrt(a_roll*R); a loaded high-CoM truck tips once lateral accel v^2/R exceeds g*(track/2)/h_com, so carrying haul-road speed into a tight curve rolls it.
lateral accel — naive speed111% (rollover)
lateral accel — limited100%
max safe corner speed13.3 m/s

On the 30 m bend (roll threshold 5.9 m/s²), a naive 14 m/s pulls 111% of the tipping accel — a rollover. The limiter caps the corner at 13.3 m/s, right at 100%.
