1

Steady the hand — tremor filtering

Our demo: a low-pass filter cuts a 10 Hz tremor 13× while passing the deliberate motion (0.45 mm → 0.03 mm).

Who actually hits it

Intuitive's da Vinci system (millions of prostatectomies, hysterectomies and more) filters the surgeon's console input; microsurgery — retinal, neuro — is even more tremor-limited, and active-cancellation handheld tools (e.g. the Micron instrument) target it directly.

What it costs when it fails

Physiological tremor runs ~8–12 Hz at tens to over a hundred microns. At the scale of suturing a millimetre-wide vessel or operating on the retina, that shake tears tissue and misplaces sutures — freehand, it is a hard limit on what is operable.

How it's solved in production

Low-pass filtering of the master input on teleoperated systems; real-time active tremor cancellation in handheld micro-instruments; scaling (below) compounds the benefit.

Toy demo → real system

Real tremor is non-stationary, patient- and fatigue-specific, and overlaps intended motion in frequency; real systems must trade suppression against latency the surgeon can feel. Ours is a fixed 10 Hz sinusoid.

2

Scale the motion — precision by ratio

Our demo: 5:1 motion scaling shrinks hand jitter from 0.80 mm to 0.16 mm at the tool.

Who actually hits it

Every teleoperated surgical robot offers motion scaling — da Vinci runs roughly 3:1 to 5:1, combined with tremor filtering, as a core selling point.

What it costs when it fails

Human hand positioning is good to about a millimetre; the task is sub-millimetre. Map hand to tool 1:1 and the surgeon simply cannot place the tip finely enough, however careful.

How it's solved in production

Adjustable master–slave motion scaling on the console, chosen per task (finer for delicate suturing, coarser for gross moves).

Toy demo → real system

Scaling interacts with haptics and latency, and higher ratios shrink the usable workspace and slow the surgeon down. Ours is pure jitter divided by a constant.

3

Pivot at the incision — remote centre of motion

Our demo: a remote-centre-of-motion constraint holds the incision at 0 mm where a naive move drags it 8 mm.

Who actually hits it

The remote centre of motion is the defining constraint of minimally-invasive surgical robots. da Vinci enforces it mechanically with a double-parallelogram linkage; research and other systems (e.g. RAVEN) enforce it in software.

What it costs when it fails

If the shaft translated at the port it would tear the incision and injure the abdominal wall — this is a hard safety requirement, not an optimisation.

How it's solved in production

A mechanical RCM linkage (da Vinci) or a software RCM constraint in the controller that permits only rotation and insertion about the fixed port.

Toy demo → real system

The real problem includes patient motion, changing insufflation pressure, and tool exchanges shifting the geometry. Ours is a fixed pivot.

4

Guard the no-go zone — virtual fixtures

Our demo: a virtual fixture holds tool penetration into a 4 mm keep-out at 0 mm instead of 3.97 mm.

Who actually hits it

Stryker's MAKO robotic knee and hip system uses haptic active constraints — the surgeon physically cannot cut outside the planned bone volume; virtual fixtures also appear in neuro and ENT robotics.

What it costs when it fails

Straying into a critical structure — a nerve, a major vessel — ends the operation badly. Active constraints exist to make that geometrically impossible.

How it's solved in production

Haptic virtual fixtures / active constraints (forbidden-region plus guidance), a concept named by Rosenberg in 1993 and now shipping in mainstream orthopaedic robots.

Toy demo → real system

The hard part in reality is registering the plan to a moving, deforming patient accurately — the boundary is only as good as that registration. Ours is a fixed sphere in a fixed frame.

5

Beat the cable slack — backlash compensation

Our demo: feeding the backlash forward cuts tip lag from 0.16 mm to 0 on reversals.

Who actually hits it

da Vinci's EndoWrist and most flexible, cable-driven instruments carry tendon backlash; continuum robots such as the Auris/Monarch bronchoscope fight hysteresis constantly.

What it costs when it fails

Backlash gives a dead-band on every direction reversal — the tip lags and the tool feels 'loose' exactly during the fine back-and-forth of suturing.

How it's solved in production

Backlash/hysteresis modelling with feedforward, distal shape sensing (fibre-Bragg-grating sensors), or stiffer transmissions.

Toy demo → real system

Real hysteresis is load- and configuration-dependent and drifts with wear over an instrument's limited use life. Ours is a fixed deadband.
