Robotic bin picking — the real problem

A warehouse arm has to see a jumbled object, choose a grasp that won't slip, reach it without the arm tearing itself apart, touch down without crushing anything, and confirm it actually holds — five hard problems back to back.

1

See the object — pose from a point cloud

Our demo: after segmenting the object off the table, RANSAC recovered the yaw to within 2 degrees and the center to 1 mm, versus 45 degrees and 108 mm if we fit the raw cloud that the 500-point table dominates.

Who actually hits it

Bin-picking vendors like Covariant and RightHand Robotics, and academic 6DoF pipelines (PS6D, point-pair-feature methods), all start by turning an RGB-D or point-cloud scan into an object pose so the arm knows where and how the item sits in the tote.

What it costs when it fails

If the pose is wrong, everything downstream fails: the grasp targets empty space, the arm collides with a bin wall, or it grips the wrong item. Textureless, reflective, dark, or transparent surfaces wreck depth sensing, and in a cluttered bin one object's geometry can dominate the fit — exactly the table-dominates-object failure our demo reproduces.

How it's solved in production

Production systems segment the scene first — RANSAC plane fitting removes the bin floor and walls, then instance segmentation isolates each item — before estimating pose per object. Reported bin-picking pipelines reach roughly millimeter-level accuracy and around 90% success on real totes once the object is properly separated from its background.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo is one clean object on a flat table with a known shape. Real bins have heavy occlusion, stacked and overlapping items, novel SKUs the model never saw, and shiny or clear packaging where the depth camera returns garbage — none of which a single RANSAC fit handles.

2

Pick a grasp — score every candidate

Our demo: across 400 candidate grasps only 31.5% achieved force closure, and scoring each by its friction-cone margin found a grasp with a +26.2 degree margin (near the 26.6 degree cone) instead of the median candidate at -15 degrees.

Who actually hits it

Berkeley's Dex-Net line (GQ-CNN grasp-quality networks) is the canonical academic answer; industrially, RightHand's RightPick uses three compliant fingers plus suction and Covariant uses suction grippers, each of which has to pick from many possible grasp points on each item.

What it costs when it fails

A grasp that merely closes on the object isn't enough — if it sits outside the friction cone the item slips or drops mid-transfer, costing a failed pick and a lost cycle. The Amazon Picking/Robotics Challenge exposed how hard this is: even the strongest teams took tens of seconds per item and could not pick every object, and difficulty rose sharply as bins got more crowded.

How it's solved in production

Modern grasp planners score a large set of candidate grasps and keep the most robust. Dex-Net 2.0 reported about 93% success planning parallel-jaw grasps in under a second on known objects, and Dex-Net 3.0 reported roughly 98% / 82% / 58% suction success on basic / typical / adversarial objects — the margin-scoring idea our demo uses in miniature.

Toy demo → real system

We score a fixed set of analytic candidates on a rigid object with a known friction cone. Real items are deformable (bags, clothing), have unknown or varying friction, and the best grasp can be occluded by neighbors — and suction versus finger choice depends on the specific item in ways our single metric ignores.

3

Reach it — inverse kinematics near a singularity

Our demo: with the target 3.05 m out on a 3.0 m reach — right at the edge — a plain Jacobian inverse commanded a 21.6 rad/s joint step, while damped least squares reached essentially the same final error with a maximum joint step of 0.19 rad/s.

Who actually hits it

Any arm-motion stack hits this — MoveIt (the standard ROS motion-planning framework) and industrial arm controllers must solve inverse kinematics to convert a target pose into joint angles, and every warehouse picker faces targets near the edge of the workspace.

What it costs when it fails

Near a kinematic singularity the Jacobian becomes ill-conditioned, so a naive inverse commands enormous joint velocities — the arm jerks, faults on a speed limit, or moves unpredictably. In a warehouse that means an aborted pick, an emergency stop, or wear on the joints.

How it's solved in production

Damped least squares (the singularity-robust inverse) is the standard fix: it trades a little tracking error for a bounded, stable solution near singularities. MoveIt Servo, for example, slows the end effector toward zero as it approaches a singularity, and variants like selectively-damped least squares tune the damping per singular direction.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo is a single planar target with no obstacles and no joint limits. A real picker must plan a full collision-free trajectory inside a cluttered bin, respect joint and velocity limits, and often has redundant degrees of freedom to resolve — problems well beyond one damped inverse step.

4

Touch down softly — impedance instead of position

Our demo: commanding a stiff position 20 mm past a wall at 0.1 m produced a 108.4 N peak contact force, while commanding a compliant force held the peak to 4.0 N for the same overshoot.

Who actually hits it

Contact-rich manipulation — placing an item in a tote, peg-in-hole assembly, dual-arm handling — relies on impedance or admittance control; collaborative and industrial arms expose Cartesian impedance modes precisely so contact doesn't turn a small position error into a large force.

What it costs when it fails

Under stiff position control a tiny position error at contact becomes a huge force: the gripper crushes fragile items, the arm faults on a force limit, or it damages the bin or the product. Assembly and placement both fail when the controller fights the environment instead of yielding to it.

How it's solved in production

Impedance control commands a target stiffness and damping rather than an exact position, so the arm behaves like a spring on contact and force stays bounded. Research on peg-in-hole assembly shows lowering translational stiffness along the insertion axis lets the part comply and seat with modest, controlled contact forces — the same principle our 4 N result shows.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo is a 1-D push against a rigid wall with hand-tuned stiffness. Real placement mixes stiff and soft contacts, unknown surface geometry, and items that themselves deform, so a single fixed impedance isn't enough — production increasingly uses variable-impedance and learned compliance.

5

Confirm the grab — watch the grip force

Our demo: monitoring grip force against the 5.0 N expected load with a 3.5 N threshold caught every slip (100% recall, 0% false alarms) an average of 6 steps before the object would have been lost.

Who actually hits it

Tactile-sensing research uses sensors like SynTouch's BioTac and MIT's GelSight/GelSlim to catch slip; warehouse pickers monitor grip or suction state after closing so a silently slipping item can be re-gripped rather than dropped.

What it costs when it fails

A slip you don't notice is a dropped item — mislabeled as picked, jammed in the mechanism, or damaged on the floor — and a lost cycle. Because tactile signals are subtle, incipient slip (part of the contact slipping while the rest holds) is easy to miss until the object is already gone.

How it's solved in production

Detecting slip sets a safe lower bound on grip force: once slip is sensed, the controller tightens before the object falls. Vibration-based sensors react fast — reported BioTac slip-detection delays are on the order of tens of milliseconds — and monitoring the ratio of shear to normal force or the onset vibration is the standard signal, the same after-close watch our demo performs.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo watches a clean 1-D force signal with a fixed threshold. Real tactile data is noisy and multi-axis, slip signatures vary by material and surface, and suction failure looks different from finger slip — so production systems need learned, sensor-specific detectors, not one threshold.
