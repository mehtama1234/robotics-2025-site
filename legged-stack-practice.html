Legged robots — the real problem

A walking robot has to feel the ground, choose where each foot lands, stay balanced on a shifting set of contacts, swing its legs clear of the terrain, and push against the world without slipping — every step, in real time.

1

Feel the ground — contact detection

Our demo: a bare force threshold reports 58 touchdown transitions on noisy sensor data where only 8 are real; hysteresis brings it down to 10.

Who actually hits it

Every dynamic quadruped — Boston Dynamics Spot, ANYbotics ANYmal, the MIT Cheetah, Unitree's robots — must know exactly when each foot touches down, since their controllers switch behavior between swing and stance on that signal.

What it costs when it fails

A mistimed or false contact makes the controller commit a foot too early or too late, so the leg either slams down, loses the stance it thought it had, or chatters between states — any of which can trip the gait or drop the body.

How it's solved in production

Production systems estimate contact from foot-force sensing and leg kinematics/dynamics (often with a momentum-based disturbance observer instead of a dedicated force sensor), and debounce the decision with hysteresis so sensor noise can't fake a touchdown.

Toy demo → real system

Our sim uses a clean 1-D force signal with hand-set thresholds; real robots fuse noisy multi-axis force, joint torques, and IMU data and must reject impacts, soft or deformable ground, and sensor drift.

2

Place the next foot — foothold selection

Our demo: with the Raibert speed-correcting offset the robot converges from 0.5 to the 1.2 m/s target in about 7 strides; drop the (v − v_des) term and it walks forever at its starting 0.5 m/s.

Who actually hits it

Foot-placement control traces to Marc Raibert's hopping and running robots at the MIT Leg Lab in the 1980s — the lab that became Boston Dynamics — and the Raibert heuristic is still integrated into quadruped controllers from research labs and companies like ANYbotics and Unitree.

What it costs when it fails

Choose the foothold badly and the robot fails to reach or hold its target speed, drifts off course, or plants a foot on a slippery, sloped, or unsupported patch of ground and slips or falls.

How it's solved in production

The Raibert heuristic places the foot at a 'neutral point' plus an offset proportional to the velocity error, so a foot ahead of neutral slows the body and behind neutral speeds it up; modern stacks combine this with terrain-aware footstep planning and MPC.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo places feet on flat, uniformly supportive ground with a simple speed rule; real robots must perceive terrain, avoid gaps, edges, and low-traction spots, and plan several footsteps ahead over rough or cluttered surfaces.

3

Stay upright — balance over the support polygon

Our demo: with the CoM projected to (0.15, −0.1), lifting the FR foot leaves a stability margin of −0.166 m (the robot tips), while lifting RL keeps a +0.1 m margin and stays balanced.

Who actually hits it

Any legged robot that lifts a foot must reason about which contacts remain — a concern for ANYmal, Spot, Unitree quadrupeds, and every biped, all of which track the center of mass relative to the polygon formed by their standing feet.

What it costs when it fails

Lift the wrong foot, or let the body's weight fall outside the remaining support, and the robot rolls over the edge of its support and tips — a fall that can damage the machine or its surroundings.

How it's solved in production

Classic stability uses the support polygon — the convex hull of the ground contacts — and keeps the center of mass (statically) or the Zero-Moment-Point / capture point (dynamically) inside it, so the robot only lifts a foot that keeps the body over the remaining support triangle.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo uses a static center-of-mass-in-triangle test on flat ground; real dynamic gaits use ZMP or capture-point criteria that account for momentum and acceleration, on inclined, uneven, or moving ground.

4

Swing the leg over — swing-foot trajectory

Our demo: a straight-line swing hits a minimum clearance of −0.05 m over a 0.05 m bump (it stubs), while arcing the foot to a 0.1 m apex keeps +0.043 m of clearance.

Who actually hits it

Every walking robot must lift and reposition each swinging leg without catching it on the terrain — Spot, ANYmal, MIT Cheetah, and Unitree all generate swing-foot trajectories with a raised apex between liftoff and touchdown.

What it costs when it fails

A swing path with too little clearance scuffs or stubs the foot on bumps, curbs, or debris, which stalls the leg, jolts the body, and can trip the whole robot mid-step.

How it's solved in production

Controllers command a smooth swing trajectory that arcs up to a clearance apex and back down to the planned foothold — instead of a straight line — giving the foot room to pass over expected terrain height.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo arcs over a single known bump of fixed height; real robots must set clearance from perceived, uncertain terrain and adapt the swing on the fly when a foot catches something the map missed.

5

Push with the stance legs — force distribution

Our demo: against 200 N of weight plus 60 N horizontal, an even force split drives the low-traction front foot (μ=0.25) to 1.2× its friction limit and it slips; the optimized split holds every foot to 0.35× and none slip.

Who actually hits it

Dynamic quadrupeds distribute how hard each stance foot pushes — the MIT Cheetah's convex MPC, ANYmal's hierarchical whole-body controller, and similar stacks on Spot and Unitree all solve for per-foot ground-reaction forces.

What it costs when it fails

Split the required force evenly and a foot on low-traction ground is asked for more than friction can supply, so it slips — the push fails, the body lurches, and the gait can break down.

How it's solved in production

Whole-body controllers pose force distribution as a quadratic program (QP) that finds ground-reaction forces producing the desired body motion while keeping each foot inside its friction cone (linearized as a pyramid) and within force limits.

Toy demo → real system

Our demo solves one static two-foot split with known friction coefficients; a real whole-body QP/MPC runs at hundreds of Hz over all legs with estimated, changing friction, full-body dynamics, and torque limits.
