Robotics 2025 · the field, visually

The loop, up close

Every robot runs the same circle — sense the world, decide what to do, move to do it — cheaply and safely enough to trust. Here is each theme inside that loop: a diagram of the idea, then the big-picture problem and how the field tackles it.

SENSE — turn sensor streams into a picture of the world

Cheap, fast, reliable seeing · 644 papers

▸ reliable perception on a tiny budget

The problem. The robot has to understand what it is looking at accurately and fast enough to run on its own small computer, even in hard cases like see-through glass, bad weather, long distances, and moving scenes.

The approach. Slimmed-down versions of vision programs that use less computing, and mixing several sensors together, for example a camera plus radar, so that when one sensor fails the other covers for it. Some read distance from two side-by-side cameras more cheaply; some handle see-through objects; some use sensors that only report what changed.

How, concretely. Trimming the computing keeps it fast enough for the onboard computer, and combining sensors fills the gaps where any single sensor is weak.

Self-driving cars, done leaner and safer · 247 papers

▸ perceive the road → a safe path

The problem. Full self-driving systems that build a heavy top-down picture of everything around the car cost too much computing and can still make unsafe plans.

The approach. Instead of describing the whole scene in dense detail, describe only the parts that matter; tie seeing, predicting, and planning more tightly together; mix radar with cameras for bad conditions; and have the car keep track of how unsure it is about its own map.

How, concretely. Focusing on what matters cuts the computing so it runs live, and tracking its own uncertainty lets the car be careful exactly where it is unsure.

One shared way to store the 3D world · 53 papers

▸ many views → one shared 3D map

The problem. Many jobs, such as mapping a room, rebuilding a scene, or making a computer copy of a place, each needed their own way to store what the world looks like in 3D, which is wasteful and hard to connect together.

The approach. A newer method builds a 3D scene out of many tiny colored blobs of light that are fast to draw and look realistic (Gaussian Splatting). Papers use it as one common format for mapping, for building a scene while tracking where the robot is, and for making practice copies of real places.

How, concretely. One shared format lets the seeing side and the deciding side speak the same language, so a scene captured once can be reused for mapping, planning, or training.

DECIDE — turn a goal into a plan

Language models that plan the job · 251 papers

▸ goal → a step-by-step plan

The problem. People give robots open-ended goals in plain language, but older planners could not handle that flexibility, and writing out every step by hand is fragile and breaks easily.

The approach. Use the same kind of AI that powers chatbots (large language models) to break a spoken goal into an ordered list of robot steps. The plans are not trusted directly; the AI must write them in a strict, checkable form and each step is then tested to make sure it is physically doable.

How, concretely. The AI supplies the flexible understanding of the goal while the strict checking catches its mistakes before the robot acts on them.

Turning seeing-and-talking AI into doing AI · 67 papers

▸ describe the scene → act on it

The problem. The big AI models that can look at a scene and describe it in words cannot actually move a robot, and they are weak at judging distance and 3D layout.

The approach. Retrain these models so their output is robot movements, not just words (vision-language-action models), sharpen their sense of depth and space, and let them keep improving by trial and error while running on the robot.

How, concretely. It keeps the model's broad grasp of scenes and words but adds the missing ability to act and to reason about real 3D space.

MOVE — turn a plan into physical action

Teaching legs and humanoids to move · 150 papers

▸ sim trials → a balanced gait

The problem. Legged robots and human-shaped robots need to walk, balance, and use their hands reliably without an engineer hand-designing a target for every single task, and a skill learned inside a computer often fails on the real machine.

The approach. Train the robot inside a computer copy of the world and then get it to still work on the real machine (sim-to-real); build one flexible controller that handles many tasks instead of a separate one for each; and give the robot an inner guess of what it will feel next so it reacts faster.

How, concretely. Practicing in a computer is fast and free, one shared controller cuts the per-task hand-tuning, and the inner guess keeps the robot steadier on rough ground.

Planning motion in real time · 103 papers

▸ search a safe path, fast

The problem. Working out the best possible way to move a complex body, moment by moment, is too slow to do properly in real time, and the fast shortcuts give jittery, low-quality results.

The approach. Instead of solving the hard math exactly, try many possible short-future motions, score each one, and blend the good ones (sampling-based control), and learn a rough model of how the body responds so those guesses are better.

How, concretely. Trying and scoring many options is fast enough to run live, and the learned model makes the guesses good enough to act on.

Handling things by feel, not just by looking · 100 papers

▸ touch feedback steers the grip

The problem. Robot controllers that only track position fail whenever force matters, such as pushing a peg into a hole, peeling a vegetable, wiping, or fitting parts together, because those jobs cannot be done by eye alone.

The approach. Make force, softness, and touch into signals the controller directly reads and manages, add touch sensors to hands, and let the robot decide how firm or gentle to be from moment to moment (compliance).

How, concretely. Once the robot senses and controls force, it can do the contact-heavy jobs that pure position control cannot.

One flexible tool for both seeing and acting · 72 papers

▸ one policy: pixels → actions

The problem. Robots often face several equally good options at once, and simpler methods either freeze up or pick a shaky average of them, giving jerky or unreliable behavior.

The approach. Reuse a method first built to make images, which starts from random noise and cleans it up into a good result (diffusion), and point it instead at making robot movements and control choices. Because it handles several good options at once, it fits messy contact tasks and can also report how unsure it is.

How, concretely. It keeps many valid choices cleanly separate instead of collapsing them into one average, giving smoother behavior and a built-in sense of confidence.

CROSS-CUTTING — make it cheap, safe, and reliable

The same methods pushed into hard real jobs · 255 papers

▸ lab method → messy real job

The problem. Medicine, flying, underwater, and outdoor field work each break the standard methods with their own troubles: tissue that shifts, wind that shoves a drone, murky water, and rough ground.

The approach. Take the conference's core tools and adapt them for each setting: control that accounts for soft tissue moving, learned flight control that shrugs off disturbances, scene rebuilding tuned for inside-the-body or underwater views, and new datasets for these places.

How, concretely. The basic sense-decide-move loop still applies; each setting just needs its own specific troubles handled directly.

Getting several robots to work together · 169 papers

▸ many agents, one coordinated goal

The problem. A team of different robots doing a long shared job is much harder to coordinate than one robot, and simple automatic planning falls apart at that scale.

The approach. Language-model planning that hands out jobs across the team but is checked against real physical limits; keeping robots in sight of one another; sharing location information; and having the team reorganize itself when one robot breaks.

How, concretely. Grounding the plan in real limits keeps the team's plan achievable, and self-reorganizing keeps the mission going after a failure.

Making training examples cheaper · 168 papers

▸ few demos → mountains of data

The problem. Teaching robots by showing them what to do needs lots of good examples, and collecting them by hand on real robots is slow and expensive, which is the field's real bottleneck.

The approach. Better ways to record a person doing the task, including through see-through headset gear and head-worn cameras; automatic ways to make many more examples from a few; and ways to turn recordings of human motion into something a robot can copy.

How, concretely. More and cheaper examples, with less human effort per example, attack the bottleneck head-on.

Safety and being sure it won't fail · 159 papers

▸ stay safe as the world shifts

The problem. As learned parts and chatbot-style AI enter the control loop, it gets much harder to promise the robot will stay safe: that it will avoid crashes, obey limits, and not be tricked into harmful acts.

The approach. Math guardrails that block any command that would cross a safety line (control barrier functions), filters that throw out unsafe plans, checks that an AI's plan obeys stated safety rules, and deliberately trying to trick AI-controlled robots to expose the danger.

How, concretely. The guardrails add provable limits on top of flexible but unproven learned parts, and the trickery studies reveal where the exposure is.

Soft and body-inspired machines · 131 papers

▸ compliant, body-inspired design

The problem. Hard, rigid robots cannot bend, cushion, or squeeze through tight spaces the way a soft body can, which limits where they can go and how safely they can touch people.

The approach. Build bendable parts that can change how stiff they are, arms pulled by cables, printable stretch sensors, muscle-like parts driven by electricity and fluid, and designs copied from living creatures.

How, concretely. A body that can flex and soften reaches and works safely in places a rigid machine cannot, and stays gentle when it touches people.
