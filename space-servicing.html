orbital satellite servicer · the rendezvous-and-service cycle, stage by stage

Servicing in orbit

An orbital satellite-servicing robot runs one rendezvous-and-service cycle end to end in zero-g: it approaches and berths on a client's docking port, holds its attitude steady against the disturbances while it works, keeps its reaction wheels from saturating, and manipulates a tumbling client — reaching out from a spacecraft that is bolted to nothing. Five stages, five distinct sub-fields — each a plain rule, run and measured, with the failure it prevents.

Each stage is one link in the service cycle: reach without a foothold, hold the pointing, unload the wheels, berth on the port at a whisper, and grasp a tumbling target without tearing the arm.

1 free-float reach → 2 hold attitude → 3 desaturate → 4 berth → 5 match & grasp
1

Move without a foothold

Manipulation

The servicer's arm reaches out to grab a fitting on the client satellite — but the whole spacecraft is floating free, bolted to nothing. Swing the arm one way and momentum conservation kicks the base the other way, so the gripper arrives in the wrong place and misses the fitting entirely.

Counter the base reaction when you move a manipulator on a free-floating spacecraft; with no foothold, momentum conservation recoils the base and the end-effector misses unless a thruster cancels the reaction.
EE error — uncompensated59 mm
EE error — thruster-compensated4 mm
base recoil59 mm

Swinging a 40 kg arm+payload 0.8 m on a 500 kg free base recoils the base 59 mm — the uncompensated end-effector error. A base-thruster reaction cancel cuts it to 4 mm.

2

Hold the attitude

Control & MPC

The servicer must keep its sensors and antenna pointed at the client while it works. But solar pressure and gravity gradients apply a small, steady disturbance torque, and with nothing to lean against that torque quietly integrates into an ever-growing pointing drift.

Close an attitude loop on the reaction wheels; a steady disturbance torque integrates into an ever-growing pointing drift, but a PD controller holds the spacecraft on target to a milliradian.
drift — no control0.45 rad
error — PD control1.2 mrad
disturbance torque0.05 N·m

Under a steady 0.05 N·m disturbance on a 200 kg·m² body, doing nothing drifts 0.45 rad off target in 60s. A PD attitude loop holds it to 1.2 mrad.

3

Unload the wheels

Safety & guarantees

Reaction wheels hold attitude by spinning faster to absorb every bit of disturbance momentum. Over a long service job that momentum piles up and the wheels spin toward their speed limit — and a saturated wheel that can't spin any faster delivers no torque at all, so attitude control simply dies.

Desaturate the reaction wheels with thrusters before they hit their speed limit; a persistent disturbance spins the wheels to saturation, and a saturated wheel delivers no torque, so attitude control is lost.
wheel speed — no desat100% (saturated)
wheel speed — desaturated65%
pointing — no desat6.1 rad

Under a persistent disturbance, the wheels run to 100% of their 600 rad/s limit and saturate — attitude blows out to 6.1 rad. Thruster desaturation keeps them at 65%, holding pointing to 1.2 mrad.

4

Berth on the port

Navigation

The final approach has to touch the docking port at a whisper. On Earth you can coast and let drag bleed off speed, but in vacuum there is no drag — coast too long and nothing slows you, so a plan that brakes late arrives at the port going far too fast and slams the mechanism.

Start braking a berthing approach at the full v^2/2a stop distance; space has no aero drag to bleed off the closing speed, so a plan that brakes late slams into the port at high contact velocity.
contact velocity — brake late0.27 m/s
contact velocity — planned0.00 m/s
stop distance6.25 m

Closing at 0.5 m/s with 0.02 m/s² braking needs a 6.25 m stop distance. A plan that assumes drag and brakes late contacts at 0.27 m/s (a hard dock); braking at the full stop distance contacts at 0.00 m/s.

5

Match the spin

World models

A dead client satellite is rarely still — it tumbles slowly end over end. Reach out and clamp a fixed point on a body that's rotating and the gripper has to absorb the full relative spin the instant it grabs: the arm gets wrenched sideways and can tear at the joint.

Match the chaser's rotation to the tumbling client before you close the grasp; grasping across a rotation rate loads the arm with the full relative angular velocity and tears it, whereas spinning up to match leaves almost none.
relative spin — unmatched10 deg/s
relative spin — matched0.3 deg/s
target tumble10 deg/s

A client tumbling at 10 deg/s grabbed from a still chaser loads the arm with the full 10 deg/s of relative spin. Spinning the chaser up to match first leaves only 0.3 deg/s at capture.
