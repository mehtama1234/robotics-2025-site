01 · the trap

Why one-shot behavior cloning breaks

The obvious way to teach a robot from demonstrations is to fit a network that maps what it sees to the action a human took, minimizing the error. It works — until the task has more than one right answer.

And most do. You can grab a mug from the left or the right; both are correct. Ordinary regression minimizes average error to ‘the’ action, so it splits the difference and outputs the mean — a reach into the empty space between the two good grasps. This is the mode-averaging trap, and it is exactly what diffusion policies exist to escape.

the actual math — one level deeper

Behavior cloning by regression minimizes Eₖₕ[‖a − fθ(s)‖²], whose optimum is the conditional mean fθ(s)=E[a|s].

If p(a|s) is multimodal (two good grasps), the mean sits between the modes — a point of near-zero probability. The regressor confidently outputs an invalid action.

02 · the fix

Generate the action by denoising

Diffusion policies steal the mechanism from image generation. Instead of directly outputting an action, the network learns to denoise: begin with a random noisy action, and repeatedly clean it up, each step conditioned on the current observation, until a crisp action emerges.

The magic is that the process starts from fresh noise every time. So it can land on the left grasp or the right grasp on different runs — but never the impossible blend of the two. Prediction stops being ‘find the one answer’ and becomes ‘sample a good answer’.

the actual math — one level deeper

Reverse diffusion: start aₖ ~ N(0, I); for k=K…1, aₖ₋₁ = denoise(aₖ, s, k).

Train a noise predictor εθ(aₖ, s, k) to match the noise added in the forward process: min E‖ε − εθ(aₖ, s, k)‖². Conditioning on s enters via FiLM or cross-attention.

This is equivalent to learning the score ∇ log p(a|s) of the action distribution.

03 · why it works

Model the whole distribution, then sample

Underneath, the shift is from learning a function (state → action) to learning a distribution (all the actions that would be good here). A regressor can only report the average of that distribution; a diffusion model represents its full, possibly many-humped shape.

Once you have the distribution, you just draw a sample. For a two-grasp task the distribution has two peaks with a dead valley between; sampling lands on a peak, so the robot commits to a real grasp. This is why the field switched: multimodality goes from a failure mode to normal behavior. (Our Octo run below shows exactly this spread.)

the actual math — one level deeper

The model represents the whole conditional p(a|s), not just its mean — so it captures every mode.

Sampling draws a ~ p(a|s), landing on one mode. Classifier-free guidance sharpens conditioning: ε̂ = εθ(a,∅,k) + w·(εθ(a,s,k) − εθ(a,∅,k)).

04 · in a loop

Predict a chunk, act, replan

A policy that outputs a single action every tick tends to jitter and to drift: tiny errors compound because each decision is made in isolation. Diffusion policies almost always predict an action chunk — a short trajectory of the next several actions at once.

The robot executes the first few, then replans a fresh chunk from where it ended up (receding horizon). The motion comes out smooth and the policy can commit to a short-term plan instead of second-guessing itself every millisecond.

the actual math — one level deeper

Predict an action chunk aₜ₃ₜ₊ₕ (a short trajectory) instead of one aₜ; execute the first k, then replan — receding horizon.

Chunking cuts the compounding-error rate and enforces temporal consistency; horizon length H trades reactivity against smoothness.

05 · the catch

Making it fast enough to run

Diffusion’s one real weakness is speed: cleaning up noise takes dozens of network evaluations per action, and a robot control loop needs an answer in milliseconds. Plain diffusion policies run at only a few hertz.

So 2024–25 work is largely about going faster. Consistency models are distilled to jump almost straight from noise to the action in one or two steps; flow matching learns a velocity field you integrate in a handful of steps. Same multimodal expressiveness, now fast enough to run on a real robot — which is why flow matching is quietly taking over.

the actual math — one level deeper

Plain DDPM sampling needs ~50–100 network evaluations (NFEs) per action → only a few Hz.

Consistency models learn f(aₖ, k) → a₀ directly, so 1–4 NFEs suffice. Flow matching learns a velocity vθ(a, s, τ) and integrates the ODE da/dτ = vθ in 1–5 steps.

Result: 10×–50× faster inference, into the 100s of Hz a control loop needs.

The benefits people actually get

Every family below leans on one or more of these — the recurring reasons the field switched to denoising actions, in the words the papers keep using.

Handles multiple right answers

The whole point: it models the full distribution of good actions and samples one, instead of averaging valid options into an invalid mean. ‘Multimodal’ goes from failure mode to normal behavior.

Stable to train

Unlike GANs, the denoising objective is a simple regression that trains stably and doesn’t mode-collapse — you reliably keep all the modes.

Expressive, high-dimensional actions

Denoising models rich, continuous action spaces — whole trajectories, bimanual and dexterous motions — that a single-output head can’t represent.

01

Imitation & multimodal behavior cloning

robotics
the problem

You want to teach a robot a manipulation skill from a handful of demonstrations — but different demonstrators (and the same one on different days) do it in different, equally valid ways.

the approaches

The base case and the reason diffusion policies exist: learn a manipulation skill from a few dozen demonstrations where several actions are valid, without collapsing to their average. Denoising captures the multimodality that sinks ordinary regression.

The 2024–25 work scales it — bimanual, deformable, contact-rich — and grounds it better in 3D and semantics for sample efficiency.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Collect a few dozen demonstrations of the task — several valid ways included.
  2. Train a denoiser to reproduce that action distribution, conditioned on the camera image.
  3. At test time, start from noise and denoise into an action.
  4. Because you sampled, you get one of the demonstrated ways — never their invalid average.
  5. Add 3D or semantic conditioning to stretch the same few demos to new objects.

    1. Lift observations into 3D: use a SLAM or depth backbone to build a point cloud from the camera frame.
    2. Feed compact 3D tokens into the denoiser, which generates actions or pose trajectories in 3D space.
    3. Because the policy reasons geometrically, it holds up under camera motion, lighting changes, and new object instances in the same spatial layout.

02

Action chunking & receding-horizon control

robotics
the problem

Denoising an action takes time the control loop doesn’t have, and deciding one step at a time is jerky. You need smooth motion AND a plan that’s always ready before the last one runs out.

the approaches

Deciding one action at a time is twitchy and lets errors compound; predicting a short trajectory and replanning fixes both. This is the temporal backbone of nearly every deployed diffusion policy.

The frontier is doing it fast — streaming or warm-started denoising so the chunk is ready inside a control tick.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Predict a chunk of the next H actions in one denoising pass.
  2. Execute the first few of them.
  3. Before the chunk runs out, start denoising the next one — warm-started from the last solution.
  4. Roll the partially-denoised future forward so a fresh chunk is always ready in time.
  5. The robot moves smoothly and never stalls waiting for the next plan.
  • ROBO
    From Imitation to Refinement — freezes a chunked BC policy and adds residual RL to fix distribution shift

    Behavior cloning from demonstrations gives you a policy, but it only replicates what the data showed—it never fixes the mistakes the demonstrators made or adapts to new situations. This paper keeps the cloned base but freezes it, then adds a residual RL layer to refine the actions where the clone errs, correcting distribution shift between training and deployment without destabilizing the learned behavior.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: train a diffusion policy to clone the demonstrations (the chunked trajectory baseline), then freeze those weights. On top, learn a small correction network via RL (typically PPO with a learned reward or human preference signal) that generates delta-actions to adjust the cloned trajectory. The residual stays bounded by KL-divergence regularization so the policy doesn't drift far from the cloned prior, maintaining stability.

    Why it works: the clone already captures the demonstration distribution; RL tunes it toward better-than-demonstrations behavior while the KL regularization keeps it from collapsing into a different mode. The denoising trajectory gives RL a smooth, multimodal prior to refine rather than starting from scratch.

  • ROBO
    Streaming Diffusion Policy — rolls over partially-denoised future actions to amortize inference cost

    Denoising actions takes ~50 network passes, too slow for a control loop that needs a decision every 10 ms. This paper rolls partially-denoised futures forward as the robot executes, so the next chunk is ready before the current one finishes, amortizing the inference cost across multiple control steps instead of stalling between decisions.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: predict a chunk of H future actions (H ≈ 8). As the robot executes the first k actions, continue the denoising of the remaining H-k steps in parallel. When k steps expire, the remaining chunk is already half-denoised; roll the partially-denoised states forward to account for elapsed time, then finish denoising. By pipelining, the wall-clock time between decisions shrinks from ~50 NFEs to ~10-15.

    Why it works: partial denoising preserves the multimodal distribution (you're not committing to a final action yet), and rolling the trajectory forward is a cheap linear operation. The robot moves continuously without waiting for fresh plans to finish.

  • ROBO
    Real-time Iteration Scheme for Diffusion Policy — warm-starts each step from the previous solution, control-style

    Control systems solve the next step by warm-starting from the previous solution—iteratively refining a trajectory at every tick. This paper applies the same idea to diffusion: instead of starting each denoising from pure noise, warm-start from the previous timestep's solution, taking only 3-5 denoising steps per cycle rather than 50, landing naturally into real-time control loops.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: at control tick t, take the denoised action chunk from tick t-1, shift it one step forward (roll the trajectory), add a small amount of noise back (re-diffusion), then denoise for only 3-5 steps. This mimics classical iterative control: the solution at t-1 is already close to the optimal t; a few refinement steps land there. Because the prior is warm, few steps suffice.

    Why it works: diffusion's denoising preserves the local structure of the trajectory. Warm-starting from a nearby solution lets you skip the first 40-45 noisy steps and go straight to refinement. Each cycle completes in milliseconds, feeding a fast control loop while staying multimodal.

  • ROBO
    RA-DP — training-free high-frequency replanning via guidance injected at every denoising step

    Most diffusion policies denoise once, offline, then execute; they can't react to unexpected obstacles or disturbances mid-trajectory. RA-DP injects guidance at every denoising step to steer the action away from hazards on the fly—no retraining, no rerolling the entire action, just nudging what's already being denoised in real time.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: run denoising as normal, but at each of the K steps, compute a cost gradient (distance to obstacle, collision likelihood, constraint violation) and push the denoising trajectory in the direction that lowers that cost. The gradient is injected into the prediction-correction loop; each denoised intermediate state bends away from the hazard slightly.

    Why it works: because denoising is iterative, the trajectory hasn't committed to a risky action yet. Steering one step at a time integrates into a globally safer path. No retraining needed: the same frozen policy responds to new costs by changing the denoising trajectory on-the-fly.

3D & point-cloud-conditioned policies

robotics
the problem

A policy that reads flat pixels breaks the moment the camera shifts or the light changes — the same scene looks different, so the learned action goes wrong.

the approaches

A policy that reads raw pixels is fragile to viewpoint and lighting; conditioning the denoiser on 3D geometry (point clouds, scene tokens) makes it robust and sample-efficient.

The denoiser predicts 3D poses/trajectories directly, fusing vision, language, and proprioception in one 3D space.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Lift the observation into 3D — a point cloud or 3D scene tokens — instead of raw pixels.
  2. Condition the denoiser on that 3D representation.
  3. Denoise directly in 3D: the output is a 3D end-effector pose or trajectory.
  4. Because it reasons in geometry, it holds up when the camera moves or the lighting changes.
  5. Same demos go further — viewpoint-robust and more sample-efficient.
  • ROBO
    3D Diffuser Actor — a 3D denoising transformer predicts pose trajectories → +18.1% over prior SOTA

    Policies that condition on 2D pixels fail the moment the camera moves or lighting shifts—the pixel distribution changes while the right action hasn't. This paper lifts observations into 3D (point clouds) and conditions a 3D denoising transformer on that geometry, predicting 3D end-effector poses directly, making the policy handle viewpoint and lighting variation while staying sample-efficient.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: encode the point cloud into a 3D scene representation (e.g., tokens per spatial region or voxel); use a transformer to fuse the cloud with the task observation (goal, language, proprioception). The denoiser conditions on that 3D representation via cross-attention and predicts a 3D trajectory (end-effector positions and orientations). Denoising happens in 3D space, not pixel space.

    Why it works: 3D geometry is viewpoint-invariant. The same point cloud under a different camera angle represents the same scene, so the learned denoising generalizes. Predicting in 3D naturally produces spatially coherent trajectories, and the policy sees far fewer irrelevant pixels—only the relevant 3D structure.

  • ROBO
    PointFlowMatch — conditional flow matching on point clouds → 67.8% RLBench, 2× the next best

    Consistency models and flow matching speed up denoising by 10-50×; this paper combines flow matching with point-cloud conditioning to get both speed and 3D invariance. Flow matching on point clouds learns a velocity field in 3D space you can integrate in 1-2 steps, hitting 67.8% on RLBench—2× the next-best—while staying viewpoint-agnostic.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: condition on the point cloud representation; learn a velocity field v(x, t, obs) that points from random initial actions toward good actions. Given a noise sample, integrate the ODE dx/dt = v(x, t, obs) for a few steps (1-2 typically). The velocity is trained by comparing trajectories sampled at different times and matching the flow. No intermediate denoising steps needed; one integration solves.

    Why it works: the velocity field is smoother than iterative denoising, so integration converges fast. Point-cloud conditioning keeps it geometrically grounded. The flow learns the entire distribution implicitly in the vector field, so sampling is as simple as following one smooth curve from noise to action.

  • CVPR

    Learning 3D-invariant action features from scratch requires heavy 3D annotation. This paper pre-trains a diffusion model on multi-view image sets, forcing the encoder to build 3D-consistent latent features that don't change when the viewpoint shifts. Fine-tuning on real manipulation then uses those invariant features, achieving 92.2% LIBERO with far fewer task-specific demos.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: in pretraining, show the model multiple views of the same scene and the same action trajectory. Denoise action from each view independently. Loss: the denoised actions must be consistent despite different camera angles (penalize differences between views). The encoder learns to extract invariant 3D structure from 2D pixels. At task time, the learned encoder feeds into a task-specific policy head.

    Why it works: multi-view consistency is a strong self-supervised signal for 3D structure. By forcing the encoder to ignore view changes, it learns geometry, not appearance. Tasks then inherit that invariance; new viewpoints generalize because the features already encode the 3D layout.

  • ROBO

    Policies struggle when the robot goes out of frame or the camera angle changes mid-task.     how it actually works

    The mechanism: predict a noisy end-effector pose; render it into the camera image as a 3D overlay (silhouette, skeleton, or texture). Condition the denoiser on both the observation and the rendered prediction. Denoise jointly over pose and pixel space so the network sees the predicted action visualized on the scene. Loss includes both pose accuracy and render-observation consistency.

    Why it works: rendering makes spatial relationships explicit and visual. The denoiser sees the pose prediction in context, so it can correct occlusions and camera variations by reasoning about the image directly. Tying denoising to the render ties the learned policy to the coordinate system that matters for execution.

Equivariant & symmetry-aware policies

robotics
the problem

Demonstrations are expensive, yet a demo of grasping a mug at one angle tells a naive network almost nothing about the same grasp rotated 30°.

the approaches

Manipulation has built-in geometry: rotate the scene and the right action rotates with it. Hard-coding that symmetry into the denoiser means the policy learns far more from each demo.

The payoff is dramatic data efficiency — minutes of demonstrations generalizing across poses and scales.

  1. Notice the symmetry: rotate the whole scene and the correct action rotates by the same amount.
  2. Build that rule into the network so it’s guaranteed, not learned.
  3. Now one demonstration automatically teaches every rotated version of itself.
  4. Train on far fewer demos for the same coverage.
  5. Generalize to object poses you never showed.

Fast sampling: consistency & flow matching

both
the problem

Diffusion policies are accurate but slow — dozens of denoising steps per action, far too many for a fast robot control loop.

the approaches

Diffusion’s Achilles’ heel is the many denoising steps; this family removes it. Consistency models distill the many-step process into one jump; flow matching learns a straight velocity field you integrate in a few steps.

This is what moves diffusion policies from ‘a few Hz’ into real-time control — and flow matching is fast becoming the default.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Instead of a long noisy path, learn a velocity field that points from noise straight toward good actions.
  2. Start at a noise sample.
  3. Follow the velocity for one or a few steps.
  4. Arrive at a clean action almost immediately.
  5. Same multimodal quality, now fast enough for a real control loop.
  • ROBO
    Consistency Policy — consistency distillation → 10× faster inference for resource-constrained robots

    Diffusion's speed problem is real: 50+ denoising passes per action is too slow for mobile or resource-constrained robots. Consistency models distill the many-step process into a single jump from noise to clean action—consistency distillation—delivering 10× faster inference while keeping the multimodal expressiveness, enabling deployment on edge hardware.

    how it actually works

    The mechanism: train a consistency function f(x, t) that maps from any noisy action at any diffusion timestep t directly to the clean action. Distill by running a teacher diffusion model on many trajectories; for each noisy intermediate state, the consistency function should predict the final action. The loss penalizes mismatch: L = ||f(x_t, t) - f(x_s, s)||, enforcing that all intermediate states point to the same clean output.

    Why it works: once trained, f skips iterative refinement and produces a clean action in one pass. The multimodal structure is implicit in f's learned parameterization (f can produce different outputs for similar noisy inputs if the teacher distribution is bimodal). One-step inference replaces 50 steps, fitting inside real-time loops on CPU/mobile.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Vision sees the scene but not what happens at the finger-object interface; when contact dynamics dominate (a fragile item, in-hand manipulation), the policy needs proprioceptive ground truth.

    The mechanism: Collect both camera images and tactile readings (pressure, shear, vibration) into a unified 3D visuo-tactile representation. The denoiser conditions on both modalities jointly, learning a fused space where tactile signals inform contact forces and camera features guide reach direction.

    Why it works: At contact, tactile feedback becomes the primary signal; the denoiser uses it to commit to a specific pressure profile or slip velocity instead of guessing from the image alone. On fragile and occluded tasks, touch + diffusion consistently outperforms vision-only.

  • ROBO
    Implicit Contact Diffuser — latent diffusion over contact descriptors guides MPC past local minima

    Model-predictive control for manipulation gets stuck at local minima when trying to solve contact transitions — press then slide requires predicting a discontinuity and reversing a trajectory if wrong. Latent diffusion over contact descriptors lets the policy propose multiple candidate contact modes; MPC then refines the best one, sidestepping local traps.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Discontinuous contact dynamics (stick, slip, lift) create local minima that gradient-based optimizers can't escape; a single forward pass either guesses wrong or leaves the policy half-committed.

    The mechanism: Train a latent diffusion model over contact state descriptors (friction cone angle, normal force, slip direction). At run time, sample a diverse set of contact trajectories from the diffusion prior, then initialize MPC from each one and solve for the full trajectory. Keep the solution with lowest cost.

    Why it works: Diffusion provides a diverse set of plausible contact transitions, and MPC refines from a warm start. Instead of climbing out of a single local minimum, the hybrid explores multiple valleys in parallel.

  • ROBO
    ProDapt — long-term contact-memory conditioning enables proprioception-only completion

    A robot hand can complete a task from tactile memory alone if it has learned to attend to long-term contact history — how the object shifted, whether it slipped, what corrective move came next. Long-term tactile memory lets a diffusion policy complete manipulation using only proprioception and touch, even if vision is occluded or the workspace is dark.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Vision-based policies fail when the target object is occluded (in-hand) or the workspace is unlit; proprioception alone (joint angles, velocities) is too sparse to guide contact-rich manipulation.

    The mechanism: Augment the diffusion denoiser with a rolling history of tactile observations — pressure maps, shear vectors, vibration spectra over the last T steps. At each denoising step, the policy conditions on this tactile history and proprioceptive state, learning to infer object geometry and friction from touch alone.

    Why it works: Tactile memory encodes object-level information (shape, stiffness, slip direction) that proprioception cannot; the denoiser learns to read that signal across a sequence of touches and generate contact-consistent actions even in darkness or total occlusion.

  • ROBO
    PolyTouch — multi-modal tactile (camera + acoustic) + tactile-diffusion policies

    Most tactile sensors are either camera-based (high bandwidth, fragile) or simple pressure arrays (reliable, coarse). Multi-modal tactile fusion — combining camera and acoustic sensing in a single learned representation — gives both richness and reliability for contact-rich policies, especially on deformable objects.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Single-modality tactile sensors are brittle: optical sensors saturate or break, pressure arrays are too coarse for dexterous control on deformable items like cloth.

    The mechanism: Fuse camera-based tactile images and acoustic emissions (sound of slip, vibration on contact) into a joint 3D tactile descriptor. Feed both into the denoiser so it learns to weight each modality's signal based on task phase: acoustic dominates early (detecting slip), optical leads when tracking fine geometry later.

    Why it works: Acoustic signals slip and stick events faster than pressure; optical captures texture and edges. The denoiser learns which sense to trust when, naturally blending modalities across the contact sequence. On soft and deformable tasks, this multi-modal reliability exceeds any single sensor.

Locomotion & whole-body control

robotics
the problem

A walking robot has many valid gaits and split-second recovery moves; averaging them (as regression does) yields a stumble, and the control loop is far too fast for slow sampling.

the approaches

Legged control has many valid gaits and recovery moves — a natural fit for a distribution-modeling policy. Diffusion brings offline-learned, multimodal locomotion to real robots in real time.

The trick is squeezing the sampler into the tight, high-rate loop a walking robot needs.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Collect offline logs of a legged robot moving — multiple valid gaits.
  2. Train a diffusion policy on them; it captures trot, bound, and recovery moves.
  3. In the control loop, sample a gait-consistent action chunk fast enough for real time.
  4. The robot commits to one coherent gait instead of averaging into a stumble.
  5. Preference or sim-to-real tuning adapts the gaits to new terrain.
  • ROBO
    DiffuseLoco — first real-time legged control by diffusion from offline data; beats RL/BC on stability

    Walking gaits are multimodal — trot, bound, walk — and regression-trained policies average them into an unstable stumble. Diffusion in the control loop samples a committed gait from offline demonstrations; because action chunking keeps sampled motions smooth and because flow matching now fits into real-time rates, deployed legged robots can move reliably for the first time.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A walking robot has several valid gaits (trot, bound, gallop); behavior cloning via MSE loss outputs the mean gait, which is typically unstable and sits between two modes. The control loop also runs at 100+ Hz, leaving no time for slow diffusion sampling.

    The mechanism: Collect offline logs of legged locomotion capturing multiple gaits. Train a diffusion policy on action chunks (a short sequence of joint commands). At run time, use consistency or flow-matching distillation to sample an action chunk in 1–5 steps instead of dozens, fitting it into a control tick. Execute the first few commands, then replan the next chunk from the current state.

    Why it works: Denoising captures the full distribution of valid gaits, so sampling commits to one coherent trot or bound instead of averaging into the invalid in-between. Real-time flow matching makes this feasible on modest hardware; receding-horizon replanning keeps the trajectory stable and reactive.

  • ROBO
    Preference-Aligned Diffusion Planner for Quadrupeds — offline diffusion planner + preference refinement → zero-shot sim-to-real gaits

    Offline data captured in simulation with one terrain rarely transfers to hardware in a new environment. Preference refinement — collecting a few trials in the new setting and using human feedback to steer the diffusion prior toward locally-successful gaits — achieves zero-shot sim-to-real without a full retraining loop.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Sim-to-real for legged robots fails because simulated friction, compliance, and motor response differ from hardware; retraining from scratch on hardware is expensive, and fine-tuning a large policy is unstable.

    The mechanism: Train an offline diffusion planner on simulation data. At deployment, run a handful of trials on the real robot and ask a human to rank which gaits worked best. Use those preferences to guide the sampling: at each denoising step, nudge the action toward high-ranked trajectories using a learned preference reward.

    Why it works: Preference guidance steers the existing diffusion prior toward local-environment gaits without retraining the full model. A few human annotations are enough to anchor the policy to the real hardware's friction and dynamics.

  • ROBO
    Transferable Latent-to-Latent Locomotion — a latent diffusion recovery module adapts one core across robots

    Each legged robot has different leg lengths, mass distribution, and actuator limits; retraining a diffusion policy for every robot is wasteful. Latent-space residual modules learn robot-specific corrections on top of a shared core policy, so one base model transfers to many morphologies with minimal adaptation.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A diffusion policy trained on one quadruped (e.g., A1) does not generalize to another (e.g., Spot) because the action space is morphology-specific; retraining per-robot is expensive and slow.

    The mechanism: Train a base diffusion policy on a reference morphology. For each new robot, learn a lightweight latent-to-latent residual module: it takes the base policy's latent noise-state and outputs a morphology-specific correction to the action. This module is small and data-efficient, trained from just a few hours of the new robot's data.

    Why it works: The base policy captures the gait distribution across dynamics; the residual module accounts for morphology-specific asymmetries (different leg lengths, actuator saturation). Together, they transfer across robots with far less data than retraining from scratch.

Human motion generation

vision
the problem

One instruction (‘a person waving’) matches countless valid motions; a model that outputs the average produces a lifeless, physically-off motion.

the approaches

The vision side of the same idea: generate human motion — from text, sketch, or music — where countless motions fit one prompt. Diffusion captures that multimodality while respecting contacts and physics.

Recent work makes it causal/streaming and physics-aware so the motion is both live and plausible.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Take a text prompt (“a person waving”).
  2. Denoise a full-body motion sequence conditioned on it.
  3. Sample again → a different but equally valid waving motion.
  4. Add physics-aware losses so contacts and balance stay plausible.
  5. Make it causal / streaming so the motion can be generated live.
  • CVPR
    Causal Motion Diffusion Models — first causal diffusion for streaming motion → order-of-magnitude lower latency

    Generating human motion from text or music typically requires looking ahead to the full prompt, which breaks streaming and introduces latency. Causal diffusion — conditioning on only the past and current frame — enables live generation with order-of-magnitude lower latency, crucial for real-time animation and telepresence.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Standard diffusion samples the full sequence conditioned on the text prompt, requiring the entire input before generation starts; this incurs seconds of latency, making live motion capture-to-motion pipelines impossible.

    The mechanism: Restrict the attention and conditioning in the denoiser to only past frames and the current frame. At each timestep, denoise the next frame's joint angles using only the history of past poses and current frame observation, not future context. Autoregressively generate frame-by-frame.

    Why it works: Causal masking makes the model commit to low-latency predictions; each frame depends only on the past, so generation can stream in real time. The latency drops from seconds (sample full sequence) to tens of milliseconds (denoise one frame).

  • CVPR
    PAMotion — a physics-aware loss on object acceleration enforces contact consistency

    Generated human motions often violate physical constraints — floating feet, unrealistic joint accelerations, impossible balance — making them unsuitable for animation or control. Physics-aware denoising losses integrate contact and acceleration constraints directly into the diffusion training, ensuring generated motions remain physically plausible.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A diffusion model trained on reconstructed motion-capture trajectories can produce unrealistic motions: feet sliding, excessive joint acceleration, or balance violations that no human could achieve.

    The mechanism: Add auxiliary losses during training that penalize physics violations: measure contact (foot-ground distance), measure acceleration norms, estimate balance stability from center-of-mass projection. At each diffusion step, backpropagate these physics losses alongside the standard noise-prediction loss.

    Why it works: Physics losses act as a soft constraint, nudging the denoiser toward trajectories with valid contact and dynamics. The model learns to trade off data likelihood (matching the distribution of training motions) against physical plausibility, naturally favoring motions that satisfy both.

  • CVPR
    SceMoS — scene-aware motion via a token planner grounded on per-frame heightmaps

    Human motion in a cluttered scene depends on local geometry — doorways, furniture, obstacles — but generative models often ignore the scene context and produce motions that collide or look misaligned. Scene-aware tokens — per-frame heightmaps or occupancy encoding — let the denoiser condition on the 3D layout, generating motions that respect the scene's affordances.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Text-to-motion models generate plausible motions in isolation but ignore the spatial environment; a motion that works in an empty space may collide with furniture or look spatially misaligned in the actual scene.

    The mechanism: Preprocess the scene as a heightmap or voxel occupancy grid at each frame. Encode this as tokens and feed into the diffusion denoiser alongside the text prompt. A token planner layer attends to the scene tokens to predict trajectory key points; the full motion denoiser then conditions on these waypoints, ensuring the body path avoids obstacles and respects the scene layout.

    Why it works: Scene conditioning anchors the motion to local geometry; the planner-level waypoints act as intermediate targets that keep the full motion coherent. The denoiser learns to generate motions compatible with both the text and the scene, naturally avoiding collisions.

  • CVPR
    Retrieval-Guided Diffusion Noise Optimization — retrieval-augmented noise init for highly-constrained motion

    Highly constrained motion synthesis — full-body IK, contact preservation, footstep timing — is hard to specify as text alone. Retrieval-augmented initialization finds similar motions in a database and uses them to seed the diffusion noise, narrowing the search space so the denoiser can refine toward a valid solution.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Text descriptions of constrained motions are ambiguous and loose; "stand on one leg" has infinite solutions, and pure diffusion samples widely instead of focusing on the valid subspace.

    The mechanism: Given a text prompt and constraints (e.g., contact points, keyframe positions), retrieve the nearest-neighbor motions from a motion database using semantic similarity. Use the nearest-neighbor's noise trajectory (in the diffusion latent space) as the initialization instead of pure Gaussian noise. Then denoise, biasing the sampler toward the retrieved exemplar's subspace.

    Why it works: Starting from an exemplar's noise state dramatically narrows the search space; the denoiser only needs to refine and adapt the retrieved motion to satisfy the text and constraints, rather than generating from scratch. This is especially powerful for highly constrained problems where the valid subspace is small.

Trajectory forecasting — multimodal futures

vision
the problem

Where a pedestrian or car goes next is genuinely uncertain — left, straight, or right — and a planner handed a single averaged prediction is dangerously wrong.

the approaches

Where will the pedestrian, cyclist, or car go? The future is genuinely multimodal — zigzag or straight, cautious or aggressive — and regression averages those into a useless middle path.

Diffusion (and flow) forecast the full distribution of futures, which is exactly what an uncertainty-aware planner needs downstream.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Observe an agent’s recent track and the scene.
  2. A regressor would predict one averaged future — often straight through where the agent won’t go.
  3. Instead, denoise a set of futures: left, straight, right — the real modes.
  4. Attach a probability to each.
  5. Hand the whole distribution to the planner, which reacts to all the likely futures, not one.
  • CVPR
    MeanFuser — one-step multi-modal trajectory generation without a fixed vocabulary, 59 FPS

    Predicting the next few seconds of pedestrian or vehicle motion for autonomous planning is multimodal — they might turn left, go straight, or accelerate — and a single averaged prediction is dangerously wrong. One-step multimodal generation using discrete flow matching samples several distinct futures in a single forward pass at real-time speed, giving planners the full distribution they need.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A car or pedestrian can move in several valid ways (turn left or right at an intersection); a regressor outputs the mean path, which often passes through the middle (where no one actually goes). Sampling diverse futures at 59 FPS is too slow for real-time autonomous driving.

    The mechanism: Encode motion as discrete trajectory tokens (e.g., quantized waypoints in a latent space). Train a flow-matching model over this discrete space conditioned on the agent's past trajectory and scene context. At inference, sample multiple distinct trajectories in parallel by drawing different flow paths through the latent space. Each sample takes one step thanks to flow matching's direct integration.

    Why it works: Discrete flow matching avoids expensive iterative refinement; one forward pass generates a trajectory. Sampling multiple trajectories in parallel gives the planner a multimodal distribution without the latency of DDPM-style sequential sampling. 59 FPS throughput makes it deployable in autonomous-driving stacks.

  • CVPR
    Gaussian-Mixture Latent Flow — a GMM latent flow for diverse, accurate stochastic 3D human motion prediction

    Forecasting human motion in 3D is multimodal and ambiguous: the person might reach left or right, sit or stand. Gaussian-mixture latent flow — learning a mixture of velocity fields over different modes — captures multiple futures and assigns probability to each, so predictions encode true uncertainty rather than blurred averages.

    how it actually works

    The problem: A single regression-based motion predictor outputs point estimates (one averaged trajectory); when the true future is multimodal (two equally plausible reaching directions), the mean is often invalid. Even sampling-based models can undercount the number of distinct modes.

    The mechanism: Represent the latent distribution as a Gaussian mixture: learn K component velocity fields, each parameterized by a flow model. At inference, sample which mixture component (mode), then integrate its corresponding velocity field for a few steps. Assign probabilities to each mode based on the mixture weights and the input history.

    Why it works: Explicit mixture modeling ensures the model maintains multiple distinct modes; each one is a separate flow trajectory, so they don't blur together. The mixture weights give downstream planning direct access to mode likelihoods instead of requiring expensive resampling or filtering.

  • CVPR
    PoseD-Flow — guided flow on the rotation manifold → SOTA pose completion under occlusion

    Completing occluded human poses (filling in missing joints when limbs are hidden) is a manifold problem — joint angles live on rotation groups, not Euclidean space. Guided flow on the rotation manifold respects the geometric constraints of joint angles, generating geometrically valid and physically plausible completions even from heavy occlusion.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Predicting missing joint angles as points in Euclidean space violates the manifold structure of rotations; the output can be physically impossible (joint angles outside valid ranges, kinematic inversions). Naive diffusion treats rotations as flat vectors.

    The mechanism: Parameterize joint angles as rotations (6D, quaternion, or axis-angle). Design the flow-matching velocity field to operate on the Riemannian manifold of rotations, respecting geodesic distances and intrinsic geometry. Use guidance to steer the flow toward visible joints and away from occlusion.

    Why it works: Manifold-aware velocity fields guarantee outputs stay in the valid rotation space; the flow naturally interpolates between poses along geodesics, not straight lines through Euclidean space. Guided sampling steers the manifold flow toward observed joints and plausible human poses, ensuring physical validity under heavy occlusion.

  • CVPR
    WAM-Flow — discrete flow matching over trajectory tokens for fast multimodal driving plans

    Trajectory planning for autonomous driving needs multimodal predictions (turn left, straight, right) and high throughput (>10 Hz). Discrete flow matching over trajectory tokens generates diverse driving plans directly from a latent vocabulary without iterative sampling, achieving 3× speedup while maintaining multimodal expressiveness.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Diffusion-based motion planning for driving is accurate but slow; iterative DDPM sampling takes dozens of steps. Multimodal predictions require either expensive per-mode sampling or lossy discretization; classical planners lose the ability to sample diverse-but-valid alternatives.

    The mechanism: Tokenize trajectories as a sequence of discrete waypoint tokens (e.g., 2D positions quantized to a codebook). Train a flow-matching model over this discrete space, conditioned on the ego vehicle's state and the scene context. At inference, sample different trajectory tokens in sequence using integrated flow (1–3 steps), pulling from different regions of the latent space to get diverse driving plans.

    Why it works: Discrete flow matching skips iterative sampling; integrating the flow field for one or a few steps gives a complete trajectory token sequence. Sampling from different latent regions naturally generates diverse plans without per-mode overhead. 3× speedup + multimodality makes this deployable in real-time autonomous stacks.

Diffusion + world models & RL fine-tuning

both
the problem

A policy cloned from demonstrations can only ever match the demonstrators; it never discovers a better way, and it inherits their mistakes.

the approaches

Two frontier seams. Pair a diffusion policy with a world model — imagine futures, then act — or fine-tune the policy with RL and human preference so it improves past what the demos showed.

Both push diffusion policies from ‘copy the demos’ toward ‘get better than the demos’.

worked example — one concrete run
  1. Start from a diffusion policy trained by imitation — it copies the demos, no better.
  2. Roll it out (in a world model, with a learned reward, or against human preferences).
  3. Score which rollouts did better than the demonstrations.
  4. Fine-tune the policy toward those — with KL regularization so it doesn’t destabilize.
  5. Now it exceeds the demonstrations instead of merely matching them.
  • BOTH
    RehearseVLA — a physically-consistent world model post-trains a VLA with <5 demos via safe RL

    A vision-language-action policy trained on demonstration data alone can only replicate what it saw; it never discovers a better strategy and inherits any inefficiencies in the demos. Physically-consistent world model post-training — imagine futures with a learned model, then use RL to refine the VLA toward better imagined outcomes — lets a policy improve past the demonstrations using just a handful of real trials.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Behavior cloning from demonstrations caps out at demonstrator skill; the policy can never exceed or correct the demos' mistakes. RL fine-tuning on real data is expensive and often unstable for complex manipulation tasks.

    The mechanism: Train a dynamics model on simulation or offline data to predict the next observation and reward given an action. Start with a VLA policy trained by imitation learning. For each batch, roll out trajectories in the learned world model, score them against a reward signal, and use safe RL (e.g., TRPO, PPO with KL regularization to the behavioral cloning prior) to improve the VLA. The KL penalty keeps updates close to the demo distribution.

    Why it works: The world model provides cheap rollouts so RL doesn't need expensive real trials; the KL penalty prevents distribution shift. The policy discovers better strategies than the demos because it has a reward signal, but stays conservative enough to remain stable. A handful of real trials for world-model grounding enables significant improvement.

  • ROBO
    FDPP — learns a reward from pairwise human preferences and fine-tunes a pretrained diffusion policy

    Improving a diffusion policy requires either labeled reward data (expensive) or real-world trial-and-error (risky). Learning rewards from pairwise preference annotations — humans rank trajectory pairs — provides a rich signal for fine-tuning without full-trajectory labels, making post-training efficient and practical.

    how it actually works

    The problem: Reward learning for RL typically requires labeling every trajectory; pairwise comparisons are easier for humans but extracting a scalar reward from preferences is statistically expensive and unstable.

    The mechanism: Collect trajectory pairs from a pretrained diffusion policy and ask annotators to rank them ("A is better than B"). Train a preference model (Bradley-Terry or similar) that assigns a scalar reward to each trajectory. Use this learned reward with RL algorithms (PPO, DPG) to fine-tune the pretrained policy, with KL regularization keeping updates close to the original.

    Why it works: Pairwise preferences are natural for humans to provide; Bradley-Terry reward models extract consistent scalar rewards from partial rankings. The pretrained policy provides a strong prior, so RL needs far fewer preference annotations than training a reward model from scratch. KL regularization ensures the policy stays safe and sample-efficient.

  • ROBO
    Diffusion Meets DAgger — synthesizes out-of-distribution states with diffusion for eye-in-hand DAgger

    Imitation learning policies fail on out-of-distribution states they never saw in training; classical DAgger queries an expert for correction but requires a real expert on standby. Diffusion-augmented DAgger synthesizes realistic out-of-distribution states by denoising from the policy's mistakes, so the system can query a simulator or learned expert without a human in the loop.

    how it actually works

    The problem: DAgger needs an expert available to label newly-encountered states; this is expensive for real robots. States the policy has never seen it may fail catastrophically, creating a compounding error problem.

    The mechanism: During training, whenever the policy makes a mistake on a test trajectory, denoise from the error signal to synthesize a realistic observation in that failure mode. Add this synthetic observation to the dataset and query the expert (simulator or learned model) for the correct action. Retrain the policy on the expanded dataset. Repeat: the diffusion process generates diverse out-of-distribution states, and the expert labels them.

    Why it works: Diffusion synthesis produces realistic OOD examples instead of random noise; the expert provides ground-truth actions for those states. The policy iteratively covers more of the state space without requiring a real expert for every failure, achieving data-efficient improvement through synthetic-then-expert-label cycles.

  • BOTH
    Chain-of-Imagination — an MLLM + diffusion generate visual sub-goals the agent acts toward

    Long-horizon robotic tasks like "make a sandwich" break when a single diffusion policy tries to predict the entire trajectory; errors compound over dozens of steps. Multi-step visual imagination — an MLLM + diffusion jointly generate intermediate visual sub-goals, then an action policy executes toward those goals — decomposes the task hierarchically without explicit sub-goal annotation.

    how it actually works

    The problem: One flat diffusion policy over a long horizon (e.g., 100+ steps for multi-stage manipulation) accumulates drift and loses the thread of the task; chunking helps but doesn't address conceptual structure.

    The mechanism: Use a multimodal LLM (e.g., GPT-4V) to read the task instruction and current image, then generate text descriptions of intermediate visual sub-goals ("bread on plate," "butter applied"). Pass these descriptions to a diffusion model to generate imagined future observations (visual frames). Use an action policy to generate the next action toward the first imagined sub-goal. Execute, replan when close, repeat.

    Why it works: The MLLM breaks the long task into conceptual milestones; diffusion synthesizes realistic images for those milestones, grounding the plan in visual space. The action policy only needs to solve short-horizon primitives to reach each imagined goal. This hierarchical imagination-then-execution structure naturally handles long, multi-step tasks without explicit reward engineering.

Part IV

Where it stands

Diffusion (and now flow-matching) policies are the default recipe for learned manipulation. What is still hard:

Real-time speed. Even with consistency and flow, running a big multimodal policy inside a fast, high-rate control loop on modest hardware is still tight.
Reliability & failure detection. Sampled actions occasionally go wrong; knowing when a diffusion policy is about to fail, at runtime, is unsolved.
Beyond imitation. Diffusion policies mostly copy demonstrations; improving past them (RL, preferences) without instability is early.
Data still rules. They are more sample-efficient than they were, but broad generalization still hinges on large, diverse demonstration sets.
Guarantees. Guided sampling nudges toward safety but rarely guarantees a hard constraint the way a classical solver can.
Long horizons. Chunking helps, but coherent behavior over very long, multi-stage tasks remains hard.
